Trust What Repeats: Capped World-Model
Policy Gradients for Humanoid Motion Tracking
Xuzhe Dang and Stefan Edelkamp
Faculty of Electrical Engineering, Czech Technical University in Prague
Faculty of Mathematics and Physics, Charles University
Prague, Czech Republic
dangxuzh@fel.cvut.cz, stefan.edelkamp@gmail.com
Abstract—Training a humanoid motion tracker by backprop-
agating imagined returns through a learned world model is fast,
because the model gives a first-order policy gradient. We ask how
much of that gradient holds in the simulator. On a tracker for
the Unitree G1, the 0.5% of imagined rollouts with the largest
gradients carry 22% of the summed gradient norm, yet neither
a second rollout from the same state nor the simulator confirms
them. When the actions are moved along the model’s gradient, the
simulator returns 94% of the predicted change in return where
the gradient norm is below the batch median, 48% between 3
and 10 times the median, and 1% above 30 times; the part of
a gradient that either check confirms stops growing at a few
times the median. We therefore cap each rollout’s action-gradient
norm at 10 times the batch median, draw imagination starts
in proportion to their predicted gradient norm so that the cap
removes less, and reset episodes where the world model and critic
predict the lowest return. Trained on 223 hours of motion and
tested on 353 motions that no actor performed in training, the
method raises the normalized return of SuperMimic, the baseline
it builds on, from 0.953 to 0.967 and the lowest 1% of test clips
from 0.65 to 0.76, halves the test clips that fall persistently, from
15–18 to 7–10, and reaches the baseline’s final return in 40% of
the baseline’s iterations and half its training time. On LAFAN1,
trained and evaluated on the same clips as prior work, it reaches
0.935 against 0.906.
I. INTRODUCTION
A general motion tracker turns a library of human motion
into a single policy that a humanoid can run: given the next
frames of a reference, the policy outputs joint targets that
make the robot follow it. Such trackers are the low-level layer
under teleoperation, motion generation and learned skills, and
their quality is set by how many motions they follow without
falling and by how long they take to train. With model-free
reinforcement learning the second number is large: trackers
for libraries of a hundred hours and more are trained for days
on many GPUs [1]–[3].
SuperMimic [4] changed that. It fits a world model to the
simulator’s transitions and improves the policy by backprop-
agating the imagined return through the model, a first-order
gradient that needs no likelihood ratio and no exploration
in action space. The authors report the return of a strong
model-free tracker after hours on one GPU. The price of a
first-order gradient is known from differentiable simulation:
through contact and over long horizons the gradient of a
rollout can be large, wrong, or both [5]–[7], and a model
that is accurate in its predictions need not be accurate in its
derivatives.
This paper measures that price for motion tracking and
removes most of it. It rests on two measurements on a trained
tracker.
A few rollouts carry the gradient. Among imagined rollouts,
the 0.5% whose action-gradient norm G exceeds 30 times the
batch median hold 22% of the summed norm and 99% of
the summed squared norm, while 88% of rollouts lie below
3 times the median. Heavy tails of this kind are known
for gradients through simulators [7] and were reported for
humanoid tracking in concurrent work [8]; what has not been
asked is whether the tail is signal.
The large gradients are not backed. We roll the same start
state out twice under independent sensor noise and ask how
much of one rollout’s gradient the other reproduces; and we set
the simulator to states from the replay buffer, step it along the
model’s gradient, and ask how much of the predicted change
of the return appears. The two tests agree (Fig. 1). Up to about
3 times the median, both confirm most of a gradient’s norm.
Beyond that the confirmed part stops growing: from 5 times
on, both confirm about 3 to 4 times the median whatever the
norm, and the simulator returns 48% of the model’s predicted
change between 3 and 10 times the median, 12% between 10
and 30 times and 1% above.
The part of a rollout’s gradient that both tests confirm is
thus roughly min(G, k·med G), with med G the batch median
and k a few units, and we make that the update rule: the
norm of each rollout’s action gradient is capped at k = 10
times the batch median. The cap acts as a trust region on the
model’s derivative, set per rollout and measured against the
batch’s own median. Two further changes go with it. Start
states whose gradients the cap would cut are drawn more
often and weighted down, which leaves the expected gradient
unchanged and gives the cap less to remove. And episodes
are reset where the world model and critic predict the lowest
return, ranked over the whole library every 2,000 iterations,
which directs data to the motions the policy cannot yet follow
and finds them without first visiting them.
We evaluate on a split that no tracker of this kind has
reported, to our knowledge: BONES-SEED retargeted to the
Unitree G1, divided by motion, so that no test motion was
performed by any actor in training. On 11,828 test clips of

Our contributions are:
• two tests of a world model’s policy gradient, one against
a redraw of the same start and one against the simulator,
and the finding that both level off at a few times the batch
median of the gradient norm;
• an update built on that finding: a cap on each rollout’s
action-gradient norm, a start distribution drawn by gradi-
ent norm, which reduces what the cap removes without
adding bias but adds little once the resets are model-
ranked, and a reset distribution for motion tracking that
is ranked by the world model and critic instead of by
visits;
• an evaluation of world-model motion tracking on mo-
tions unseen in training, with ablations of each part, on
two versions of the Unitree G1, and a comparison on
LAFAN1.
II. RELATED WORK
Motion tracking at scale. Since DeepMimic [9], physics-
based trackers are trained with model-free reinforcement learn-
ing from random reference phases with early termination.
Scaling them to large libraries has relied on where episodes
start. PHC [1] evaluates the whole dataset and samples the
clips the policy fails; BeyondMimic [2] and SONIC [3] keep a
failure rate per bin of a clip and sample bins by it; GMT [10]
adapts sampling to tracking error. SuperMimic [4] keeps a
return per bin from the simulator’s rollouts. All of these score
a bin from episodes that visited it. We score every bin through
the world model and critic without visiting it.
First-order policy gradients. Differentiating a simulator or a
learned model gives low-variance policy gradients [11], [12],
and a known set of failures: the gradient’s variance grows with
the horizon in chaotic systems [5], [6], and through contact a
first-order estimate can be biased by rare large samples [7].
The usual remedies are short horizons closed by a critic and a
clip on the norm of the batch gradient [12], [13], or a horizon
adapted to contact stiffness [14]. SuperMimic [4] showed that
a learned model makes the approach practical for humanoid
tracking. Our redraw and simulator tests address the question
of [7] directly for a learned model: whether the rare large
samples carry the true gradient. In our setting they do not.
Clipping and truncation. The operator we use, a clip of each
sample’s gradient at a threshold set by a quantile of the norms,
is that of differentially private training [15], [16], used there
for privacy and here for accuracy; what is ours is the unit it
acts on, a rollout’s action gradient, and the evidence for its
level. Clipping biases the update [17], and the trade between
that bias and the variance removed is the one studied for
truncated importance weights [18], [19]; [19] derive clipping
as the minimizer of a worst-case bias plus the second moment,
an argument that carries over to gradients.
Sampling by gradient norm. Drawing training examples
in proportion to their gradient norm and weighting them
by the inverse is the variance-optimal importance sampling
for stochastic gradient descent [20]–[23], and prioritized re-
play [24] does the same with the temporal-difference error.
We apply it to the start states of imagined rollouts, with the
norm predicted from the critic’s value, and note that its purpose
here is different: it leaves a cap less to remove.
Curricula from critics and models. Prioritized level re-
play [25] ranks tasks by a value loss from actual rollouts.
ProCuRL [26] and SPaCE [27] score every task with one
forward pass of the critic, which is our ranking at a horizon
of zero. Closest in mechanism are methods that roll the policy
through a world model to score goals or start states [28], [29],
and the score itself is a model-based value expansion [30]. We
are not aware of a reset distribution for motion tracking that
is ranked by a learned model.
Weight averaging. Evaluating an average of the weights that
never acts in training [31] is standard in imitation learning and
uncommon in reinforcement-learning trackers. We use it for
both methods and claim nothing for it.
Concurrent work. ∇Mimic [8] reports heavy-tailed first-
order gradients for humanoid tracking and clips the action gra-
dient of each step and environment at a multiple of the batch
median inside the backward pass; we cap once per rollout
and test what the capped part is worth. InstantTrack [32] and
BlinkMimic [33] speed up SuperMimic’s line with a different
optimizer, a residual policy, GPU graph capture and, in the
latter, resets from failures of the deployed policy in simulation,
and train 40 LAFAN1 clips in minutes; InstantMimic [34] is a
GPU-native system for model-free tracking. These changes are
largely orthogonal to ours. None of them evaluates on motions
held out from training.
III. BACKGROUND
A. Motion tracking with a world model
We build on SuperMimic [4] and keep its environment,
networks and losses, in our own implementation. The robot is a
Unitree G1, simulated in Newton [35] with its MuJoCo Warp
solver [36], [37] at 200 Hz and controlled at 50 Hz through
joint position targets. A motion library holds retargeted clips;
an episode starts at a frame of a clip and ends when the clip
ends or when the robot’s height or tilt leaves a band around
the reference. The reward combines exponential kernels on the
tracking errors of body positions, orientations and velocities
with small action penalties. As in [4] we report the normalized
return of a clip: the sum of its rewards when played from the


first frame to the last, over the largest value that sum can take,
so that 1 is perfect and a fall forfeits the rest of the clip.
The policy πθ sees a history of 10 noisy proprioceptive
observations and the next 10 reference frames and outputs an
action at. A world model fω predicts the next simulator state
from the state and the action, st+1 = fω(st, at), and is fit to
transitions that 4,096 parallel environments write to a replay
buffer. A critic Vψ is fit to λ-returns of imagined rollouts.
Reward, termination and the observations are known functions
of the state, so nothing but the dynamics is learned.

C. Where episodes start
Episodes that end are reset to a frame of the library.
SuperMimic divides clips into bins of about one second and
keeps for each bin a score from the returns of the episodes
started there. A reset goes to a uniformly drawn bin with
probability 0.5, to a bin drawn with a probability that grows
as its score falls with probability 0.4, and to a bin drawn by
how often bins were drawn so far with probability 0.1. The
score of a bin changes only when the bin is visited.
IV. HOW MUCH OF THE MODEL’S GRADIENT IS REAL?
The measurements of this section are made on trained
trackers with the policy and the models held fixed. We make
them on two policies trained on the full 29-dof library for
25,000 iterations, the baseline’s and the one the method below
trains, and they agree. Where one number is given it is of the
method’s policy, since an update rule has to hold at the policies
it produces, and the baseline’s follows in brackets or beside it.
The same conclusions hold for policies trained on a subset of
the 23-dof library under other update rules and at an earlier
checkpoint (Appendix A).

D. What the tests say about the update
Trust is a matter of the norm, not of the state. One might
expect the model to be wrong where the motion is hard.
Within the band of 3 to 10 times the median, ranking states
by the critic’s value or by how far the policy falls short of the
reference from them, the third where the policy tracks worst
has a trust of 0.51 to 0.53 and the third where it tracks best
0.40 to 0.41. The large gradients are common on hard states,
but a gradient of a given norm is no less reliable there, and if
anything more.
The rule. Both tests give a rollout of norm G a worth near
min(G, k · med G) with k of a few units. An update that
weights every rollout by its full gradient spends most of its
step on the part neither test backs. An update that normalizes
every rollout to the same norm discards the difference between
a gradient of 0.3 medians and one of 3, which both tests say
is real, and on a subset of the library it leaves more than ten
times as many clips persistently failing in training as the cap
(Appendix C). The cap sits between the two.
The level. The tests do not single out a value of k. A lower
cap removes more of the worth and leaves less noise. Capping
at 10 removes 24% of P
b Gb but 3% (1 to 6%) of the summed
worth; capping at 3 removes 39% and 16% (12 to 19%),
and leaves 2.4 times less energy E[min(G, k med G)2], which
above a few medians is mostly noise. We use k = 10, the level
of these two that removes least of the worth.

Why per rollout. A cap on the norm of the whole batch
gradient, as in standard gradient clipping [13], rescales the
step and leaves its direction to the largest rollouts. A cap
on each step’s action gradient inside the backward pass, as
in concurrent work [8], changes the direction of a rollout’s
gradient. The tests of Sec. IV are statements about a rollout
as a whole, and the rule acts on that unit: about 60% of the
variance of log∥gb,t∥lies between rollouts, so the norm of a
step’s action gradient is mostly set by the rollout it belongs
to. Measured offline against a large-batch reference, a per-step
cap gives a gradient of similar quality (Appendix A); we did
not train with it.
Horizon, batch and learning rate. With the cap we use H =
64 imagined steps and B = 1,024 rollouts in place of 32 and
4,096. A longer horizon gives the policy more of the reference
to plan over and a later bootstrap, and is affordable once its
heavier gradient tail is cut; fewer rollouts keep the number
of imagined steps per iteration the same. We also double the
baseline’s policy learning rate, a setting carried over from early
short runs in which it helped, and not tuned for the cap.
B. Starts drawn by gradient norm
The cap takes weight from the states whose rollouts have
large gradients, and those are not a random subset: the gradient
norm is largely a function of the start state (rank correlation
0.96 between two rollouts of a start). Instead of cutting a large
gradient, we can sample its state more often and weight each
of its rollouts down, as in importance sampling of training
examples by gradient norm [21], [23]. Let q(s) be the rate at
which a replay state is drawn relative to uniform. Weighting
each rollout by 1/q leaves the batch gradient’s expectation
unchanged for any q > 0, and if q is proportional to the
gradient norm the weighted contributions all have the same
norm, so a cap finds nothing to remove. We call this the start
draw.
We approximate this with a small table. Each iteration takes
8B candidate states from the replay buffer and evaluates the
critic on each. The candidates are divided into 32 quantiles of
the critic’s value, and a table holds for each quantile a running
mean, over about 200 updates, of the gradient norms of the
rollouts started in it (norms above 30 medians count as 30).
The B starts are drawn with q proportional to the table’s entry
for their quantile, kept within a factor of 30 of the median
quantile’s. Every rollout is weighted by 1/q in the policy
gradient, before the cap of Eq. (5) is applied to the weighted
norms, and by the same 1/q in the critic’s loss. The critic’s
value predicts the gradient norm because both are functions
of how well the policy tracks from a state: the table explains
58% of the variance of log G. With the policy held fixed, the
draw lowers what a cap removes of the worth from 3% to 1%
at k = 10 and from 16% to 5% at k = 3 (Appendix A).
C. Model-ranked resets
The baseline’s reset distribution learns which bins are hard
from episodes started in them. On a large library that is slow:
a bin’s score changes only when it is visited, and at 4,096
environments the 746,000 bins of our training library are swept
once in about 25,000 iterations. The world model and the critic
can score every bin without visiting it. Every 2,000 iterations
we start an imagined rollout at the reference state of each bin,


follow the policy for 32 steps and bootstrap with the critic,
as in Eq. (1); the lowest 5% of bins by this imagined return
form the hard set. Resets keep the baseline’s mix and go to a
uniformly drawn bin with probability 0.5, to a bin of the hard
set with probability 0.4, and to a bin of earlier hard sets with
probability 0.1. No episode is started in the last 50 frames of
a clip, where too little of the motion is left for a return to
say how well it was tracked. The ranking is cheap: scoring all
bins of our training library takes about 30 s, and the twelve
rankings of a run add 3% to its training time.
On held-out clips the lowest imagined bin of a clip predicts
whether the policy fails on it with an area under the ROC
curve of 0.91 to 0.92, against 0.74 to 0.77 for the critic’s
value alone, which is the signal of critic-based curricula [26],
[27].
D. An average of the policy for evaluation
The policy that acts in training is noisy from update to
update: at the last checkpoint the baseline’s test return ranges
over 0.018 across three seeds, and that of an exponential
average of its weights with a time constant of 1,000 iterations
over 0.003. We keep such an average from iteration 5,000
and evaluate it. It never acts in training and is applied to the
baseline as well, so no difference we report comes from it.

A. Setup
Data and split. We use BONES-SEED [38], a library of
142,220 motion-capture clips, without the clips on stairs,

random offset, is held out. The training library has 109,233
clips (222.7 h) and the test library 11,828 clips of 353 motions
(21.3 h) that no actor performed in training. Both robots share
the split.

training budget.
VII. LIMITATIONS
The cap level is chosen, not derived. The two tests locate
where a gradient stops being worth its norm, at a few times
the median, and training is flat in the cap from 3 to 10. They
do not give a value, and we did not tune k on the full library.
The baseline’s settings. The baseline runs at its published
settings apart from the learning rate, which we tested: doubled,
it raises the baseline’s return by 0.0055 at 15,000 iterations,
about a third of the method’s gain there, and leaves its failing
clips unchanged (Table III). We have not tuned its other
settings, and the main comparison at 25,000 iterations is
against the published ones.

the update in a GPU graph as recent systems do [33], [34],

What the tests measure. The simulator test perturbs actions
along one direction per state, the model’s gradient, at two
scales, and it compares the model with the simulator it was
trained on, not with a robot. Both tests are of policies at the
end of training; at an early checkpoint, with a model that has
seen less data, the simulator confirms less in the middle bands
(Appendix A).
Motions beyond the robot. A handstand and burpees fail for
both methods on every seed (Fig. 6). Their references need
several times the torque of the G1’s wrist motors (Sec. VI-B),
so the failures come from our data filter, which kept them, and
not from either method. Filtering the library by the reference’s
torque, which the retargeter already computes, would remove
them. Since both methods train and are tested on the same
clips, these failures favor neither.
VIII. CONCLUSION
A learned world model gives an exact gradient of itself,
and for most imagined rollouts of a humanoid tracker that
gradient is nearly the simulator’s. For the few rollouts with
the largest gradients it is not: neither a redraw of the same
state nor the simulator backs more than a few medians of it.
Capping each rollout’s gradient at that level, drawing starts so
that the cap has less to remove, and resetting episodes where
the models predict failure turns this observation into a tracker
that follows unseen motions more closely, fails on fewer of
them, and reaches the baseline’s quality in less than half the
training time. The tests themselves need only a trained policy,
its world model and the simulator, and apply to any method
that differentiates a learned model.
