Abstract
General-purpose whole-body trackers can execute diverse
humanoid motions, but they require dense reference trajec-
tories. However, tasks usually specify only where, in which
posture, and when the robot should arrive, leaving the mo-
tion in between to a separate planner. Inspired by human mo-
tor coordination, our key idea is that a low-level controller
can organize the intervening motion and its pace by itself
from sparse goals with arrival times. In this paper, we pro-
pose S-BFM, a novel behavioral foundation model superior
in its autonomy: it generates whole-body transitions from
target postures, poses, and arrival times alone. Specifically,
we first introduce Spatial Grounding, which adds world-
frame position and heading to a forward-backward behavior
representation through spatial reconstruction. Then, Time-
Conditioned Distillation is designed to learn transitions and

dense-reference teacher. Lastly, Generative Fine-Tuning
is introduced to broaden the covered distances and arrival

to the best baseline under fixed and mixed horizons, and

spatiotemporal goals thus suffice for natural, well-timed
whole-body motion.
1. Introduction
Humanoid control is moving from task-specific skill policies
toward general-purpose whole-body control. Large motion
datasets and physics simulation have enabled universal track-
ers that turn diverse motion references into executable joint
commands. These trackers connect motion generators and
multimodal interfaces to the robot. TextOp continuously
generates short motion segments for a tracking policy to exe-
cute [39], SONIC links input modalities to control through a
unified motion interface [21], and SuperMimic trains whole-
body trackers efficiently with a shared differentiable world
model [19]. These trackers can execute diverse humanoid
motions, but they require dense reference trajectories.
However, task planning and interactive control require
robots to organize motion around task goals and adapt as
those goals change. With dense-reference interfaces, this
responsibility falls to a separate planner or generator. It must
supply the intervening motion and update future references
when the task changes while ensuring a smooth transition
from the motion already underway [21, 39]. Human move-
ment instead combines goal-directed decisions with auto-
matic coordination. For example, when walking toward a
table, the cerebral cortex helps select the goal and route.
Brainstem–spinal circuits support gait and posture while the
cerebellum uses sensory feedback to coordinate movement.
Together, these mechanisms allow people to focus on their
destination and surroundings without consciously control-
ling every step [6]. Inspired by this coordination, we ask
a novel question: Can a low-level controller accept sparse
goals with arrival times and organize the intervening motion
from its current physical state?
Prior work provides foundations: BFM-Zero supports
goal reaching and motion tracking via a shared behavior
space [18], and BeJEPA models whole-body posture together
with world position and heading [41]. MaskedMimic and
KINO produce physics-based motion from partial constraints
or keyframes [2, 32], and BFMTrack tracks sparse timed
goals by latent optimization [28]. Yet no existing method
delivers all three sparse-goal requirements at once: accurate
control of the world-frame pose, natural transitions between
goals, and timing that adapts to the requested arrival. A target
state alone does not specify how the intervening motion
should unfold, and imitating the pace of a demonstration
does not adapt it to new distances and durations.
Inspired by human motor coordination, our key idea is
that a low-level controller can organize the intervening
motion and its pace by itself from sparse goals with ar-
rival times. In this paper, we propose S-BFM (Sparse Be-
havioral Foundation Model), which is superior in its au-
tonomy: it generates whole-body transitions from target
postures, poses, and arrival times alone (Fig. 1). S-BFM is
trained in three stages within a reusable forward-backward
behavior space [8, 18, 34, 36]. Each stage targets one re-
quirement. Specifically, we first propose Spatial Grounding
(Sec. 3.3), which adds world-frame position and heading to
the behavior representation and uses spatial reconstruction
to make the encoder retain them. Then, Time-Conditioned
Distillation (Sec. 3.4) is designed to learn transitions and
pace from real motion: with the encoder and actor frozen,
a controller conditioned on the remaining time imitates a
dense-reference teacher in closed loop. As real demonstra-
tions cover only a limited range of distances and durations,
Generative Fine-Tuning (Sec. 3.5) is lastly introduced to
broaden this range with a lightweight motion generator dis-
tilled from Kimodo [25]. At deployment, S-BFM acts from
robot observations, sparse goals, and the remaining time,
without any intermediate reference.
We evaluate S-BFM in simulation on a 29-DoF Unitree
G1 against SONIC, UFO, BeJEPA, and PLAT, and on the
AGIBOT A3 humanoid. Under both fixed and mixed com-
mand horizons, S-BFM lowers global joint-position error
by over 32% relative to the best baseline. For fixed goals,
it raises the reach rate from 43.5% to 74.6% over BeJEPA,
increases the rate of holding the goal for five seconds from
7.4% to 35.2%, and cuts terminal position error from 1.67
to 0.26 m. At 3 to 4 m under lateral and downward pushes, it
still reaches 68.0% of goals, against 23.6% for BeJEPA. On
the real robot, S-BFM moves from a shared start pose to five
distinct goal postures (Fig. 1). Sparse spatiotemporal goals
thus suffice for natural, well-timed whole-body motion.
In summary, our main contributions are threefold:
1) We formulate sparse spatiotemporal goal control, in which
a low-level policy, much like the cerebellum, generates natu-
ral whole-body transitions that reach target postures at pre-
scribed world poses and times.
2) We propose S-BFM, trained through Spatial Grounding,
Time-Conditioned Distillation, and Generative Fine-Tuning,
which combines spatial goal reaching with the transitions
and pace of real motion.
3) In simulation, S-BFM outperforms all evaluated baselines
in sparse tracking accuracy, spatial goal reaching, and goal
retention, with over 32% lower global joint-position error
than the best baseline, and it runs on the full-size AGIBOT
A3 humanoid.

2. Related Work
Dense-reference whole-body control. Physics-based mo-
tion imitation learns whole-body skills by tracking reference
trajectories in simulation [4, 20, 23, 31]. H2O, TWIST,
and ExBody series enable versatile humanoid motion track-
ing and teleoperation [5, 11, 12, 16, 45, 46].
SONIC
scales tracking with model capacity, data, and compute [21],
SuperMimic and BlinkMimic train trackers with differ-
entiable world models [1, 19], and other work extends
whole-body control to rough terrain and highly dynamic
skills [3, 9, 13, 14, 17, 22, 30]. These trackers are general,
but they execute a reference rather than create one: given
only sparse goals, they still need another module to supply
the motion in between. In contrast, S-BFM needs no such
module at deployment.
Sparse and time-conditioned motion control. Sparse con-
trol specifies only desired states and leaves the intermediate
motion to the controller [15, 44]. Goal-conditioned value
functions and temporal difference models support goal- and
horizon-conditioned control [24, 29]. Forward-backward
representations enable zero-shot goal reaching and track-
ing, as demonstrated by BFM-Zero [18, 34, 35]. BeJEPA
adds world position and heading to body representation [41].
MaskedMimic inpaints physics-based motion from partial
constraints [32], KINO links vision-language planning to
control through whole-body keyframes [2], PLAT learns priv-
ileged latent transitions for sparse timed tracking [37], and
BFMTrack optimizes latent behavior sequences [28]. Un-
fortunately, none of these methods delivers accurate world-
frame pose control, natural transitions, and adaptive timing
at once. S-BFM addresses all three within one forward-
backward behavior space, with one stage per requirement.
Motion in-betweening and controllable generation. Mo-
tion in-betweening generates transitions between constrained
states. Robust Motion In-Betweening conditions on time-to-
arrival embeddings [10], diffusion models such as MDM and
CondMDI generate motion under keyframe constraints [7,
33], Kimodo supports text and kinematic constraints [25, 42],
and ARDY handles streaming prompts and sparse con-
straints [47]. To connect generation with execution, TextOp
pairs a generator with a tracker [39], InterPrior learns gener-
ative control for physical interaction [40, 43], and GRAIL
generates interaction demonstrations with video world mod-
els [38]. However, a kinematically valid motion does not
guarantee that the target pose and timing are kept during
closed-loop physical execution. S-BFM therefore uses a
generator only during training, to broaden the distances and
durations its controller learns from.
3. Method
Our goal is a single controller that receives a target posture,
position, heading, and arrival time, and produces the whole-
body motion that gets there on time. We first define this
problem (Sec. 3.1) and then describe how S-BFM learns it
in three stages (Fig. 2).
3.1. Problem Formulation
Each goal g specifies a target body state at a planar position
and heading, together with an arrival time T. The controller
must generate the intervening motion and reach g at time
T. The full state xt contains the whole-body configuration
and motion together with the root pose, and the control
observation ot contains proprioception, the estimated root
pose, and a short history of observations and actions.
Reference frame. Goals are placed in the world, so the
robot and all goals share a fixed frame W defined at the
start of execution. Its origin is the initial planar root position
p0,xy, and its reference direction is the initial heading ψ0:

3.2. Overview
Reaching a sparse goal on time requires the controller to
know where the goal is and how to move toward it at
the right pace. S-BFM learns this in three stages (Fig. 2).
Stage I (Sec. 3.3) builds a forward-backward behavior rep-
resentation that retains world-frame position and heading.
Stage II (Sec. 3.4) freezes this representation and trains a
time-conditioned controller to master the transitions and
pace of real motion. Stage III (Sec. 3.5) fine-tunes the con-
troller on generated motions that cover distances and time
horizons missing from real data.
The stages are trained in sequence rather than jointly so
that later stages do not overwrite what earlier ones learned.
After Stage I, the encoder and actor are frozen, and Stages II
and III train only a controller on top of them. At inference,
the encoder maps the goal to a goal code, the controller
combines this code with ot, ∆t, and m, and the actor turns
the resulting behavior code into joint actions. The spatial
readout, the dense teacher, and the motion generator are used
only during training.

follows a dense reference into a student that sees only the
sparse goal and the remaining time.

3.5. Stage III: Generative Fine-Tuning
Real motion pairs each transition with one distance and
one time horizon, whereas at deployment the same pair of
postures may be requested at other distances and horizons.
Stage III fills these gaps with generated motion and fine-
tunes the controller on it.
Distance-horizon expansion. We distill a pretrained Ki-
modo model [25] into a compact conditional motion gener-
ator Gω that accepts a text description together with spatial
and temporal constraints. For an initial state xs and a goal g,
we vary the travel distance d and horizon H and generate

Kd,H = K(xs, Td(g), H),
(12)
where Td moves the target to distance d without changing

The text ℓd,H comes from reusable templates that describe
the motion and its pace, while the numerical constraints fix
distance and horizon. For the same endpoint postures, differ-

velocities, which supervise the controller across remaining-

4. Experiments
We evaluate S-BFM through four questions: Q1: Can S-
BFM jointly track posture and world-space position from
sparse timed keyframes? Q2: Can S-BFM autonomously
reach and maintain spatial goals across distances and
perturbations without intermediate reference trajectories?
Q3: What control capabilities do spatial grounding, time-
conditioned distillation, and generative fine-tuning each con-
tribute? Q4: How does S-BFM perform on physical robots?

4.3. Spatial Goal Reaching and Retention
For Q2, we evaluate autonomous reaching of fixed, zero-
velocity goals over 25 s rollouts. We pair 15 initial and
15 target configurations covering prone and seated poses,
crouching, bowing and single-leg stance. For each pair,
two area-uniform target placements in each of four distance
annuli (0.4–1, 1–2, 2–3 and 3–4 m) yield 1,800 trials per
method and perturbation condition, with 450 trials per annu-
lus. S-BFM and PLAT receive a 4 s countdown that remains
zero after expiration. We evaluate unperturbed reaching and
goal completion with torso forces applied at 1 s for 0.2 s.
Disturbances use a lateral impulse per unit mass of 0.5 m/s,
or stronger lateral/downward impulses of 1.5/ −1.0 m/s.
Goal acquisition and retention. S-BFM achieves the high-
est Reach and Hold5 and the lowest terminal errors (Table 2).
Compared with BeJEPA, Reach increases from 43.5% to
74.6% and Hold5 from 7.4% to 35.2%, while terminal XY
error decreases from 1.667 to 0.257 m. The larger relative re-
duction in global than local MPJPE indicates more accurate
world-space placement alongside posture matching.
Long-range reaching under perturbations. Figure 4
shows that S-BFM maintains high goal-completion rates
over longer distances and under disturbances. In the 3–4 m
annulus, Reach is 67.1% for S-BFM versus 24.9% for Be-
JEPA without perturbations, and 68.0% versus 23.6% under
stronger lateral/downward perturbations. These results show
that S-BFM coordinates whole-body posture and world-
space displacement to autonomously reach goals across dis-
tances and perturbation conditions.

4.4. Ablation Study
To answer Q3, we compare P1, P2 without spatial grounding,
P1+P2, and P1+P2+P3 on the same motion tracking and
fixed-goal tasks (Table 4). P2 alone uses the released UFO
policy as its base.
Contributions of the training stages. P1 alone provides
effective motion and world-space position tracking, but its
on-time rate is only 4.63%. Without explicit supervision
of transition motions and execution timing, it may adopt
the prone goal posture while still far from the target loca-
tion (Fig. 5). This premature posture change can restrict
subsequent locomotion, making it difficult to complete the
remaining displacement and potentially preventing the robot
from reaching the target. P2 without P1 yields a terminal
XY error of 3.502 m, indicating that reference-motion dis-
tillation alone has not provided this variant with reliable
goal-position control in world coordinates. Combining P1
and P2 raises the on-time rate to 17.35% and reduces termi-
nal XY error to 0.597 m; the robot also approaches the target
before lying prone. These results show that time-conditioned
distillation built on the spatial behavior model coordinates
target-position control with transition organization. Adding
P3 further reduces sparse tracking errors and joint jerk.
Target retention under short commands. To assess P3 un-
der short remaining-time commands, we fine-tune the same
P1+P2 checkpoint for 10M steps using equal proportions of
real and generated references. We evaluate standing goals
at 2 and 3 m with remaining-time commands from 1 to 3 s
in 0.5 s increments, using 250 paired trials per distance. Ta-
ble 3 shows that P3 increases Hold5 from 13.6% to 92.0% at
2 m and from 39.2% to 89.6% at 3 m, while reducing Emax
at both distances. P3 thus substantially improves the reli-
ability of target-position retention after arrival under short
remaining-time commands.

4.5. Real-Robot Experiments
To answer Q4, we deploy the complete three-stage S-BFM
on the full-size AGIBOT A3 humanoid. Figure 6 presents
representative sparse tracking and transitions between whole-
body goal postures. Figure 7 illustrates time-adaptive lo-
comotion and natural get-up. From the issued goals, the
robot organizes intermediate steps and coordinates adjust-
ments of its arms, legs and trunk throughout the transitions.
Together with the goal-posture examples in Fig. 1, these
demonstrations show that the sparse-goal interface supports
coordinated whole-body control on physical hardware.
5. Conclusion, Limitation and Future Work
S-BFM enables humanoid robots to organize whole-body
motion and execution pace from sparse spatiotemporal goals,
without dense reference trajectories at deployment. Spatial
grounding and time-conditioned distillation support accurate
spatial control and coordinated transitions. Generative fine-
tuning extends control across a broader range of arrival-time
commands. Our current approach has three main limitations.
First, these capabilities are acquired through three succes-
sive training stages, which complicates the training pipeline.
Future work will explore incorporating remaining time and
explicit terminal conditions into forward–backward repre-
sentations and Bellman updates. Combining this formulation
with goal- and time-conditioned adversarial motion priors
may enable spatial control, natural transitions, and timing
control to be learned within a single stage.
Second, integration with vision–language–action and
world-action models remains unexplored. We will investi-
gate interfaces that allow these models to specify executable
spatial goals and arrival times. This will involve developing
suitable real-robot data collection procedures and adapting
high-level outputs and training objectives.
Third, we have not studied how high-level architectures
should be redesigned around S-BFM’s control capabilities.
Future work will explore architectures that focus on scene
understanding, task reasoning, and behavior goals critical to
task success, while S-BFM organizes intermediate motion.
Reachability and execution feedback could further help high-
level models refine their goals and timing decisions.
