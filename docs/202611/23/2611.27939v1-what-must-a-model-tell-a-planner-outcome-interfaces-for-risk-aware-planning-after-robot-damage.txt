What Must a Model Tell a Planner?
Outcome Interfaces for Risk-Aware Planning After
Robot Damage
Hassan Iqbal
The University of Texas at Austin
hassan.iqbal@utexas.edu
Krishna Kumar
The University of Texas at Austin
krishnak@utexas.edu
Abstract: When a robot is damaged, it must decide which routes it can still afford,
and the amount of risk it may accept can change from one mission to the next.
We argue that whether a learned model helps such a robot depends on its decision
interface, that is, on what the model hands to the planner. A decision policy tells the
planner what to do, whereas an outcome model, a kind of world model, predicts the
consequences of each route. We study simulated Unitree Go2 quadrupeds and H1
humanoids with weakened motors, added payloads, or reduced foot friction. We
compare four interfaces. The first is a decision policy, and the second is the same
policy trained with fixed penalties for infeasible routes. The third is an outcome
model that predicts each route’s feasibility and travel time. The fourth, which we
call a capability contract, is an outcome model that predicts the robot’s chance of
success and its travel time on each terrain and composes them along a route. All
share the same collected experience, matched tuning and risk calibration, and an
uncertainty guard. Two comparatively low-risk probes let the capability contract
rank hazardous terrains about as well as four new trials on every hazard. At a
calibrated 5% limit on selecting empirically infeasible routes, outcome models cut
declined missions from about 32% to 7–8% on Go2 and from 57% to 35–36% on
H1 compared with the plain policy. Fixed penalties narrow this gap, but the best
penalty changes with the risk target. Predicting outcomes lets the planner preserve
missions by changing routes as risk requirements tighten.
Keywords: World models, decision interfaces, risk-aware planning, conformal risk
control, legged robots

Introduction
Consider a quadruped whose front-left knee has lost half of its torque. Its planner can send it up
a short flight of stairs, along a mixed path with a single obstacle, or around a long detour over
gentle slopes, and the robot can always stop and ask for help. The terrain and available routes have
not changed, but the robot’s chances of completing them have. Testing the hazards by trial would
invite the falls the planner exists to prevent. The acceptable risk may change from one mission to
another, with stricter requirements near people than during a survey. If the preferred route becomes
unacceptable, the planner must still decide which alternative the damaged robot can attempt.
Policies map observations to choices [1, 2, 3], while world models predict consequences that can
inform those choices [4, 5]. We study their decision interface, what the learned component hands to
the planner. A decision policy scores routes, while an outcome model estimates feasibility or success
probability and travel time. Confidence that a route is preferred is not the same as the probability that
the robot can complete it.

Related work.
Damage recovery uses Gaussian process predictions to search for compensatory
behaviors [6], while adaptive locomotion learns how to act under changed dynamics [7, 8]. Whereas
these methods improve how the robot moves, we assess where its existing controller can still succeed.
Related assessment methods use competence estimates to decide when autonomy should defer to
human assistance [9], or predict perception performance from experience to avoid failure [10]. Our
Gaussian process instead learns across damaged robots how responses on easier terrain predict the
probability of successful traversal and travel time on hazards. This relationship lets two comparatively
low-risk probes assess a new robot without retesting every hazard. We evaluate the resulting hazard
ranking and the trials and falls required to obtain it against direct hazard retesting.
Model-free RL can learn locomotion without an explicit transition model [11], while model-based RL
can combine policy proposals with predicted rollouts [12, 13]. These approaches establish that learned
policies and outcome predictions can play complementary roles. We ask what a route planner gains
when information about physical execution remains explicit rather than being compressed into a route
choice. Navigation methods already incorporate deployment instructions [14, 15], and conformal
planners calibrate uncertain predictions or decisions [16, 17, 18, 19]. With shared locomotion,
experience, and risk calibration, we compare filtering a learned route choice with reevaluating
alternatives from route or terrain predictions. This comparison measures the missions retained as risk
targets change and tests whether predictions for individual terrains help when missions recombine
them.

The risk target α bounds the expectation of this loss under the calibration assumptions. A 5% target
therefore limits empirically infeasible selections to at most 5% of all offered missions in expectation,
including declined missions in the denominator. It does not bound falls, mission failure, or the error

comparison is how many missions each interface retains at the same calibrated target.

across robots and matched tuning. The decision policy learns scores for the oracle’s choice among
three routes and fallback. The oracle selects the fastest empirically feasible route using measured
traversal outcomes, not Gaussian-process predictions. The penalized decision policy subtracts a fixed
penalty P from training scores for infeasible routes. The route-outcome model learns each route’s

Gaussian process [20], and composes these estimates along routes. The contract learns how probe
observations predict hazardous-terrain outcomes from paired measurements on fitting robots. Probes
are chosen greedily to reduce error in predicted success. Route and mission descriptions, supervision,
and tuning appear in Appendix B.
Each planner uses an adjustable decision margin. The contract compares composed success estimates
with pmin plus the margin, while the route model compares its feasibility scores with 0.5 plus the
margin. Each takes the fastest admitted route, or falls back if none qualifies. The decision planner
commits only when its top route score exceeds the fallback score by the margin, so increasing the
margin can only replace commitments with fallback. Conformal risk control calibrates each margin
on 100 robots [21]. Changing α changes this calibration, while pmin remains fixed. A shared guard
uses predictive variance over probe observations to send unfamiliar robots to fallback. Appendix A
states the assumptions and the expected-loss guarantee.

Results
Outcome models retain more missions under risk limits.
Both outcome interfaces retain more
missions than the plain policy on Go2 and H1 under calibrated risk limits, despite similar agreement
with the oracle when no risk target is imposed (Fig. 2, Table 2). A well-chosen fixed penalty recovers
much of this benefit, nearly closing the gap on Go2 and narrowing it on H1, but the best penalty
changes with the risk target (Table 3). At the strictest target, the contract still retains more missions
than the best tested penalty on both platforms. The practical benefit is that the planner can change
routes as requirements change while reusing the same outcome predictor.
Two probes improve hazard assessment at low trial cost.
Observing the damaged robot improves
the ranking of hazardous terrains over the population average. Two designed probes raise AUC from
0.67 to 0.87 on Go2 and from 0.75 to 0.86 on H1 (Table 4). Four trials on every hazard achieve similar
ranking but require 16–18 times more runs and produce roughly 16–18 falls per robot, compared with
fewer than 0.1 during probing. Increasing the number of designed probes beyond two yields little
further improvement (Table 5).
5
Discussion and Future Work
Two comparatively low-risk probes let a damaged robot assess hazardous terrain without retesting
every hazard itself. The outcome interfaces then let the evaluated planners reconsider alternatives
and retain more missions than the plain policy under calibrated risk limits. At deployment, we can
change the risk target by recalibrating the planner’s margin without refitting its outcome predictor,
while the contract can compose predictions for new combinations of represented terrains.
A risk-conditioned policy can instead take the requested target as an input and learn the route
switch directly, but the policies evaluated here do not receive that target. Our comparison therefore
establishes the value of exposing reusable outcome estimates to this planner, rather than showing that
changing risk requirements intrinsically requires an outcome model.
As risk requirements tighten, the planner becomes increasingly conservative, trading mission retention
for fewer infeasible route selections. Future work should preserve useful route choices at stringent
targets, validate complete-route execution on physical robots, and test assessment and calibration
under broader shifts in robot damage and mission distributions.

Conditional on the fitting data, we assume that calibration robots and a new robot r⋆are exchangeable,
that probe design, feature transformations, model fitting, and tuning are fixed before calibration,
and that losses use a common fixed mission distribution or shared mission bank. The envelopes are
bounded by one, non-increasing, and at least as large as the original losses. Applying conformal risk
control gives E[Lr⋆(bλ)] ≤α under these assumptions [21, Corollary 1]. Conditional on the fitting
data, the expectation is over the calibration robots and the new robot, and over mission sampling
when missions are not drawn from a fixed shared bank. This is an application of the published result,
not a new theoretical guarantee.
The uncertainty guard compares the new robot’s probe variance s2(zr⋆) with a calibration quantile
τ. For δ = 0.05, τ is the ⌈(n + 1)(1 −δ)⌉-th smallest calibration variance. The guard replaces a
route selection with fallback when s2(zr⋆) > τ. Applied after calibration of the underlying rule,

The controlled loss concerns selection, not the physical probability of falling or failing a mission,
and it averages over all missions rather than only accepted ones. The guarantee is marginal, so the
observed test loss for an individual calibration split can exceed α. For example, at the 5% H1 target,
the largest observed split rates were 5.7% for the contract and 5.8% for the decision policy. The
guarantee does not extend to the new-combination mission distribution or the robots damaged beyond
the source population.

Descriptions and supervision.
A route is described by counts of edges in each context and terrain,
with 96 + 12 entries on Go2 and 128 + 16 on H1. A mission stacks the descriptions of its three
routes. The decision learners receive these descriptions and z, so they already have observations of
the damaged robot. Their oracle uses measured terrain outcomes to choose the feasible route of least
composed duration, or fallback if no route is feasible. The plain policy learns oracle-choice scores;
each penalized policy subtracts P ∈{0.3, 1, 3, 10} from the training score of every infeasible route.
Each penalty is trained separately and evaluated at all risk targets. The route-outcome model learns
feasibility and duration directly, tuning them separately. Its feasibility score is clipped to [0, 1] and
must exceed 0.5 plus the calibrated margin; the grid extends to 0.51, where every route is rejected.
Fitting and tuning.
Each split uses 200 fitting robots and 100 calibration robots, with 100 separate
test robots. All interfaces use kernel regression across robots, with exact leave-one-robot-out residuals
for tuning so a held-out robot contributes none of its items to fitting. The grid uses six robot-kernel
length scales and ridge penalties {0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1}. The route and decision models additionally use
linear or squared-exponential item kernels with scales of 0.5, 1, or 2 times the median pairwise item
distance. The fitting data and procedures are matched, while the targets, route representations, and
selection rules differ. The probe variance supplies the common uncertainty guard.
B.1
Robustness of the Policy Comparison
The decision policy selects the largest ridge penalty in the original grid, motivating a check with
penalties up to 100 and item length scales down to 0.125. This changes its fallback rates by at most
2.3 percentage points and its infeasible-selection rates by at most 1.0 point. A neural policy with
hidden layers of 256 and 128 units, ReLU activations, Adam optimization, and early stopping uses
the same inputs, labels, decision margin, and guard on the first five splits. Its infeasible-selection
rates are at most 4.6%, and it declines more familiar missions than the kernel policy. A further
check changes pmin to 0.5, which changes feasibility labels and oracle choices, so every interface is
retrained and recalibrated. Table 1 reports these comparisons. They test the sensitivity of the specified
policy family, without substituting for a policy conditioned on the requested α.

C.2
Damage Beyond the Source Population
On the 80 more severely damaged Go2 robots, the guarded interfaces select infeasible routes on fewer
than 5% of missions on average, while declining 44.8–60.5% (Table 2). This is an observed result
outside the calibration guarantee. The test examines more severe damage on one platform and does
not establish reliable detection of arbitrary distribution shifts.

Benchmark Details
Damage.
Each robot has zero to three weakened leg joints with probabilities 0.10, 0.35, 0.35, and
0.20, chosen among 12 Go2 joints or 10 H1 leg joints. Weakening factors in [0.25, 0.80] scale the
torque–speed envelope on Go2 and actuator torque limits on H1. The payload perturbation uses a
mass multiplier of 1.0–1.3 with a center-of-mass shift up to 3 cm, and foot friction is in [0.4, 1.0]. The
80 additional Go2 robots exceed these ranges in one factor, using four or five weak joints, actuation
factors 0.10–0.25, mass factors 1.30–1.45, or friction 0.25–0.40.
Terrains and probes.
Go2 terrains include flat ground, ascents of 12, 20, and 24◦, a 26◦descent,
stairs with 8, 12, and 14 cm steps, a 28 cm descent, rough ground of ±14 cm, and boxes of 12, 16, and
20 cm. H1 terrains include flat ground, ascents of 9–22◦, descents of 18 and 29◦, stairs of 26–34 cm,
a 44 cm descent, rough ground of ±26 and ±32 cm, and boxes of 38–50 cm. Terrains with mean
source success in [0.05, 0.8] are designated hazardous, giving nine on Go2 and eight on H1. Probe
9


candidates are flat ground, a 12◦ascent, and a 26◦descent on Go2, and flat ground, 9 and 12◦ascents,
and an 18◦descent on H1, each from four start states. The benchmark contains 145,920 Go2 and
109,072 H1 rollouts.
Successful traversal and falls.
Success requires covering 1.5 m on flat ground or slopes and
2.0–2.2 m elsewhere within 6–10 s without falling. A fall is registered when base clearance above
terrain drops below 0.14 m on Go2 or 0.55 m on H1, when roll or pitch exceeds 55–60◦on Go2 or
45◦on H1, or when the base or torso touches the ground.
