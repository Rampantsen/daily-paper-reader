ABSTRACT
Model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) can improve pretrained vision-language-action
(VLA) policies through imagined experiences. However, existing VLA-MBRL methods
primarily rely on large-scale image or video generation world models, incurring large
computational overhead. Latent world models offer a more efficient alternative but face
two challenges: compounding errors during recursive prediction and incompatibility with
VLAs’ visual input interfaces. In this work, we propose LS-MBPO, a lightweight residual
MBRL framework that leverages latent world models to improve upon pretrained VLAs.
LS-MBPO introduces two key techniques: (1) Hierarchical Forcing, which combines dense
causal supervision with autoregressive feedback to mitigate compounding prediction errors;
and (2) Latent Subchunk Rollouts, which leverage action chunks from a frozen VLA policy
and a latent residual policy for successive subchunk refinement, bridging the gap between
latent imagination and VLA policy execution. We further introduce a Progress-Conditioned
Branch-Start Curriculum that progressively shifts rollout starting points from later to earlier
task stages, enabling effective policy learning from short imagined rollouts. Extensive
experiments on LIBERO, RoboTwin, and a real-world ALOHA robot show that LS-MBPO
achieves superior task performance and training efficiency compared to existing methods.

Introduction
World models [Yu et al., 2026, Zhang et al., 2026b] provide a promising avenue for improving vision-
language-action (VLA) policies through model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) [Janner et al., 2019],
as they enable the generation of imagined experiences for policy optimization while reducing reliance on
costly real-world interactions. Recent approaches [Zhang et al., 2026b, Zhu et al., 2026, Sheng et al., 2026]
have primarily explored this direction by leveraging large-scale generative world models to predict future
observations in the pixel space. However, generating high-dimensional visual observations introduces
substantial computational overhead, while pixel-level prediction does not necessarily provide the compact
state representations most suitable for downstream policy learning. Latent world models offer a promising
alternative by modeling environment dynamics directly in a compact representation space, avoiding the
need for explicit future image generation. Moreover, recent advances in latent world modeling, such as
LeWorldModel [Maes et al., 2026], demonstrate the potential of learning task-relevant representations that
capture essential information for downstream decision-making. These properties make latent world models
particularly appealing for developing more computationally efficient MBRL frameworks for VLA policies.
Despite these advantages, integrating latent world models into VLA-based MBRL remains challeng-
ing due to two fundamental challenges.

objectives, where predictions are conditioned on ground-truth latent histories. However, during imag-
ined rollouts, the model must recursively take its own predictions as inputs, creating a discrepancy between
training and inference conditions. Prediction errors at earlier steps can propagate to subsequent predictions,
while the resulting deviations in latent inputs may further amplify these errors, leading to increasingly unre-
liable imagined trajectories. Second, there exists a mismatch between the interfaces of latent world models
and pretrained VLAs. Latent world models predict future states in compact latent spaces, whereas pretrained
VLAs typically require visual observations as inputs, making it difficult to directly use predicted latent
states for subsequent action generation. Besides the interface mismatch, existing VLA-MBRL approaches,
such as VLA-MBPO [Zhang et al., 2026b], often rely on fine-tuning all VLA parameters using imagined
experiences. Such full-parameter optimization introduces substantial computational and memory overhead,
which undermines the computational advantage of using latent world models for policy learning. These
challenges raise an important question: how can we leverage latent imagination for VLA reinforcement
learning while ensuring reliable recursive prediction and computationally efficient policy optimization?
To address these challenges, we propose Latent Subchunk Rollout for MBPO (LS-MBPO), a lightweight
residual model-based reinforcement learning framework for VLA using latent world models, which is
shown in Fig. 1. First, we introduce Hierarchical Forcing to improve the reliability of latent dynamics under
recursive prediction. It combines dense causal prediction supervision within each temporal window with
autoregressive feedback across consecutive windows, enabling the world model to progressively adapt to
its own predictions while mitigating compounding errors. Second, we propose Latent Subchunk Rollouts
to bridge the interface gap between pretrained VLAs and latent world models. Specifically, we freeze the
pretrained VLA and divide its generated action chunk into shorter subchunks as base actions, while training
a lightweight residual policy conditioned on the current latent state and the corresponding base actions.
During imagination, the residual policy progressively refines the base actions based on the updated latent
states predicted by the world model. In this way, a single VLA query provides a behavioral prior for multiple
successive residual decisions, allowing the policy to be optimized through the lightweight residual policy
and value network. Furthermore, we introduce a Progress-Conditioned Branch-Start Curriculum that gradually
shifts the sampling distribution from states near task completion to earlier task stages, enabling more efficient
learning from imagined rollouts. On LIBERO Long, LS-MBPO completes the world model training on 4
RTX 5090s and the RL stage on a single RTX 5090 with a success rate of 71.2%, compared with 64.4% for
VLA-MBPO trained on 4 H100 GPUs, demonstrating a practical route towards resource-efficient VLA policy
improvement.
Our contributions are as follows: First, we propose LS-MBPO, a lightweight residual model-based reinforce-
ment learning framework for improving VLA with latent world models. To our knowledge, this is the first

Related Works
2.1
World Models for Visual Control
World models have been widely explored for visual control by learning action-conditioned environment
dynamics. Early approaches, such as Visual Foresight [Finn and Levine, 2017], predict future visual observa-
tions to support planning. Advances in generative modeling have enabled increasingly general and realistic
world simulation: UniSim [Yang et al., 2024] learns an interactive simulator supporting vision-language
planning and reinforcement learning, while NVIDIA Cosmos [NVIDIA et al., 2025] demonstrates the po-
tential of large-scale video models for physical world simulation and synthetic data generation. More
recently, Ctrl-World [Guo et al., 2026] enables policy-in-the-loop imagination for robot manipulation, allow-
ing pretrained vision-language-action (VLA) policies to be evaluated and improved using generated visual
trajectories. However, synthesizing high-dimensional visual observations incurs substantial computational
overhead, and visual fidelity alone does not guarantee representations suited to downstream control. In par-
allel, latent world models learn action-conditioned dynamics in compact representation spaces, supporting
planning and policy learning without decoding images during imagined rollouts. Representative meth-
ods, including PlaNet [Hafner et al., 2019], Dreamer [Hafner et al., 2020], and TD-MPC [Hansen et al., 2022],
demonstrate the effectiveness of latent-space imagination for model-based control. More recent methods
[Pang et al., 2025, Zhou et al., 2025, Maes et al., 2026, Liu et al., 2026, Zhang et al., 2026a] further explore vi-
sual control through predictive dynamics over pretrained visual features and jointly learned representations.
These advances motivate investigating how latent-space imagination can support efficient reinforcement
learning of VLA policies while remaining compatible with their visual observation interfaces.

Method
This section presents the method LS-MBPO, a lightweight residual model-based reinforcement learning
framework for VLA using latent world models. It is supposed that there exists an offline dataset D and a
small-scale expert dataset G. We will use D to train a latent world model W (Sec. 3.1) and optimize the latent
residual policy using W and G (Sec. 3.2). We also include a branch-start sampling strategy to improve the
training efficiency in Sec. 3.3.

Given the latent representations, we learn an action-conditioned dynamics model to predict future states

representations and the corresponding actions as input, and predicts the next-step latent representations.
Prior works [Zhou et al., 2025, Maes et al., 2026] typically train the predictor using one-step prediction

of imagined trajectories.

Residual Policy Optimization via Latent Subchunk Rollouts
To optimize VLA policies using imagined trajectories generated by the latent world model, we need to
enable closed-loop policy decisions within latent imagination. However, directly integrating pretrained
VLAs with latent world models is non-trivial due to their inherent interface mismatch: while latent world
models predict future states in a compact latent space, pretrained VLAs typically require visual observations
as input to generate actions. Consequently, the predicted latent states cannot be directly fed into the original
VLA to continue the rollout. To address this issue, we introduce a lightweight residual policy that directly
takes the latent states predicted by the world model as input to do Latent Subchunk Rollouts.

3.3
Progress-Conditioned Branch-Start Curriculum
Although latent subchunk rollouts provide an efficient way to generate imagined experiences, their limited
rollout horizon may prevent branches starting from early task stages from reaching successful states. This
makes it difficult for the residual policy to obtain informative success feedback, particularly during early
training. To address this issue, we introduce a Progress-conditioned Branch-start Curriculum (PBC), which
gradually shifts the sampling distribution of imagined rollout starting points from later task stages to earlier
ones.
We train a progress predictor gp using the expert dataset G to estimate task completion levels from latent
representations and corresponding language instructions:
ˆpt = gp(zt, ℓ)
where ˆpt denotes the estimated progress at time step t. We use the predicted progress exclusively for
branch-start sampling rather than directly as a reward signal. The progress predictor is trained on expert
demonstrations, making its predictions unreliable on states induced by imperfect or exploratory policies.
Using such predictions as dense rewards may introduce exploitable estimation errors, allowing the policy to
optimize the predictor’s outputs rather than make genuine task progress. In contrast, branch-start sampling
does not require highly accurate progress estimates, as it primarily relies on coarse task-stage information.
For example, a state with an actual progress of 0.9 may be predicted as 0.7, but it can still be recognized as a
relatively late-stage state and assigned a higher sampling probability.
To be specific, let Kstart denote the set of candidate branch starting states sampled from trajectories collected
through environment interaction. At training epoch e, we construct a progress-conditioned sampling

Baselines.
Since our method adopts a residual policy attached to a frozen pretrained VLA for RL optimiza-
tion, we follow the same design principle for all other baselines to ensure a fair comparison. Comparisons
with methods that update the full VLA parameters are deferred to the ablation studies. The baselines are as
follows: (1) π0.5-SFT: The base model trained using SFT for LS-MBPO along with the other baselines. (2)
RLT-BC: A residual policy which takes RL token as the task feature input and is trained with BC. Unlike the
original RLT, we follow the approach adopted in VLARLKit [Sun, 2026] and directly apply mean pooling to
the PaliGemma embeddings to obtain the RL Token. (3) Latent-BC:A residual policy which takes the world
model latent of W as the task feature input and is trained with BC. (4) RLT-RL: A residual policy which takes
RL token as the task feature input and is trained using online RL. (5) Latent-RL: A residual policy which
takes the world model latent of W as the task feature input and is trained using online RL. (6) Dreamer: A
latent MBRL baseline that trains the latent world model in a Dreamer [Hafner et al., 2023] style and uses
subchunk rollouts for residual policy optimization.
Implementation Details.
For the residual policies trained by both LS-MBPO and the baselines, we adopt
the following inference procedure: the VLA generates a base action chunk of length H, and the residual
policy refines its first h actions. These refined actions are executed in the environment before querying the
VLA again with the updated observation. During imagined rollouts in LS-MBPO, we instead reuse a single
VLA-generated base action chunk and successively refine its subchunks as described in Sec. 3.2. We denote
the actual imagined rollout horizon by L ≤H, measured in environment steps, where L is a multiple of
h. Each branch therefore processes the first L/h subchunks without re-querying the VLA. We use h = 5,
H = 50, and L = 50 for all tasks unless otherwise specified.
During the training of MBRL algorithms, including LS-MBPO and Dreamer, the residual policy interacts
with the simulator to collect 10 trajectories per epoch, following the rollout procedure described above. These
10 trajectories are then used as starting points for branch rollouts within the corresponding epoch. After
completing the branch rollouts, we perform RL updates using the collected rollout data. The algorithms
are trained for a total of 10 epochs. For RLT-RL and Latent-RL, we ensure the same number of simulator
interactions as LS-MBPO and Dreamer, resulting in a total of 100 trajectories.

robot. As shown in Fig. 3(a), both Dreamer and LS-MBPO achieve higher success rates than the SFT-trained
π0.5 baseline through MBRL with latent subchunk rollouts. Moreover, the Hierarchical Forcing helps mitigate
the compounding error of the latent world model, which empowers LS-MBPO to outperform Dreamer in
both tasks.
4.3
Effectiveness of Hierarchical Forcing
To evaluate the effectiveness of Hierarchical Forcing, we compare the latent world model in LS-MBPO with:
(1) LeWM, a LeWM-style baseline [Maes et al., 2026] trained with one-step latent prediction under teacher
forcing and auxiliary multi-view image reconstruction; and (2) Recursive-only, which uses the same recursive
feedback as Hierarchical Forcing but applies the latent prediction loss only to the final output position of
each window. All variants use the same model architecture and auxiliary loss terms. We evaluate the three
world models on 10 held-out trajectories from LIBERO Long, using identical initial image observations and
recorded action sequences. Each model recursively predicts 100 environment steps, corresponding to 20
subchunks. We report two metrics: (1) PSNR-FINAL, evaluated at the final prediction step; and (2) PSNR-
ALL, averaged over all prediction steps. Both metrics compare decoded images with their corresponding
ground-truth observations. The results are reported in Tab. 2. Hierarchical Forcing achieves the highest
PSNR-FINAL and PSNR-ALL, indicating improved decoded-image fidelity during recursive rollouts. More
qualitative comparisons are provided in Appx. B.4.
Furthermore, we train separate residual policies using subchunk rollouts generated by the three world
models, while keeping the base VLA and policy optimization protocol identical. The corresponding success
rates are reported in the Policy column of Tab. 2. Compared with Recursive-only, Hierarchical Forcing
improves policy success from 68.0% to 71.2%. Together, these results support the benefit of dense within-
window latent prediction supervision for both recursive prediction quality and downstream residual policy
learning.

4.4
Effectiveness of Latent Subchunk Rollouts
Since our latent world model is trained with an auxiliary decoder, it is also possible to perform recursive
rollouts by re-querying the VLA with reconstructed observations. After each corrected h-step subchunk,
we decode the predicted latent state into multi-view observations and query the frozen VLA to generate
a new base action chunk. The residual policy then refines the first h actions of this chunk to continue the
imagined rollout. This process can be repeated to enable closed-loop recursive rollouts. The performance
is reported in Fig. 3(b), where LS-MBPO outperforms this decoder-based VLA requery approach, which
we call VLA-Requery in the figure. Two factors, in our perspective, contribute to the performance drop.
First, in our latent subchunk rollouts, the VLA already provides a complete base action chunk that serves
as a strong behavioral prior for multiple successive residual decisions, making repeated VLA inference
unnecessary. Second, image reconstruction inevitably introduces extra errors, which may further affect the
actions generated by the VLA when conditioned on reconstructed observations.
4.5
Ablation Study
Effect of PBC.
To evaluate the effectiveness of Progress-Conditioned Branch-Start Curriculum (PBC), we
compare it with two alternatives: (1) Uniform sampling, which samples branch starting states uniformly;
and (2) Late-stage sampling, which favors later-stage states using a fixed sampling rule throughout training.
All variants are trained for the same number of epochs. As shown in Fig. 3(b), both uniform sampling and
late-stage sampling achieve lower success rates than PBC. These results suggest that prioritizing later-stage
states benefits policy learning, while gradually expanding the branch-start distribution toward earlier task
stages yields further improvement.
Binary Success Reward Versus Progress Reward.
Our method uses binary success rewards (0/1) to
optimize the residual policy, while the progress predictor is exclusively used for PBC. A natural question is
whether replacing the binary reward with progress-based rewards could provide denser learning signals
and potentially lead to better performance. To investigate this, we conduct two additional comparison
experiments: (1) using the progress predictor as the reward function; (2) using the combination of progress
reward and binary reward. However, the results, seen in Fig. 3(b), show that progress-based rewards do
not yield satisfactory performance. We consider this is caused by limited generalization capability of the
progress predictor, which is trained on a small set of expert demonstrations and may produce unreliable
estimates on states outside the expert distribution. As the policy explores such states, inaccurate progress
predictions can introduce misleading reward signals and distort policy updates.

5
Conclusion
This study explores how to apply a latent world model to improve VLA through RL optimization. We propose
a novel approach, LS-MBPO, which optimizes a residual policy attached to pretrained VLA using imagined
Subchunk Rollouts from a latent world model learned by Hierarchical Forcing. Extensive experiments
demonstrate that LS-MBPO is capable of being applied to a variety of robotic platforms, enabling a robust
and practical pathway towards lightweight model-based RL for robotics. Despite the promising results,
LS-MBPO still leaves room for further exploration. First, future work can extend latent world models to
support full-horizon rollouts for longer imagined interactions. Another potential direction is developing
more generalizable dense reward models in latent space to provide richer feedback for policy optimization
beyond binary success signals. Last but not least, future research can focus on learning generalizable latent
world models from diverse tasks and robotic platforms to enable knowledge transfer and facilitate more
scalable model-based policy learning across embodiments. We believe these directions hold great potential
for advancing latent world models and enabling more effective VLA training in the future.

A.1
More Details for World Model Training
The structure of decoder.
For Image reconstruction in the latent world model, we reconstruct multi-
view observations by splitting the predicted latent vector into view-specific components and decoding
each component with an independently parameterized convolutional decoder. An MLP followed by a
linear projection maps each component to a 192 × 16 × 16 feature map. Four residual upsampling blocks
progressively increase the spatial resolution to 256 × 256, with output channel widths of 96, 48, 32, and
32. Each block performs 2× nearest-neighbor upsampling followed by two 3 × 3 convolutions with Group
Normalization and SiLU activations, while a final 1 × 1 convolution produces the three-channel image.
Proprioceptive prediction.
In addition to image reconstruction, we train an auxiliary proprioceptive
decoder to predict the robot’s proprioceptive state from the full predicted latent representation. At each
recursive training step, this decoder is applied to all output positions and supervised using the corresponding
ground-truth proprioceptive states. During imagined rollouts, the next proprioceptive state is decoded from
the predicted next latent and used to update the proprioceptive history provided to the residual actor and
critics. For simplicity and clarity, proprioceptive inputs and their prediction are not included in the notation
and figures in the main text of the paper.

A.3
More Details about PBC
Starting imagined branches from later task stages can reduce the exploration required to encounter successful
outcomes. This allows the residual policy to first learn the local corrective behaviors required for task
completion. Meanwhile, successful trajectories provide positive reward samples, i.e., the binary reward, for
the critic, helping it distinguish state-action pairs with different expected task returns. This is particularly
useful during early training, when insufficient successful experience may limit the critic’s ability to establish
meaningful value differences.
As training proceeds, PBC gradually shifts the preferred branch-start states toward earlier task stages. The
policy must then learn to reach and complete the later-stage behaviors practiced previously. The critic can
use its existing value estimates for these later states to propagate success-related information to earlier states
through temporal-difference updates. This curriculum is therefore intended to establish task-completion
behaviors and useful value estimates before extending learning to earlier portions of the task.

B.1
Effect of VLA Prior
In LS-MBPO, the VLA provides base actions as behavioral priors, upon which the residual policy is optimized.
To investigate the contribution of the VLA prior, we additionally train a latent policy without access to
VLA-generated actions. Specifically, we keep the latent world model fixed and first pretrain the latent policy
using behavior cloning (BC) on expert demonstrations. We then optimize this policy through branch-rollout
MBRL within the latent world model. We consider two variants: one optimizes the policy using only the RL
objective during MBRL, while the other additionally incorporates the BC loss as a regularization term.
The results are reported in Fig. 5. We observe that, without BC regularization, the policy achieves very low
success rates, likely because the challenging tasks make policy learning highly sensitive to deviations from
the expert behavior. Once the policy deviates from successful behaviors, it may fail to discover successful
trajectories through exploration, leading to further performance degradation. Adding BC regularization
does yield improvements, but the performance is still below ours. In contrast, LS-MBPO achieves much
better performance by leveraging the VLA to provide a stable behavioral prior, which guides residual policy
learning and makes policy optimization more effective.
B.2
Effect of Decoder
To evaluate the impact of the decoder in latent world model training, we compare our world model with
a variant trained without the decoder, where the image reconstruction loss in Eq. 1 is removed. We then
use both world models for subsequent MBRL with latent subchunk rollouts. The results in Fig. 5 show that
our world model achieves greater policy improvement, suggesting that the reconstruction objective helps
preserve additional information in latent representations that is beneficial for downstream policy learning. It
is observed in LeWorldModel [Maes et al., 2026] that removing the decoder leads to better performance in
planning. We think that this finding does not contradict our results, as planning and policy learning may have
different representation requirements. Planning often benefits from compact and abstract representations
that capture task-relevant dynamics while filtering out unnecessary visual details. In contrast, RL-based
policy learning involves learning state-dependent action selection and value estimation, which may benefit
from retaining richer information about the environment and object states.
