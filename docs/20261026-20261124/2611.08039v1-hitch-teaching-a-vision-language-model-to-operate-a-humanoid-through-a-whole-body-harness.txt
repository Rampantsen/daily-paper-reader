# HITCH: Teaching a Vision-Language Model to Operate a Humanoid through a Whole-Body Harness

Abstract—Enabling vision-language models (VLMs) to control humanoid robots requires both reliable whole-body interfaces and knowledge of how to use the body that carries over across tasks. We present HITCH (Harness Interfaces To Command Humanoids), a harness in which perception returns the 3D position of any point the VLM selects in its image, and execution turns commands for the pelvis, torso, hands, walking, and gaze into whole-body motion with a learned tracker. We find that both measuring the scene and executing commands accurately raise task success. We then fine-tune a VLM on trajectories in which teacher policies solve five base tasks through HITCH, so that it learns how to operate the humanoid. Without task-specific trajectories, the fine-tuned model completes 51% of SIMPLE episodes and 42.5% of HumanoidArena episodes, and adding five trajectories per SIMPLE task raises its SIMPLE success to 95%. On a real G1, it carries a box and walks to a goal without real training data.

## I. INTRODUCTION

General-purpose humanoid robots should carry out everyday loco-manipulation tasks from language instructions, for example, walking to a table, crouching to pick up a cup, and placing it in a basket on another table. Each of these steps requires the legs, torso, and arms to act together while the head camera keeps the target in view, and every motion must match the geometry of a scene the robot has never seen.

A common approach trains vision-language-action (VLA) models [1], [2] or world action models [3] that map observations and instructions directly to whole-body actions. These models produce fluent motion, but they usually need many demonstrations of each task, and their performance can drop when only a few are available [4]. For a humanoid, each demonstration requires whole-body teleoperation or motion planning, which makes every new task expensive to add.

Agents built on foundation models offer another route, but on a humanoid they lack two things. Prompted language models and vision-language models (VLMs) already solve many tabletop tasks without task-specific training [5]–[7]. The first thing they lack on a humanoid is an interface to the whole body. Existing humanoid agents mostly select skills from a library or command task-space controllers [8], [9], which leave the posture of the legs and torso to the controller. A task may require the agent to set this posture itself, and the interface must execute each command accurately, since the agent observes again only after a command finishes. The second is skill in operating a humanoid body. Pretraining contains few examples of humanoid motion: a model may understand 3D space well yet know little about how far a humanoid can reach, or when it must step or crouch to bring its hands to a target. RAX acquires such knowledge through online exploration of each new task, with success that grows with the exploration budget [10]. Yet much of this knowledge is shared across tasks and could be learned once.

We first build HITCH (Harness Interfaces To Command Humanoids), a harness through which a VLM observes and controls the robot. Its perception interface lets the model measure the scene: it selects a point in the image and receives the 3D position of that point from depth, so it does not have to guess distances from the image alone. Its execution interface exposes commands for each body part, such as pelvis height, torso orientation, hand waypoints, walking, and gaze. A planner converts them into whole-body references, which a learned tracker executes on a Unitree G1. We train this tracker further on the planner’s references. Given the same commands, it executes them more accurately than baseline trackers, and more tasks succeed with it.

With this harness, the VLM only has to choose the next call, but nothing in the harness tells it how to choose. We therefore fine-tune Qwen3.8-27B on trajectories in which teacher policies use the same interfaces to solve five base tasks: tabletop pick-and-place, carrying objects between pieces of furniture, navigation, opening drawers and cabinet doors, and opening room doors. From these trajectories, the model learns skills for operating a humanoid through the harness, such as where to stand, how low to crouch, how far to lean, and what to measure before acting.

We find that these skills carry over to new tasks in simulated benchmarks [4], [11], and to real scenes on a G1 without real training data. On the SIMPLE humanoid benchmark, the model trained on the base tasks completes about half of the episodes without task-specific trajectories, and adding five trajectories per task raises its success to 95%. Compared with training on the task trajectories alone, training on the base tasks as well raises success at every number of trajectories we tried. Without its perception interface, the model trained on the base tasks solves only 2.8% of SIMPLE’s episodes. On a real G1, the model completes 13 of 20 carrying and 6 of 10 navigation episodes.

Our contributions are:

• a harness for humanoid agents with two interfaces: perception, which measures the scene on demand, and execution, which turns body-part commands into accurate whole-body motion;

• teacher policies that solve five humanoid base tasks through these interfaces, acting only on what the agent observes, and a VLM fine-tuned on their trajectories that carries over to new tasks;

• experiments on two humanoid benchmarks and a real G1, covering transfer to new tasks, learning from a few trajectories per task, transfer to real scenes, and ablations of perception and execution.

## II. RELATED WORK

## A. Humanoid Whole-Body Tracker

The low-level controller is a central component of a humanoid system, as it largely determines the precision and robustness of the executed motion. With recent advances in motion tracking and teleoperation, such controllers are now mostly learned. Whole-body trackers trained with reinforcement learning on human motion follow full-body kinematic references [12]–[18], and SONIC [19] scales this approach to one controller that can be driven by teleoperation, a kinematic planner, or a VLA. Decoupled designs pair a learned lowerbody policy with directly commanded arms [20], [21], and HOVER [22] supports several command modes in one policy. Since a tracker reduces whole-body control to producing a feasible reference, it can execute the output of a higher-level module, such as these planners or a grasp and motion planning pipeline [23]. We extend this design to serve an agent: a planner converts the agent’s body-part commands into whole-body references, and a tracker trained with HEFT [18] executes them. We further train the tracker on references produced by this planner so that it follows the agent’s commands more closely, and compare it with other trackers as executors of the same commands.

## B. Vision-Language-Action and World Action Models

Vision-language-action (VLA) models build on pretrained VLMs to map images and instructions to actions [24]–[26], and world action models predict future observations together with actions [27], [28]. Humanoid models of this kind are either pretrained on human or cross-embodiment data and fine-tuned on whole-body demonstrations [1]–[3], [29], or trained to predict locomotion, posture, or motion commands for a lower-level controller [30]–[32]. Benchmarks such as HumanoidArena and SIMPLE evaluate them on shared tasks [4], [11].

Because the humanoid models above learn motion directly from demonstrations, their generalization is often limited to variations of the trained tasks. We take a different approach: our agent leaves motion to the tracker and learns how to act through interfaces, and what it learns transfers to unseen tasks with little or no task-specific data.

## C. Foundation Models and Agentic Robot Control

Language and vision-language models can control robots without task-specific training. They select skills [33], [34] or write policy code [5], [6], and solve many manipulation tasks this way. Most of this progress concerns arms and mobile manipulators; on humanoids, foundation models are mostly prompted to drive skill libraries or task-space controllers [8], [9], [35]. When a model lacks the knowledge a task requires, it can gain it from experience: RAX explores at scale and compiles successful attempts into reusable programs [10], but each new task still requires online exploration. Fine-tuning offers another route, and open VLMs trained on trajectories collected in a harness, which defines the observations, tools, and feedback of the agent, learn to operate robot arms [36], [37].

A humanoid poses a harder problem for agents: its hands reach a target only after it walks, crouches, or turns its torso, and its camera moves with its head, so the agent must learn how to operate the whole body. Our agent measures the scene and commands each body part through separate interfaces, and learns these skills from teacher trajectories on base tasks so that they carry over to new tasks.

## III. METHOD

Our agent is a VLM that follows a language instruction on a Unitree G1 humanoid over a sequence of turns (Fig. 2). In each turn, it makes one call: a query to a perception interface, which measures the scene, or a command to an execution interface, which moves the body. Together, the two interfaces form the harness through which the VLM observes and controls the robot. The agent learns to operate the robot through these interfaces from the trajectories of teacher policies. Section III-A defines the agent loop and two properties of this setting that shape our design, Sections III-B and III-C describe the two interfaces, Section III-D how we collect teacher trajectories, and Section III-E how we fine-tune the agent on their trajectories.

## A. Problem Setting

We model an episode as a multi-turn interaction between the VLM and the robot. It starts from a language instruction ℓ and an initial observation o<sub>1</sub>. At turn t, the agent produces a response $a _ { t }$ that contains one interface call and may contain free-form reasoning, and the call returns the next observation:

$$
a _ {t} \sim \pi_ {\theta} (\cdot | \ell , h _ {t}), \qquad h _ {t} = (o _ {1}, a _ {1}, o _ {2}, \ldots , a _ {t - 1}, o _ {t}),\tag{1}
$$

where $\pi _ { \theta }$ is the VLM and $h _ { t }$ is the history. The agent conditions on the whole interaction so far, so it can refer back to positions it measured in earlier turns.

A perception query returns the measured positions and the image with the queried points marked. A command returns a new head-camera image and the body state after the motion, covering the pelvis, torso, feet, palms, fingers, and camera. Each item of the body state gives both the value the agent commanded and the value read from joint encoders and the IMU, so the agent can detect when a hand was blocked by an object or a crouch fell short. In a typical episode, the agent looks for the target, measures it, moves, and looks again to check the result, repeating this cycle until the task is complete.

Two properties of this setting shape our design. First, the agent decides at a low rate. A VLA observes again after each short chunk of actions and adjusts its motion as it goes, whereas our agent spends seconds on each turn, and a command runs to completion before the agent observes again. The agent must therefore know where its targets are before it acts: if it could only judge positions from the image, it would have to home in on a target over several turns, moving, looking, and adjusting by the offset it sees. Its commands must also be executed accurately, with the body reaching the commanded posture and the palms their commanded positions. The next observation reveals an error, but correcting it costs another turn, and some errors cannot be undone, such as knocking the object off the table.

Second, every decision in the training data must be explainable from what the agent receives: the instruction and its observations. Our teachers are code policies in simulation and could read the pose of any object from the simulator, which the agent cannot. Suppose the robot faces a table and must put an object into a basket on a second table behind it. If the instruction does not mention the basket and the teacher turns straight to it, nothing in the agent’s input explains the turn. A policy trained on such demonstrations can reproduce it only by remembering the training layouts, and in a new scene it may place the object at the remembered position without having looked. This is a known failure of imitation when the teacher acts on information the student lacks [38]. The same holds for facts that are constant across a task, such as the side on which the destination lies or how the object must be left. A policy trained on many demonstrations of a task picks up such facts from the demonstrations, so a short instruction suffices for it. An agent that has not seen these demonstrations must be told such facts in the instruction.

The first property shapes the two interfaces: the perception interface (Section III-C) lets the agent measure the positions of its targets before it acts, and the execution interface (Section III-B) turns each command into accurate whole-body motion. The second shapes the teachers (Section III-D), which act only on the instruction and their own measurements, and the instructions we write for benchmark tasks (Section IV-A), which state the facts that hold in every episode of a task but no positions or other facts that vary between episodes.

## B. Whole-Body Execution

The execution interface must expose the degrees of freedom that our tasks use, including pelvis height and torso pitch and yaw, and execute each command as stated. A planner converts each command into a whole-body reference, and a learned tracker follows this reference.

1) Observation Frame: Commands and measurements use the observation frame O. Its origin lies on the ground below the pelvis, with x along the pelvis heading, y to the left, and z up. O is redefined at every observation and stays fixed while a command runs, so positions measured since the latest observation apply directly to the next command. If the robot has not walked since an earlier observation, the new observation gives the pose of the new O in the old one, so earlier positions can be converted. After a walk, the robot’s displacement is known only approximately, so this pose is uncertain and earlier positions are no longer reliable.

2) Tracker: The tracker is a whole-body motion tracking policy trained with HEFT [18]. It is trained for a G1 with either Dex3 hands or rubber hands, runs at 50 Hz, follows a reference for all joints, and keeps the robot balanced. We further train it on references that the planner produced while teachers solved the base tasks (Section III-D). Some of the postures the agent commands are rarely covered by human motion data, and training on them improves tracking accuracy and avoids unneeded compensating motion. We also apply random forces to the torso and hands, which makes the tracker more robust and lets the hands exert the forces that tasks require, for example when opening a door or holding an object. Given the same commands, the resulting tracker executes them more accurately than baseline trackers, and more tasks succeed with it (Section IV-B).

3) Planner: A command states what each body part should do through the following fields, which are executed in the order listed. Body: the pelvis height between 0.30 m and 0.79 m, and the torso pitch and yaw relative to a default posture for that height. Hands: for each hand, a sequence of waypoints for the palm frame in O, each with a position, an orientation, and a duration; while the body changes height or leans, a hand can instead stay fixed in space or move with the pelvis or the torso. Fingers: close or open, on dexterous hands. Walking: to a target pose in O or along given footsteps. Gaze: the yaw and pitch of the head camera, set last so that the next observation is taken from the requested view. Because one command sets all body parts, the agent can compose postures such as a crouch with a forward lean and a two-hand reach (Fig. 3). The system prompt names the robot model and the fields it supports, defines the palm frame, and gives the hand geometry that the agent needs to place the palm.

The planner solves inverse kinematics for the commanded pelvis, torso, and hand targets under joint, velocity, and selfcollision limits. It adds footstep motion for walking and streams the result to the tracker as one continuous reference.

The planner covers walking on flat ground, crouching, leaning, reaching, and grasping. Other motions can be added by producing their references for the same tracker, for example with a motion generation model.

## C. Active Perception

The perception interface lets the agent measure the positions of the objects and surfaces it will act on before it acts. VLMs without dedicated spatial training are poor at estimating metric positions from an image [39], [40], whereas VLMs trained on pointing data localize image points accurately [41], [42]. The interface splits the work accordingly: the agent chooses where to look and which points to measure, and depth supplies their positions.

![](images/601ee6b489e729fc56202597504c24b6ea2e4b2aab1246c28f5ab659fbe7966f.jpg)  
Fig. 4. Active perception. (a) The D455 on a 2-DoF mount on the G1’s head, on the real robot and in simulation, seen from the side and from above. The mount turns the view from level to 60<sup>◦</sup> down and up to 45<sup>◦</sup> either way. (b) A perception query of the agent in SIMPLE: it selects six points on the box and the table in the head-camera image and receives their positions in O.

To let the agent look for what it needs, we add a 2-DoF mount on top of the G1’s fixed head and place an Intel RealSense D455 on it (Fig. 4a), and reproduce its geometry in simulation. Every command sets the gaze of the camera, with yaw within ±45<sup>◦</sup> and pitch from level to 60<sup>◦</sup> downward. Since the agent observes nothing outside the camera’s view, it has to look down at the table in front of it, turn toward a destination at its side, or look ahead before walking.

On the real robot and in simulation, the agent receives a 640 × 400 RGB image with a field of view of 90<sup>◦</sup> × 64<sup>◦</sup> (horizontal by vertical), together with an aligned depth map. On the real robot, the D455 captures RGB at 1280 × 800 and depth at 640×360; the RealSense SDK aligns the depth to the RGB image, and we downsample both to 640×400. Raw D455 depth is noisy and has holes, so we refine each frame with LingBot-Depth [43], a depth completion model. We calibrate the camera on its mount with a ChArUco board held in the robot’s hand, and for each commanded gaze we solve for the servo angles that align the camera’s optical axis with the one in simulation. We configure the cameras in our simulation and in both benchmarks to this setup. In the benchmarks, this camera replaces the default ones, which are fixed to the head and have wider fields of view, about $1 1 0 ^ { \circ } \times 7 8 ^ { \circ }$ in SIMPLE and $1 0 6 ^ { \circ } \times 8 9 ^ { \circ }$ in HumanoidArena.

A perception query turns points that the agent selects in the latest image into positions in O (Fig. 4b). Each point is given as integer coordinates normalized to [0, 1000], the convention that Qwen3-VL uses for grounding [44]. The interface reads the depth at that pixel and back-projects it into O with the camera pose at the time of capture, correcting for lens distortion on the real robot. It reports points without depth as invalid and flags points whose 5 × 5 neighborhood has a large depth spread, which usually indicates an object edge. The returned image marks each point, so the agent can check whether a point landed on the intended surface and point again if it did not.

## D. Collecting Teacher Trajectories

The interfaces define what the agent can do, but a pretrained VLM knows little about how a humanoid should use them. We teach the agent to use them through trajectories of teacher policies, code policies that solve tasks in simulation through the same interfaces as the agent.

1) Tasks: Five base tasks provide the training data shared by all tasks (Fig. 2, bottom): picking and placing objects with one hand on low and high tables, carrying a box with both hands between pieces of furniture, walking to a goal around obstacles, opening or closing drawers and cabinet doors, and pushing or pulling open a room door with a lever handle. Within each base task, we randomize the objects and their placement, the destinations, the furniture layout, the robot’s starting pose, and the appearance of the scene. Steps such as approaching a table, crouching, and measuring a grasp point recur across the base tasks, and we expect an agent that learns these steps to reuse them in new tasks that combine them differently.

Six tasks of the SIMPLE benchmark serve as new tasks that the agent learns from a few trajectories. For each task, our teachers solve the scene layouts of 50 of the demonstrations that SIMPLE releases, and we evaluate on SIMPLE’s released evaluation episodes.

2) Teacher Policies: The teachers measure where things are before they act. When a target is not in view, for example, a teacher first looks where the instruction points. The teacher chooses its posture and where to stand from a precomputed map of what the hands reach in each posture. The map depends only on the robot’s body, and the teacher applies it to positions it has measured, so each choice is still a function of the agent’s observations. The agent never sees the map and has to learn these choices from the teachers’ actions.

To enforce this behavior, we require that every action of a teacher can be derived from its own observations and reasoning, which it writes in its response. The teacher receives only what the instruction states and computes every position it acts on from its measurements and the robot’s body state.

We wrote the teachers with Claude Opus 5.5. Each teacher episode is recorded as a multi-turn trajectory with the same system prompt and observations that the agent receives, with the wording of each response sampled from equivalent phrasings, and we keep only successful episodes.

## E. Training

We fine-tune Qwen3.8-27B on the teacher trajectories. Each trajectory forms one multi-turn sample. Each response holds one call and may hold the teacher’s reasoning. We update all weights of the language model, keep the vision encoder frozen, and apply the loss only to the teacher’s responses,

$$
\mathcal {L} (\theta) = - \sum_ {t} \log \pi_ {\theta} (a _ {t} \mid \ell , h _ {t}),\tag{2}
$$

summed over the turns of each trajectory.

The base-task model is trained on the five base tasks, with 500 trajectories each. To learn a new task, we train on its few trajectories either alone or mixed into the base task data; both start from the pretrained VLM. The mixture lets the new task build on the skills of the base tasks.

We train every model in two versions, with and without the teacher’s reasoning in the responses, to study how reasoning affects an agent that controls a humanoid through our harness. The version without reasoning also acts without it at test time.

## IV. EXPERIMENTS

We first test whether the execution interface carries out commands accurately enough for the tasks. We then evaluate the agent: whether training on the base tasks lets it solve benchmark tasks without task-specific trajectories, how quickly it learns a new task from a few trajectories, how much it relies on perception, and how reasoning in its responses affects it. Finally, we run the agent on a real G1.

## A. Experimental Setup

We test the agent mainly on two humanoid benchmarks, SIMPLE [4] and HumanoidArena [11], which play different roles. On SIMPLE, we measure success both without taskspecific trajectories and after training on a few trajectories of its tasks. HumanoidArena is held out: no model is trained on its tasks. We keep the scenes, objects, physics, success checks, and evaluation episodes of both benchmarks, and change only what our harness requires.

Benchmarks. For SIMPLE, we wrote teachers for six core tasks and evaluate each on its 30 released evaluation episodes. From HumanoidArena, we use four tasks: navigation, boxing, opening a door, and moving a box onto a shelf, each with 20 official episodes. The remaining tasks require motions that our planner does not yet support, such as sitting, kicking a ball, or stepping over obstacles. Figure 5 shows episodes from both benchmarks.

Adapting the benchmarks. Our planner and tracker drive the G1 in both benchmarks, and we make two further changes. First, our head camera on its 2-DoF mount replaces the fixed head camera of each benchmark, so the agent observes the scene as in training and on the real robot. Second, we rewrite the official instructions in the wording of our training data. Each instruction states the facts that hold in every episode of the task. For example, SIMPLE’s instruction for carrying an object between tables calls them “table1” and “table2”, while ours says that the basket is on the table behind the robot; HumanoidArena’s “Move the box from the table onto the shelf” does not say where the shelf is, while ours places it on the robot’s left. No instruction gives positions, and each is the same for every model and episode.

## B. Execution

We evaluate the execution interface with the teacher policies in place of the agent, so that the results reflect execution and not the agent’s decisions. We measure two things (Table I). The first is tracking error. Each tracker follows the same references, which the planner produced from the commands of a finetuned agent in its SIMPLE and HumanoidArena evaluation episodes and on which no tracker was trained. We measure the distance of each hand from its reference position relative to the pelvis, over the frames in which the arm moves, and the error of each walk relative to the commanded distance. The second is task success: the teachers solve 240 episodes, half of the base tasks and half of the SIMPLE tasks in our simulation, first with the tracker replaced and then with some commands removed. We compare our tracker, HEFT further trained on harness data, the references that the planner produced in teacher episodes of the base tasks, with HEFT before this training, with SONIC [19], which follows the same whole-body references, and with AMO [20]. AMO takes pelvis height, torso orientation, and walking velocity for its lower body and joint targets for its arms, which we derive from the same references.

Our tracker executes the agent’s commands most accurately. Its hands stay within 0.7 cm of the reference, and its walks end within 5% of the commanded distance. Training on harness data mainly reduces the walk error, from 11% to 5% on SIMPLE and from 8% to 3% on HumanoidArena. SONIC and AMO track the hands 2.0 to 3.1 cm from the reference, and their walk error depends mostly on the direction. SONIC follows forward walks closely, covering 90% of the commanded distance, and both turn in place within 4 to 13% of the commanded angle. Side and back steps are harder: SONIC covers 6% of a side step and 39% of a back step, and AMO about half of either. SONIC also keeps its torso more upright than commanded, leaning 11<sup>◦</sup> when the reference leans 34<sup>◦</sup>.

These differences decide task success. With our tracker, the teachers succeed in 97.9% of the episodes, against 94.2% with HEFT before training on harness data, 27.9% with SONIC, and 12.9% with AMO. The teachers line the robot up with a table or a handle through short side and back steps, and lean over tables to reach. When such a step falls short, the robot stops far from where the teacher planned. The teacher then adjusts its position again, but these corrections are again side and back steps, so they fail in the same way. Grasps, pushes, and pulls then fail because their targets are out of reach.

The tasks also rely on the torso and palm commands. Without torso pitch and yaw, teacher success falls to 67.1%: picking from a low table and carrying a box fail in most episodes. Without palm orientation, it falls to 18.8%, since grasps and door handles need the palm turned toward them. The fine-tuned agent uses these commands as the teachers do:

## C. Transfer from the Base Tasks

Training on the base tasks alone raises success from 3.3% to 51.1% on SIMPLE and from 16.3% to 42.5% on HumanoidArena (Table II), although neither benchmark’s tasks appear in its training data. The much larger Qwen3.8-Max succeeds in only 12.2% of SIMPLE’s episodes without thinking and 7.8% with it (Table III).

The transfer to HumanoidArena comes mainly from the base tasks. The model trained only on ten SIMPLE trajectories per task succeeds in 95.0% of SIMPLE’s episodes but in only 18.8% of HumanoidArena’s, close to the pretrained model. Adding the same SIMPLE trajectories to the base tasks raises SIMPLE success from 51.1% to 98.9% but lowers HumanoidArena success from 42.5% to 28.8%. Every SIMPLE trajectory picks up an object from a table in front of the robot, and the agent leans toward this pattern: on HumanoidArena, it succeeds more often at carrying a box from a table to a shelf, but less often at the tasks that differ from it, such as navigation and opening a door.

## D. Learning New Tasks from a Few Trajectories

A few trajectories per task suffice for the agent to learn a SIMPLE task (Table II). Five trajectories per task alone raise success from 3.3% to 90.0%. Added to the base tasks, five trajectories per task reach 95.0%, and ten reach 98.9%.

Training on the base tasks as well raises success at every number of trajectories. Among the models trained on SIMPLE trajectories, the gain is largest at N=5, 95.0% against 90.0%, and narrows as both approach saturation.

## E. Reliance on Perception

To measure how much the agent relies on perception, we train models without it: we remove every perception call, together with its returned points and image, from the training data and from the agent’s interface. The agent still sees each head-camera image, sets its gaze, and receives its body state, but it can no longer measure 3D positions.

Without perception, success drops on both benchmarks, and the drop grows with how much a task relies on precise positions (Table II, bottom). For the model trained on the base tasks only, SIMPLE success falls from 51.1% to 2.8%. Every SIMPLE task requires a grasp, and a palm placed off target often knocks the object over. HumanoidArena success falls less, from 42.5% to 27.5%, since its tasks are mostly walking and only a few steps need precise positions. With ten SIMPLE trajectories per task added, SIMPLE success still reaches 80.6%, against 98.9% with perception. Most SIMPLE tasks pick up the same cracker box, so the agent can learn from these trajectories to judge where the box is from how it looks in the image.

## F. Effect of Reasoning

The effect of reasoning depends on the model and the benchmark (Table III). The pretrained Qwen3.8-27B succeeds more often with reasoning on both benchmarks. After finetuning, the models without reasoning succeed more often on SIMPLE, while on HumanoidArena neither variant is consistently ahead.

On SIMPLE, three kinds of errors account for most failures with reasoning. The main one starts from a wrong reading: the agent selects a point off the object, such as on the table behind it, and its reasoning computes the next targets from this reading without questioning it, for example concluding that a box is several times deeper than it is. The teachers almost never make errors in perception or reasoning, so the demonstrations never show how to notice and correct such an error. The other two are an arithmetic error in the reasoning, made even when the readings are correct, and reasoning that drifts into the steps of another task, which the actions then follow. Without reasoning, the agent keeps its targets close to those of the demonstrations, which suits SIMPLE’s similar scenes. Reasoning does help when a grasp needs an offset from the measured point, which it often computes correctly. Demonstrations that include errors and their corrections, such as a wrong reading that the reasoning detects and measures again, could teach the agent to check its own reasoning and let reasoning help in scenes that vary more.

## G. Transfer to a Real G1

We run the model trained on the base tasks, without reasoning, on a real G1. Its training data contain no real images, and the harness is the same as in simulation except for the camera calibration. The robot has rubber hands without finger control. With these hands the robot can hold an object only between its palms, so we test two tasks: carrying a box with both hands from one table to another, and walking around obstacles to a goal. The model runs on a remote server and exchanges observations and commands with the robot over Wi-Fi. An operator judges each episode by the conditions in its instruction: the box stands upright on the other table after both hands let go, or the robot stops about 0.5 m from the goal without touching it or an obstacle.

Besides real images and depth, the scenes differ from those in training: the white folding table is smaller and the office desk is wider than any table in training, and the navigation goal is a stool, while every goal in training is a colored mat on the floor. For carrying, the robot moves a box about 20 cm on each side in two mirrored layouts with ten episodes each: from the folding table to the desk on its right, and from the desk to the folding table on its left. Between episodes, we vary the robot’s starting position, the box’s position, and the orientation of the tables. For navigation, cardboard boxes stand between the robot and the stool, and between episodes we vary the robot’s starting position and the placement of the boxes.

The agent succeeds in 13 of 20 carrying episodes (8 of 10 toward the desk and 5 of 10 toward the folding table) and in 6 of 10 navigation episodes (Fig. 5).

Table IV splits each successful episode into perception, decision, and execution. Most of the time goes to moving the robot. Decision takes about a fifth: each request is short without reasoning, but an episode makes many of them. Image capture takes as long, mostly waiting for the head and body to settle, so faster capture and planning would shorten episodes more than a faster model would.

## V. CONCLUSION

We built HITCH, a harness through which a VLM controls a humanoid. Its execution interface turns body-part commands into references through a planner, and a tracker trained further on these references executes them accurately enough that teacher policies succeed in 97.9% of episodes, against 27.9% and 12.9% with baseline trackers. Its perception interface returns the 3D position of any point the model selects; without it, the model trained on the base tasks solves only 2.8% of SIMPLE episodes. Fine-tuned through this harness on teacher trajectories of five base tasks, Qwen3.8-27B learns skills that carry over: it succeeds in 51.1% of SIMPLE and 42.5% of HumanoidArena episodes without task-specific trajectories, reaches 95.0% on SIMPLE with five per task, and operates a real G1 without real training data.

Several limitations remain. The planner does not support motions such as sitting or stepping over obstacles; a motion generation model could produce their references for the same tracker. Adding SIMPLE trajectories lowers HumanoidArena success, so data from one benchmark can narrow what the base tasks teach. After fine-tuning, reasoning lowers SIMPLE success, mostly because the agent builds on wrong readings; demonstrations with such errors and their corrections could teach it to check its own reasoning. Each command runs to completion before the agent observes again, so the agent cannot react during a motion. On the real G1, hands without finger control limited us to two tasks.

## APPENDIX B

## BASE TASKS

Each episode of a base task draws a new scene. In every task, we also randomize the head-camera image (exposure, gamma, white balance, contrast, saturation, blur, vignetting, and sensor noise) and the look of the room (lighting, wall color, floor, and furniture textures); objects keep their colors, since the instructions refer to them by color.

Pick and place. The robot picks up one of one to three objects, cubes, balls, boxes, or cylinders of 50 to 300 g, from a table, round table, or counter whose top lies between 0.40 m and 0.90 m, and puts it into a bin or bucket or onto a mat, on the same top or on another piece of furniture. In a quarter of the episodes, it only picks up the object and holds it. It uses one hand. In 40% of the episodes, the robot starts at the table; otherwise, the table stands 0.85 to 1.35 m ahead and the robot faces up to $6 0 ^ { \circ }$ away from it.

Carrying. The robot carries a box with a footprint of 0.18 to 0.28 m with both hands from one piece of furniture to another: tables, round tables, high stools and chairs, long counters, and shelving units, with tops between 0.40 m and 0.77 m. The two pieces stand side by side, at a corner, or facing each other, and the instruction gives the direction of the destination as one of eight coarse bearings. In a quarter of the episodes, the robot only picks up the box and holds it.

Navigation. The robot walks onto a mat 2.5 to 6.5 m away, named by its color and shape and sometimes by a flag, sign, cone, pillar, or ball beside it, among up to three other mats. Up to three structures, such as partitions, slaloms, and clusters of blocks, stand across the way, with more obstacles around them, and the robot starts facing up to $7 5 ^ { \circ }$ away from the goal.

Drawers and cabinet doors. The robot opens or closes one drawer or door of a cabinet with one to three columns of drawers, doors, or both, standing on a plinth or on legs. The instruction names the front by its row and column. Doors are hinged on the left, the right, or the bottom, the handle of the target lies between 0.70 m and 1.05 m, and a front to be closed starts partly open.

Room doors. The robot opens a door by pressing its lever handle down and pushing it, or closes a door that stands 20 to 35<sup>◦</sup> open. Doors are 0.80 to 0.90 m wide and hinged on either side, the lever lies between 0.95 m and 1.05 m, and the robot starts 1.4 to 2.0 m away.

## APPENDIX C

## TRAINING

All models start from the pretrained Qwen3.8-27B. We update all weights of the language model and keep the vision encoder and its projector frozen. Every model uses AdamW with $\beta = ( 0 . 9 , 0 . 9 5 )$ , a weight decay of 0.01, and gradient clipping at 1.0; a learning rate of $5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 6 }$ with 3% warm-up and cosine decay to zero; a global batch of 16 trajectories; bf16; and a maximum length of 49,152 tokens. Depending on the task, a trajectory has a median of 14 to 33 turns and about 20k to 40k tokens, including the images.

The number of steps depends on the training data. A model trained on the base tasks only runs for 450 steps. A model trained on SIMPLE trajectories only runs for 100 steps, and we keep its checkpoints at 60, 80, and 100 steps. A model trained on the base tasks and SIMPLE trajectories runs for 750 steps, draws each trajectory from SIMPLE with probability 0.1 (0.15 for N=30), and we keep its checkpoints at 450, 600, and 750 steps. For each model with several checkpoints, we report the checkpoint with the highest success on SIMPLE.

## APPENDIX D

## EXECUTION EXPERIMENTS

Tracking error. The references are those the planner streamed to the tracker in the evaluation episodes of a finetuned agent: 180 SIMPLE episodes and 80 HumanoidArena episodes. Each tracker follows them in the same simulator, on a G1 with rubber hands, without replanning. A step is the motion of one command. We measure the robot and the reference from their own poses at the start of each step and compare the displacement of the pelvis over the step, counting walks of at least 0.2 m; the walk error is the distance between the executed and the planned displacement, divided by the planned distance. The hand error is the distance between the wrist and its reference position in the pelvis frame. It is averaged over the frames in which the reference hand is at least 5 cm from its position in the default stance, measured in the reference torso frame; in the other frames the arm rests at its default pose.

Trackers. HEFT is the tracker before training on planner references, with the same architecture and inputs as ours. SONIC is the released model, connected through the adapter of the sim2real tracking leaderboard (https://github. com/EGalahad/sim2real), and follows the same whole-body references. AMO commands its lower body through pelvis height, torso orientation, planar velocity, and heading, and its arms through joint targets. We derive these commands from the reference at every control step: the height, torso orientation, and heading from the reference pose, the velocity from the reference pelvis motion, and the arm and wrist joints from the reference joints. We run AMO at its 2 ms training step and let it step for any commanded velocity or heading, since its release steps only at a forward speed of at least 0.1 m/s and cannot turn in place.

Teacher success. The teachers solve 20 episodes of each of twelve tasks: the five base tasks, with picking and placing split into a high-table and a low-table task, and the six SIMPLE tasks on the layouts of SIMPLE’s demonstrations. Without torso pitch and yaw, every command keeps the torso at the default posture for its pelvis height. Without palm orientation, every hand waypoint keeps only its position, and the planner chooses the orientation.

## APPENDIX F

## TIME LIMITS AND EPISODE DURATIONS

Table VII gives the official time limit of each benchmark task and how long our agent takes in its successful episodes: on SIMPLE, the model trained on the base tasks and ten SIMPLE trajectories per task; on HumanoidArena, the model trained on the base tasks only; both without reasoning. Times are simulated seconds from the agent’s first image to the first time the benchmark’s check passes, and turns are the calls made up to that point. The official limits match the speed of the policies these benchmarks evaluate, which act at high frequency. Our agent moves through a sequence of commands and observes the scene between them, so it takes longer, and we allow 45 s on SIMPLE and 60 s on HumanoidArena. A successful episode uses on average 4.8 perception calls and 10.3 commands on SIMPLE, and 6.8 and 10.6 on HumanoidArena. BendPick takes longer than XMoveBendPick, although the robot starts closer to its table: SIMPLE starts it right at the low table, with the box at the table’s near edge and the edge below the camera’s view. The agent first steps back to see the edge and set its stance from it, and then steps forward to grasp, while in XMoveBendPick it sees the table from the start and walks to it directly.
