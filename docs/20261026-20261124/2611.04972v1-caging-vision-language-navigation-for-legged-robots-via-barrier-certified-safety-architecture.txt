Caging Vision-Language Navigation for Legged Robots
via Barrier-Certified Safety Architecture
Abhijeet Mangesh Kulkarni1, Saimouli Katragadda1, Yuze Du1, Zijun Sha2, and Guoquan Huang1
Abstract— By harnessing the inherent commonsense capabil-
ities of pretrained Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-
Language Models (VLMs), current Vision-Language Action
(VLA) models for legged robots excel at interpreting language-
based navigation commands but lack the physical grounding
and explicit safety assurance required for real-world operation.
Therefore, this paper aims to cage Legged-Vision-Language
Navigation (VLN) through a safety architecture that provides
physical grounding and ensures navigation safety. The key
idea is not to abandon the powerful capabilities of end-to-
end VLA planning, but rather to augment and cage them.
In particular, we integrate a high-level VLA planner with
a low-level physical-grounding layer that leverages efficient
state estimation, in-situ 3D spatial perception, and an Signed
Distance Field (SDF) representation to generate executable
trajectories. We provide a navigation planner with provable
collision-avoidance guarantees under the stated assumptions
by combining a low-dimensional B´ezier-parameterized MPPI
planner with Discrete Exponential Barrier Function (dEBF)
constraints. The proposed Legged-VLN system is modular,
making it compatible with various VLM/VLA models and
agnostic to the robot platform. The proposed system is vali-
dated with extensive real-world experiments on humanoid and
quadrupedal robots, demonstrating versatile, efficient, and safe
vision-language driven navigation capabilities.
I. INTRODUCTION
Legged robots, including humanoids and quadrupeds, are
being engineered to work hand-in-hand with humans and
perform practical, real-world tasks with precision and pur-
pose (e.g. [1]). A fundamental prerequisite for accomplishing
such tasks is the ability to navigate safely to locations
where subsequent actions can be performed. Traditionally,
navigation missions are specified as sequences of goal poses
through a navigation interface, which typically requires either
a trained operator or an expert-authored behavior tree [2].
Recent advances in VLMs/VLAs [3] have enabled VLN, al-
lowing users to specify navigation missions through intuitive
natural-language instructions rather than manually defined
waypoints. However, to deploy these robots alongside hu-
mans, it is imperative that VLN systems are demonstrably
safe—generating motion that are both semantically correct
and collision free. This work directly addresses this critical
challenge of ensuring physical safety in Legged-VLN.

The core issue is that while pretrained models offer pow-
erful visual-semantic reasoning, their navigation outputs [4]–
[6] typically ignore the robot’s kinematics, local obstacle
configurations, and formal safety constraints. Consequently,
an end-to-end VLA planner might propose an instruction-
following navigation commands that are physically unexe-
cutable or prone to collisions. Therefore, rather than exe-
cuting these outputs directly, they must be treated as high-
level navigation proposals that are physically grounded and
verified. We refer to this verification process as caging: the
interposition of a safety-assured physical-grounding layer
between the VLM/VLA planner and the robot’s locomotion
controller, preserving the reasoning capabilities of pretrained
models while providing provable collision-avoidance guaran-
tees under the stated assumptions.
To this end, we propose a dual-layer caging architecture
for Legged-VLN that integrates high-level semantic rea-
soning with low-level, safety-assured motion planning. We
evaluate the complete framework in real-world indoor and
outdoor environments using a Unitree G1 humanoid and a
Ghost Robotics Vision 60 quadruped.
Specifically, the main contributions of this work are:
• A dual-layer caging architecture that decouples se-
mantic reasoning from physical execution by treating
pretrained VLM/VLA outputs as high-level proposals
and mapping them to locally feasible goal poses with
open-vocabulary terminal alignment.
• A safety-assured motion planner utilizing an ef-
ficient, low-dimensional B´ezier-parameterized Model
Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) formulation with SDF-
based dEBF constraints, providing provable collision-
avoidance guarantees under stated assumptions.
• Extensive real-world validation on both humanoid and
quadrupedal platforms, demonstrating cross-platform
applicability and reliable, safe execution of natural-
language navigation instructions.
II. RELATED WORK
Vision-language navigation for legged robots. VLN was
formalized by the Room-to-Room benchmark [7] and later
extended from discrete navigation graphs to continuous
environments through R2R-CE [8]. Recent VLAs directly
map visual and language observations to robot actions. Of
particular relevance, QUAR-VLA targets quadruped con-
trol [9], while NaVILA performs language-conditioned nav-
igation on legged robots [4]. These models provide strong
semantic action proposals, but their learned outputs do not

explicitly encode the robot footprint, the current local ob-

Recent safety-aware VLA methods address this gap either
by aligning the policy through constrained learning, as in
SafeVLA [10], or by correcting manipulation actions with
a plug-and-play Control Barrier Function (CBF) layer, as
in VLSA/AEGIS [11]. Our work instead targets legged
navigation: it leaves the semantic model unchanged and
cages its proposals using online geometric perception and
horizon-level motion certification. This separation allows the
front end and robot platform to change without transferring
responsibility for navigation safety to the learned model.
Semantic and geometric grounding. Methods such as 3D-
LLM [12] and Chat-Scene [13] enrich language models with
3D scene structure for spatial reasoning. Open-vocabulary
detection and segmentation models, including Grounding-
DINO [14] and SAM2 [15], additionally enable language-
specified objects to be localized without a fixed task vocab-
ulary. Splat-Nav [16] more tightly couples these domains by
using a language-embedded Gaussian-splat map to specify
goals, construct safe polytope corridors, and generate B´ezier
trajectories. Our architecture instead uses the learned front
end and open-vocabulary perception to generate semantic
goals and refine terminal alignment, while an incrementally
updated environmental SDF grounds every proposed motion
in locally observed free space. Safety is therefore evaluated
from geometric clearance rather than semantic confidence.
Safety in sampling-based predictive control. For legged
robots, sequential MPC over convex safe corridors enables
reactive bipedal planning in cluttered environments [17].
MPPI supports derivative-free trajectory optimization with
nonlinear and nonsmooth costs [18]; low-dimensional trajec-
tory parameterizations further improve sampling efficiency in
cluttered environments [19]. Discrete-time barrier conditions
provide a route to forward-invariant safety [20], [21], and re-
cent sampling-based controllers incorporate barrier functions
through shielded refinement [22], barrier-rate guidance with
learned SDFs [23], or discrete barrier states [24]. In contrast,
our physical-grounding layer combines B´ezier-parameterized
MPPI, value-only queries to an incrementally updated en-
vironmental SDF, soft dEBF-guided sampling, and hard
rollout certification with a safe backup. This construction
cages interchangeable VLM/VLA proposals and is deployed
unchanged at the planning level on both quadrupedal and
humanoid platforms.
III. SYSTEM OVERVIEW
We address the problem of language-driven, safety-critical
navigation for legged robots operating in cluttered, unknown
environments. That is, given (i) a natural-language instruc-
tion describing a high-level task and optionally a target
object, (ii) onboard sensing (e.g., vision, LiDAR, IMU, leg
kinematics), and (iii) platform actuation limits, the objective
is to synthesize a control sequence {uk}T −1
k=0 that steers the
robot from an initial pose x0 to a a goal region or an object-
relative pose while being caged to safely execute the com-
mands. Formally, the robot must: (i) ground the language into
a sequence of high-level, spatially referenced subgoals; (ii)
convert these into feasible, collision-free subgoals; and (iii)
ensure safety at all times. The solution should be modular
(compatible with different legged platforms and VLM/VLA
models), and real-time capable. Success is achieved when the
robot reaches the goal region or establishes a valid object-
relative pose (for open-vocabulary targets), without violating
safety constraints throughout the trajectory.

This planar body-twist model is suitable for motion planning

The local environment is encoded by a SDF ϕ : R2 →R.
For any point p ∈R2, ϕ(p) returns the minimum Euclidean
distance to the nearest obstacle surface; ϕ(p) > 0 indicates


free space, ϕ(p) = 0 lies on an obstacle boundary, and
ϕ(p) < 0 is inside an obstacle.
B. The Proposed Caging Framework
Our framework employs a hierarchical control architecture
that translates high-level semantic instructions into safety-
assured motion (see Fig. 1). The upper layer converts visual
and language inputs into spatial navigation goals. The lower
layer converts these goals into feasible control commands
while enforcing safety constraints. This separation allows
the semantic planner and the robot platform to be changed
independently, while preserving a common safety-assured
execution layer.
1) Semantic Grounding Layer: This layer converts vi-
sual observations and natural-language instructions into a
sequence of high-level navigation actions. A VLM processes
the user’s command together with the current scene and
predicts discrete actions, such as turning left or right in 15◦
increments up to 45◦, or moving forward in 25 cm increments
up to 75 cm. The Kinematic Goal Projector maps each action
to a planar goal pose xgoal in the local map and verifies
that the resulting waypoint is reachable and collision-free.
After the mission-level action sequence is completed, an
open-vocabulary object-centric grounding module may be
activated to define the robot’s final approach relative to a
specified object. The semantic grounding components are
detailed in Section IV.
2) Physical Grounding Layer: This layer converts the
goal poses xgoal provided by the semantic grounding
layer into executable control commands while enforcing
the prescribed safety constraints. For the humanoid plat-
form, which is equipped with high-quality LiDAR, we use
FastLIVO2 [25] to obtain robust LiDAR–inertial odometry.
For the quadruped platform, an invariant EKF [26] fuses
leg-kinematic and IMU measurements. The local environ-
ment is represented by an incrementally updated SDF [27],
which provides obstacle-clearance information directly to
the motion planner. To enable real-time reactive planning
with formal safety guarantees under the stated assumptions,
we develop an efficient low-dimensional MPPI planner aug-
mented with dEBF constraints as discussed in Section V.
Additionlly an optional JPS global planner [28] can be
included to reduce the susceptibility to local minima.
IV. HIGH-LEVEL VLA PLANNING
The Kinematic Goal Projector converts discrete high-level
action commands generated by the VLA model (e.g., turn
left, turn right, move forward) into, obstacle-free
goal poses in the map. To achieve this, the module integrates
odometry, and an SDF representation of the environment.
The SDF grid is queried for the signed distance ϕ(p) at
candidate position p, which is obstacle free if ϕ(p) ≥dclear,
with dclear = 0.3 m as the minimum clearance threshold. If
the primary position pd is occupied, we select a valid alterna-
tive within a local radius which is after sampling candidate
poses on the radius. Candidate poses are evaluated with a
cost function that prioritizes proximity to the desired pose,
alignment with the robot’s heading, and obstacle clearance:

The open vocabulary object-centric grounding module
is activated after the mission-level language navigation is
complete. Its purpose is to semantically ground the final stage
of the task: detecting and approaching a user-specified target
object. To achieve this, we integrate Grounding-DINO [14]
for open-vocabulary object detection and SAM2 [15] for
precise segmentation, producing both bounding boxes and
fine-grained masks. The segmentation mask or bounding box
centroid is then projected into 3D using the robot’s lidar,
odometry and camera intrinsics, yielding a target position
in the global frame. To reject spurious detections, the target
position pt must satisfy dmin ≤∥pt −pr∥≤dmax, with
dmin = 0.5 m and dmax = 3.0 m.
The navigation state transitions through SEARCHING,
APPROACHING, and CLOSE, depending on detection confi-
dence and the measured target distance. When the distance
falls below a threshold dstop (set to 0.50 m in our experi-
ments), the robot halts in front of the target. This ensures
the robot reaches semantically meaningful endpoints, rather
than merely completing geometric navigation. As will be
shown in our experiments, enabling this module allows the
robot to consistently approach user-specified objects, thereby
extending the system from pure language-conditioned motion
planning to semantically grounded, object-aware interaction.
V. PHYSICAL GROUNDING LAYER
The semantic layer specifies where to move but does not
account for robot geometry, obstacles, or closed-loop safety.
The Physical Grounding Layer in Fig. 1 therefore acts as
an execution cage, converting each semantic goal xgoal into
a dynamically feasible and safe motion. This separation
allows the VLM/VLA front end to remain independent of
the mechanisms responsible for physical safety.
The planner combines: (i) low-dimensional MPPI, with
horizon controls parameterized by B´ezier control points;
(ii) an SDF-based dEBF cost that biases sampling toward
collision-free trajectories; and (iii) a hard rollout filter with
a one-step-safe backup policy. The cost improves sampling
efficiency, while the filter and backup policy establish safety
under the stated modeling, mapping, and tracking assump-
tions. Unlike collision penalties, which may trade safety for
goal progress, the proposed method explicitly certifies each
executed command. It also avoids numerical differentiation
of an incrementally updated SDF, as required by many


gradient-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) formula-
tions [21] and also avoids storage and querying of a separate
gradient map. We instead evaluate it along sampled rollouts
using only SDF value queries, accommodating nonsmooth
costs, nonlinear constraints, and online map updates.

VI. EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION
We structure the evaluation around three questions. (Q1)
How does the proposed dEBF affect obstacle clearance and
object-approach behavior relative to standard MPPI? (Q2)
Does dEBF reduce observed collision incidence both without
a global path planner and when JPS-based global replan-
ning [28] is available? (Q3) Does the complete physical-
grounding cage remain effective across environment scales,
semantic front ends, and legged platforms? We first charac-
terize dEBF in simulation, then isolate its effect on a Unitree
G1 humanoid, and finally evaluate end-to-end navigation on
the G1 and a Ghost Robotics Vision 60 quadruped.
A. Experimental Setup and Metrics
Simulation. We use Gazebo to simulate a Vision 60, LiDAR,
and ground-truth odometry. The mapping and perception
pipeline mirrors the hardware implementation. The goal is
held fixed so that the experiments isolate the local planner
under different dEBF parameters and obstacle configurations.
Hardware. The G1 and Vision 60 carry an RGB camera,
IMU, and LiDAR for online state estimation, mapping,
and geometric grounding. Platform-specific locomotion con-
trollers track the commanded body-frame velocity, while an
NVIDIA A4500 executes the mapping and motion-planning
stack. The semantic front ends are executed locally or
accessed through their respective interfaces.
Planner parameters. For all experiments, the dEBF decay
parameter is set to γ = 0.95. At each control update, MPPI
uses 200 samples and 10 optimization iterations. Each rollout
is discretized with a 0.05 s step over a horizon of 50 time
steps, corresponding to a 2.5 s prediction horizon.
The G1 is the primary controlled-evaluation platform. We
conduct physical grounding layer ablations in a 4 m × 4 m
arena, an end-to-end study in a 6 m × 6 m static-clutter
environment, and a system-level caging study in a 12 m ×
12 m environment. The controlled studies compare MPPI
with and without dEBF across ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini,
and NaVILA. The moderate-scale study uses 25 instructions,
each repeated three times under both planner configurations,
yielding 75 trials per configuration. The large-scale study
evaluates 15 instructions once with and once without the
complete the physical-grounding cage for each of the four
semantic front ends, yielding 120 executions. The Vision 60
experiments assess cross-platform deployment in real indoor
and outdoor environments.
Metrics. Success Rate (SR) is the fraction of trials that
complete the instructed navigation task. Collision Rate (CR)
is the fraction in which the robot contacts an obstacle or vio-
lates the predefined collision boundary. End-to-End Success
Rate (E2E SR) requires both task completion and collision-
free execution. For object-centric navigation, Object SR
denotes successful localization and approach, and Navigation
Error (NE) is the final distance to the desired object-relative
terminal position, following standard VLN practice [4]. We
report only metrics annotated for each study and do not infer
unrecorded task success from collision outcomes.
B. Simulation Characterization of dEBF
Figure 3 illustrates how dEBF shapes local motion. In-
creasing the decay parameter γ requires a predicted trajectory
to retain a larger fraction of its current barrier value (10)
and therefore produces more conservative motion around
an obstacle (Fig. 3(a)). In a narrow passage, the standard
collision-penalty MPPI maintains a relatively high speed un-
til it approaches the obstacle boundary, whereas MPPI+dEBF
slows as predicted clearance decreases (Fig. 3(b)). The
object-approach example similarly shows a smooth reduction
in speed during the final approach (Fig. 3(c)).
These behaviors arise from the temporal coupling in the
discrete barrier condition. A conventional collision penalty
evaluates individual predicted states and may trade obstacle
proximity against goal progress. In contrast, dEBF constrains
the permitted decrease in clearance between consecutive
predicted states, regulating both intersection with the safe-
set boundary and the rate at which a rollout approaches it.
Making dEBF+MPPI highly suitable for safe and smooth
navigation through cluttered and narrow spaces.

C. Controlled dEBF Ablations on G1
We conduct two complementary studies in the 4 m × 4 m
G1 arena. In the first, JPS is disabled and a fixed obstacle
interferes with the nominal direction to a target object. Thus,
avoidance is determined by local MPPI, with or without
dEBF. In the second, JPS is enabled in both configurations
and a previously unobserved obstacle is suddenly introduced
after the robot begins moving. The obstacle is incorporated
into the online map and triggers global replanning. This
paired design evaluates whether dEBF adds short-horizon
protection during perception, map updating, and replanning
rather than comparing local and global planning. Initial pose,
obstacle and target placement, velocity limits, planner param-
eters, and timeout are held fixed within each comparison.
Tables I and II show a consistent collision reduction in
both settings. Without JPS, the aggregate CR decreases from
80% to 0%; with JPS shared by both configurations, it
decreases from 100% to 0%. No collisions were observed in
any of the 40 dEBF-enabled controlled trials. The aggregate
Object SR changes from 35% to 40% in the static study
and from 55% to 70% in the sudden-obstacle-introduction
study. The corresponding aggregate E2E SR increases from
15% to 40% and from 0% to 70%, respectively. JPS and
dEBF serve complementary roles. JPS supplies global route
guidance around mapped obstacles, reducing local MPPI’s
susceptibility to local minima; dEBF constrains short-horizon
rollouts during map-update and replanning transitions.
The consistent collision reduction shows that dEBF im-
proves physical execution across all four tested semantic
models. The proposed Legged-VLN architecture physically
grounds the model’s navigation proposals but does not mod-
ify its semantic reasoning. Task completion therefore remains
dependent on the underlying model; for example, the Claude
trials in the no-JPS setting are collision-free with dEBF yet
do not complete the object-approach task. Thus, these results
demonstrate improved collision avoidance without implying
improved semantic reasoning or target selection.
D. End-to-End Scaling and Cross-Platform Transfer
We next evaluate longer language-conditioned missions.
On G1, the 6 m × 6 m environment contains multiple static
obstacles and tasks ranging from one- or two-action in-
structions to longer sequences involving multiple landmarks
and turns. Each of 25 instructions is executed three times
under standard MPPI and MPPI+dEBF, yielding 75 trials
per configuration. On Vision 60, we use the same planning-
level interface in real indoor and outdoor environments.
State estimation, sensor coverage, footprint geometry, and
low-level locomotion remain platform specific; in particular
a platform-specific multi-point footprint is used for SDF
queries as in Fig 2.
As shown in Table III, dEBF reduces observed CR on both
platforms. On G1, CR decreases from 0.33 to 0.00 for simple
tasks and from 0.50 to 0.00 for complex tasks, while SR
remains 0.75. The unchanged SR indicates that the reduction
was not obtained by suppressing a larger fraction of task
executions. On Vision 60, dEBF reduces CR from 0.67 to
0.20 for simple tasks and from 0.91 to 0.08 for complex
tasks while increasing the recorded SR.
The remaining Vision 60 collisions are primarily associ-
ated with incomplete environmental representation, including
thin wires, table edges, narrow poles, and geometry in sensor
blind spots due to an older LiDAR VLP16. Since the barrier
is evaluated against the current SDF, absent geometry cannot
be certified. Overall, introduction of the proposed caging
architecture enhance the safety of the commands proposed
by VLM/VLA models.
E. System-Level Caging Across Semantic Front Ends
The final G1 safety study uses a 12 m × 12 m indoor
environment and 15 instructions ranging from single-stage
commands to multi-stage tasks with multiple landmarks and
spatial constraints. For each front end, the same instructions
are executed with the complete physical-grounding cage
disabled and enabled. Caging enables MPPI, JPS, and dEBF
jointly. In uncaged they are turned off to clearly demonstrate
the benefits of the proposed caging architecture. Table IV
summarizes 120 executions. All of the 60 trails without
the proposed caging architecture results in collisions for
these longer tasks, whereas no collision is observed with
the caging architecture. The same trend occurs within every
semantic frontend VLM/VLA model, showing that caging is
a necessity for all the models tested with longer tasks.

F. Object-Centric Grounding and Evaluation Scope
Collision-aware execution does not ensure that a mission
terminates at the intended semantic object. We therefore
evaluate an auxiliary terminal-grounding stage on Vision 60.
After mission-level navigation, Grounding-DINO and SAM2
detect and segment the language-specified target. Its esti-
mated position is projected into the global frame, and the
robot performs a final object-relative approach. Table V
reports a decrease in mean endpoint error from 1.50 m to
0.29 m with object-centric grounding. This layer is beneficial
for missions that require approaching an object.
G. Discussion and Limitations
The controlled ablations in Sec. VI-C and end-to-end
evaluation in Sec. VI-D show that uncaged collision risk
increases with task length and complexity. This trend cul-
minates in the long-horizon G1 ablation (Sec. VI-E), where
all 60 uncaged trials collide, whereas caging reduces the
aggregate CR to 0% across four semantic models. JPS
provides global route guidance around mapped obstacles,
reducing the local planner’s susceptibility to local minima,
but does not certify local execution; with JPS enabled, adding
dEBF reduces CR from 100% to 0% (Table II). Similar
improvements on Vision 60 support portability across the
tested task scales, environments, and platforms.
These results remain subject to the assumptions of Theo-
rem 1. Certification uses the current SDF and cannot account
for unobserved geometry. The planar body-twist and multi-
point models also exclude state-estimation error, latency,
and tracking error. Object-centric grounding (Sec. VI-F)
improves terminal alignment, but the cage cannot correct
erroneous or unreachable semantic goals.
VII. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
We presented a modular caging architecture for legged
VLN that separates semantic reasoning from physically
grounded execution. High-level VLM/VLA outputs are
treated as navigation proposals and converted into geometric
goals, while a low-dimensional B´ezier-parameterized MPPI
planner uses the online SDF and dEBF constraints to gen-
erate executable motion. This separation permits different
semantic front ends and legged platforms to share the same
planning-level safety interface. We prove collision avoidance
for dEBF-constrained MPPI under the stated assumptions.


We extensively evaluated the proposed Legged-VLN archi-
tecture on a Unitree G1 humanoid and a Ghost Robotics
Vision 60 quadruped. The real-world experiments demon-
strate modularity across platforms and substantially reduced
observed collision incidence, while open-vocabulary object
grounding improves semantic terminal alignment under the
tested conditions. Future work will incorporate uncertainty
in state estimation and mapping, as well as moving obsta-
cles, into the safety analysis to narrow the gap between
the formal model and hardware execution. We will also
extend the capabilities of VLA models beyond navigation to
richer humanoid skills, including grasping, object handover,
and fine-grained target interaction. Finally, integrating multi-
modal memory will support longer-horizon reasoning toward
general-purpose robotic assistants.
