# ∇Mimic: Ultra-Eficient Humanoid Control with Diferentiable Simulation

Weyl Lu Yubei Chen

UC Davis<sup>∗</sup>

## Abstract

Robot policy learning is dominated by zeroth-order methods such as PPO, which treat the simulator as a black box and pay for it with enormous sample counts. First-order policy learning diferentiates through the dynamics, but on contact-rich whole-body control it has rarely worked: its gradients explode. A recent world-model method showed that derivative information can make humanoid motion tracking far more eficient, using surrogate gradients of a learned model. We show that exact gradients of the simulator itself do better, once two obstacles are removed. Exact gradients contain rare unbounded samples, because contact dynamics is not diferentiable everywhere and its derivative diverges near some events; and they are expensive, because the simulator’s adjoint has to replay the contact solve.

∇Mimic bounds these samples with median-relative per-sample gradient clipping at every step of the backward pass, and reuses each costly simulated window by multistep regression onto gradient-derived action targets, which bounds every action target independently of the gradient scale. NablaEngine, a diferentiable GPU simulation engine for the Unitree G1, removes the cost: it computes MuJoCo’s full contact dynamics with fused per-world kernels and the adjoint of a whole control step as a single megakernel, with analytic gradients that match float64 finite diferences, 11.5× faster than the available MuJoCo Warp adjoint.

On whole-body tracking of all 40 LAFAN1 clips with one policy on one GPU, ∇Mimic reaches the score reported by the state-of-the-art world-model method, 0.82, in 20 minutes instead of 1.2 h, and 0.874 in 1.8 h. SHAC, the standard first-order baseline, stops on exploding gradients within minutes. We release NablaEngine and ∇Mimic as open-source infrastructure for first-order humanoid policy learning.

## 1 Introduction

Motion tracking has become a central task in humanoid learning. A tracking policy imitates reference motions in physics, so any human motion that can be retargeted to a robot becomes a behavior it can execute. The idea goes back to DeepMimic and its successors in character animation [41, 58, 71], and has moved to real humanoids [24, 26, 30, 78, 87] Two developments made it a foundation rather than a single skill. Large motion datasets of better quality, from AMASS and LAFAN1 to BONES-SEED, together with robot retargets of them, now cover hours of diverse motion [7, 23, 43, 46]. And a single general tracker trained on such data can serve as the low-level policy of larger systems: its encoder–decoder structure provides a motion interface for teleoperation, generation and downstream tasks [9, 40, 42].

Almost all of these trackers are trained with zeroth-order policy gradient methods, chiefly PPO [63], which need only rewards from a black-box simulator and pay for it with enormous sample counts. Massively parallel GPU simulation [20, 21, 47] made the samples cheap, yet a whole-body tracking policy still takes hours to days of GPU time, and this cost bounds how far the approach can scale.

First-order policy learning diferentiates the return through the dynamics, so every simulated step contributes an update direction instead of a scalar reward [11, 14, 70, 81]. On contact-rich whole-body control this promise has rarely held: gradients through stif contact dynamics explode [49, 70]. SHAC [81], the standard first-order method, is no exception. On whole-body tracking with the Unitree G1, every SHAC run hits exploding gradients and stops within minutes (Section 4.1).

Policy learning through the derivatives of a learned dynamics model has a long history, from stochastic value gradients and model-augmented actor-critic to Dreamer and to tracking controllers for physics-based characters [10, 15, 22, 27, 37, 82]. SuperMimic [39] recently brought this approach to humanoid whole-body tracking at scale: it diferentiates through a learned world model instead of a simulator and trains a tracking policy far faster than PPO. We read SuperMimic’s result diferently: not as evidence for world models, but as evidence for derivatives. The gain comes from using the dynamics’ derivatives at all; the world model is the price SuperMimic pays to make those derivatives bounded and cheap. This raises the question of what that price buys, and whether it has to be paid. A world model supplies surrogate gradients: smooth, bounded and cheap, but derivatives of a diferent system. A simulator supplies exact gradients: derivatives of the simulated system itself, but with rare unbounded samples, because contact dynamics is not diferentiable everywhere and its derivative diverges near some events, and expensive, because the simulator’s adjoint has to replay the contact solve (Section 4).

This paper removes both obstacles. ∇Mimic handles the samples: it bounds the rare extreme gradients per sample and turns the rest into action targets whose step size is fixed in physical units, so that no single sample can take over an update (Section 5). NablaEngine handles the cost: a diferentiable GPU engine specialized to the G1, with the adjoint of a whole control step as a single megakernel, whose exact gradient costs less than an extra forward step (Section 6).

On all 40 LAFAN1 clips, with one policy and one GPU, ∇Mimic reaches SuperMimic’s score of 0.82 in 20 minutes, where SuperMimic reports 1.2 h, and 0.874 in 1.8 h, with no seed falling on any clip from 1.5 h on (Fig. 1). PPO on the same engine stays below 0.82 for 6 h. The engine behind this simulates 18.8 h of robot motion per second of wall clock (Fig. 1b). Motion tracking suits exact gradients, since its reward is dense and points toward the reference almost everywhere; for sparse rewards, a longer gradient window trades reliance on the critic against accumulated extreme gradients (Section 4.2).

## Contributions.

1. An analysis of exact and surrogate gradients for policy learning. Unbounded exact derivatives near nondiferentiable contact events, accumulating along long windows, are consistent with the failure of prior first-order methods on whole-body control; the low cost of a world model, with the success of surrogate gradients.

2. ∇Mimic, a first-order policy learning method that is stable on exact contact gradients at the scale of thousands of parallel environments, with each component ablated.

3. NablaEngine, an open-source diferentiable simulation engine for the Unitree G1 whose analytic gradients match float64 finite diferences.

4. Humanoid motion tracking at the level of the world-model state of the art without a world model, on one GPU: the LAFAN1 score of 0.82 in 20 minutes instead of 1.2 h.

∇Mimic and NablaEngine are open source; the links are on the first page.

## 2 Related work

Zeroth-order motion tracking. Physics-based motion imitation with RL was established by DeepMimic [58], and universal trackers such as PHC [41] imitate thousands of clips from datasets such as AMASS [46] with one policy. On real humanoids, H2O and OmniH2O [24, 25], ASAP [26], GMT [9] and others [78, 83] track retargeted human motion on the Unitree G1. BeyondMimic [40] tracks agile LAFAN1 motions [23, 43] with one compact PPO formulation and shared hyperparameters, and SONIC [42] scales PPO tracking to 700 h of motion capture and 21k GPU-hours (128 GPUs for 7 days). These trackers use zeroth-order methods, chiefly PPO [63]. We use BeyondMimic’s PPO configuration as our zeroth-order baseline.

First-order policy learning in diferentiable simulators. Full-horizon BPTT through diferentiable simulators [11, 14, 29] sufers from chaotic, exploding gradients [49], and gradients through stif contact can be biased or high-variance [55, 69, 70]. PODS [50] regresses the policy onto gradient-improved actions. SHAC [81] introduced short windows [77] with a TD(�) critic; successors adapt the horizon (AHAC; 17), add maximum entropy (SAPO; 79), combine analytic gradients with PPO (GI-PPO, RPO; 67, 91) or decouple rendering from the gradient (D.Va; 84). On legged robots, policies transferred to hardware were trained with smoothed foot contact [6, 64], a simplified surrogate for the backward pass [68], or an exact forward pass with a smoothed contact sensitivity in the backward pass [31]. DifMimic [61] tracks single clips in Brax without a critic. BCG [3] combines SHAC and an ADD imitation reward [90] on the smoothed-contact simulator of Schwarke et al. with contact-local randomized bundling, and trains G1 trackers for four LAFAN1 clips with 64 environments. These works use 32–1024 environments; we use 4096 BPTT windows per update on full contact dynamics.

Gradients through learned models. SVG, MAAC and Dreamer optimize policies through learned dynamics [10, 22, 27]. PWM [18] and Ma et al. [44] report that learned models can give smoother optimization landscapes than the true dynamics, and Amigo et al. [4] roll out in the simulator and backpropagate through a learned model. For physics-based characters, SuperTrack [15], ControlVAE [82] and Lei et al. [37] train tracking controllers through learned dynamics. SuperMimic [39] trains one world model online with the policy and reports the state of the art on LAFAN1 and BONES-SEED [7]. Their gradients are those of a learned surrogate; ours are of the simulator itself.

Diferentiable and GPU simulators. GPU simulation [47, 54, 62, 85, 86] made samples cheap for zeroth-order methods. Among diferentiable engines, DifTaichi and Warp generate adjoint kernels [29, 45], and dflex [80] and Rewarped [79] use penalty contacts. Brax and MJX [14, 20] use JAX autodif; MJX is forward mode only on our model [52]. Nimble [76] diferentiates LCP contact analytically, and Dojo, Simple and Ostrich [28, 34, 36] apply the implicit function theorem at the contact solution. DifMJX [57] studies gradients of MuJoCo’s soft contacts [73, 74]. Analytic rigid-body derivatives [8] and articulated-body adjoints [60] avoid generic autodif. MuJoCo Warp [21] is not diferentiable in any release; an adjoint exists in an open pull request [13]. Newton [72] and Genesis [16] have diferentiable modes, which hold the contact normal fixed [53] or leave some collision operations without gradients, respectively. Kernel fusion for RL simulation [19, 65] and the run-to-run non-determinism of GPU simulators [88] have been studied before. NablaEngine gives exact, deterministic adjoints of MuJoCo’s dynamics for one robot at high throughput.

Clipping and regression onto action targets. Norm clipping of BPTT gradients goes back to Pascanu et al. [56]. Per-sample clipping is standard in private SGD [1], with median-relative [5] and quantile thresholds [48]; clipping helps under heavy-tailed noise and gradient-dependent smoothness [66, 89] but is biased in general [33]. We apply it to the action cotangents inside BPTT, as a heuristic outside these guarantees. Regressing a policy onto improved actions follows trajectory-optimization teachers [38, 51], MPO [2], AWR [59] and target-based surrogates [35, 75]. We keep PODS’s target regression and difer in two points: per-sample median-relative clipping inside BPTT, and a step size fixed in radians by the batch RMS of the clipped gradients (Section 5.2).

## 3 Background

Task. Whole-body motion tracking on the Unitree G1 (29 actuated joints), mjlab model [86]. Control runs at 50 Hz with 4 physics substeps of 5 ms. The policy observes a 10-frame history of noisy proprioception (joint positions and velocities, base angular velocity, gravity direction, previous action), the next 10 reference frames, and the orientation of the reference anchor body (torso) relative to the robot’s, which needs the IMU but no global position. The reward is BeyondMimic’s tracking reward in SuperMimic’s setting [39, 40]: exponential kernels on the position, orientation and velocities of 14 bodies and on the anchor pose, with action-rate and joint-limit penalties. An episode terminates when the root height error exceeds 0.25 m or the tilt error exceeds 0.8. Section A gives the full definitions.

## 4 Exact and surrogate gradients

A first-order method uses the derivatives of the dynamics, which a zeroth-order method ignores. It is therefore commonly expected to extract more information from each sample, and to be the better choice whenever these derivatives are available. The record says otherwise: methods that use the simulator’s own derivatives have failed on whole-body control, while a method that uses the derivatives of a learned imitation of the simulator succeeded. This section explains the paradox. The derivatives of contact-rich dynamics are unusable as they are, the systems that make them well behaved describe something other than the target dynamics, and computing them costs time. We discuss these trade-ofs and the challenges they pose; the rest of the paper addresses them.

Two sources of gradient. To discuss these trade-ofs we introduce two terms. We call a policy gradient exact when it contains the analytic derivatives of the system the policy is trained and evaluated in: here, when the dynamics $f$ in Eq. (1) is the simulator itself. We call it surrogate when $f$ is replaced by another, more tractable system, so that the gradient contains that system’s derivatives. The surrogate can be a learned dynamics model ${ \hat { f } } ,$ as in model-based policy optimization [10, 15, 22, 27, 39], or a simplified diferentiable simulator that softens contact, for example by penalty springs of limited stifness in place of rigid contact [14, 80, 81]. The two are the ends of an axis rather than a dichotomy: a simulator with softened contact lies between them, and even NablaEngine’s training gradient departs from the exact derivative where it freezes unbounded terms (Section 6). Below we compare the two ends: the exact gradients of NablaEngine and the surrogate gradients of a learned world model, the surrogate SuperMimic showed to work for humanoid tracking.

Neither exactness nor boundedness is what learning needs. What it needs is learning signal: the part of a gradient that improves the return at the scale of one policy update. Two trade-ofs decide how much of it each source delivers per unit of compute (Table 1).

Unbounded exact, bounded surrogate. An exact gradient has no bias, but contact-rich dynamics is not diferentiable everywhere. Where contacts appear or vanish, actuators saturate or the solver’s active set changes, the derivative jumps or is undefined. Near some of these events it is unbounded. Computed faithfully, it can be infinite. We measured this on the G1 (Section B):

• when the feet nearly touch, the derivative of the self-contact normal grows as the inverse of their distance; on training windows the gradient grew by up $_ { 0 } 4 . 9 \times 1 0 ^ { 2 1 }$ through a window, and 32 rows were not finite;

• for nearly parallel capsules (crossed feet), one control step amplified the gradient by up to $3 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 }$ , and repeated steps overflowed to non-finite values in 40 of 172 such states.

A surrogate gradient, in contrast, is bounded everywhere. For a world model that is a multilayer perceptron with weight matrices $W _ { 1 } , \dots , W _ { n }$ and an activation with Lipschitz constant � (SiLU, as in SuperMimic, has $c \approx 1 . 1 )$ , the Jacobian of one predicted step satisfies

$$
\left\| \frac {\partial \hat {f}}{\partial (s , a)} \right\| _ {2} \leq L = c ^ {n - 1} \prod_ {l = 1} ^ {n} \| W _ {l} \| _ {2}\tag{2}
$$

at every state, and the action gradient of an �-step window is bounded by a constant that depends only on �, � and the reward. The bound can be loose and grows with �, but it is uniform: no state makes it infinite. A simulator’s step Jacobian has no such bound. The surrogate gradient, however, describes a diferent system than the one being simulated, so it is biased.

Same bulk, diferent tail. The two gradients agree on the bulk of the batch and part ways only at the extremes. Each on its own rollouts, they have similar tails. With the policy of a SuperMimic reproduction, the same objective and 32-step windows, the 99.9th percentile of the per-sample action-gradient norm is 25–37 times the median on both routes, and the maximum a few hundred to about 1,350 times, with no consistent ordering between them. A paired comparison along the same trajectory shows where the two difer. We ran the same policy in NablaEngine and, at every recorded state and action, computed the action gradient once with the engine’s Jacobian and once with the world model’s Jacobian at that state, everything else identical. The two agree closely on most samples (Spearman correlation of the norms 0.94–0.95, median cosine 0.90–0.91, at two checkpoints) and almost perfectly on small gradients (cosine 0.96–0.98 for the smallest 10%). Agreement falls as the exact gradient grows: for the largest 0.1% of exact gradients, the world model’s gradient has only 0.35–0.43 of the norm (median), a median cosine of 0.39–0.67, and in some samples points the opposite way. The world model does not create large gradients of its own: where its gradient is largest, the exact one is large as well.

The diference is therefore one of tails, not of bias. Along the same trajectory the world model damps the exact tail (99.9th percentile 16–21 against 24–31 times the median). And with exact derivatives the unbounded events reappear: without NablaEngine’s rules for them, single samples reach $6 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 2 4 }$ times the median and some are not finite. Such samples are rare, but one of them dominates a batch-mean gradient. A few samples carry huge or infinite values and no learning signal; the rest of the batch is as good as the surrogate’s, and unbiased.

More exactness is not more signal. Making the derivative of force-saturated actuators exact changed about 20% of per-state gradients but did not improve learning: −0.023 after 1 h (3 seeds, within two seed standard deviations), and no diference together with the self-contact rule below or after 3 h.

Bias versus cost. A world model is cheap to evaluate and to diferentiate: one network pass, independent of contact complexity. SuperMimic reports that its world model runs more than 10× faster than MuJoCo Warp at the batch sizes used in practice and 62× faster at 64k parallel environments, and that its training needs over 1000× fewer GPU-hours than SONIC’s cluster-scale PPO pipeline [39]. The simulators that robot learning commonly uses are slower still on this robot. One control step of 4096 G1 worlds takes 5.8 ms in MuJoCo Warp, 12.9 ms in Isaac Lab with Newton and 27.5–54.1 ms with PhysX, 34.7 ms in MuJoCo C on a 16-thread CPU and 255 ms in MJX, all forward only (one RTX PRO 6000; Table 5). Few of them provide a gradient of the full contact dynamics at all: PhysX does not, MuJoCo C ofers finite diferences, MJX only forward-mode derivatives on this model, and MuJoCo Warp only through an open pull request.

Per policy update, one SuperMimic iteration takes about 0.1 s on an H100 (estimated from its paper, Section E). The same first-order recipe as ours on the MuJoCo Warp adjoint takes 1.63 s per update, 16× more. Even on NablaEngine an update takes 0.37 s, still more than the world model. Exact gradients make up for it in the number of updates: ∇Mimic reaches 0.82 after 3.2k updates, about 1/13 of the roughly 41k iterations SuperMimic needs (estimated from its paper; Section E gives the estimate and an alternative reading). This is how we read SuperMimic’s result. Per update, training on exact gradients needs far fewer: 3.2k updates against about 41k. Per second, at the cost of the simulators available at the time, the surrogate made faster progress. The world model won on price.

What exact gradients need. Both trade-ofs point at the same two requirements, and at how to meet them diferently from a world model. A surrogate pays one bias, on every sample, to buy two things at once: bounded derivatives and a cheap update. We buy them separately. Boundedness costs a bias confined to the few samples that are cut, with the informative bulk of the batch left exact; this is the job of ∇Mimic (Section 5). Cheapness costs engineering, not bias: the adjoint has to come down to where the higher learning signal per update pays of in wall clock; this is the job of NablaEngine (Section 6). Applied to the world-model gradients of a public SuperMimic reproduction, ∇Mimic’s components did not give a lasting gain: with per-sample clipping the return after 2.7 h was 0.80 against 0.79 without it, and with clipping and action targets together training collapsed after 1.5 h (one seed each).

## 4.2 When first-order methods fit

Dense rewards. Exact gradients are most useful when the reward is dense and smooth in the state. Motion tracking is such a task: the reward at every step is a sum of exponential kernels of tracking errors, so its gradient points toward the reference everywhere except at contact events, and those are what per-sample clipping handles

Horizon trade-of. With sparse rewards and long-range dependencies the picture changes, and the window length � sets a second trade-of. A long window lets the gradient reach a distant reward and relies less on the critic $V _ { \phi }$ , whose estimate is biased. But the tail of the gradient grows with every step it is backpropagated through (Fig. 3): over 32 steps the 99.9th percentile grows several-fold and, for SHAC’s raw gradients, the maximum by eight to nine orders of magnitude. With the same policy, exact and world-model gradients grow alike, so the accumulation is a property of long chains of Jacobians, not of contact alone. A world model does not escape this trade-of; it only starts from a lighter tail.

A short window keeps the gradient clean but leaves more to the critic. Section 5.4 measures this trade-of for tracking. For tasks whose reward lies seconds away, whether diferentiability can be exploited depends on this trade-of, and first-order methods may not pay of.

## 5 ∇Mimic

We design ∇Mimic to learn from exact gradients as they are, with their rare extreme samples and a scale that varies by orders of magnitude (Section 4). It is a windowed first-order actor-critic built around two components: per-sample clipping and multistep regression onto gradient-derived action targets. The two are a pair. The action targets fix the size of every update in radians, and clipping keeps any single sample from spending that budget alone: without clipping, one extreme sample moves its target by about 15 rad and every other sample’s by almost nothing; with it, no sample moves more than 0.13 rad (Section 5.3). Each update simulates a batch of BPTT training windows of � steps on NablaEngine, backpropagates the window return (Eq. (1)) through the analytic adjoint, and updates the actor from the resulting action gradients. Algorithm 1 summarizes one update. Each component has its own subsection and ablation (Fig. 4, Table 2).

Inherited components. We keep the data path of SHAC and SuperMimic [39, 81] unchanged, so that our comparison isolates the source and handling of the gradient: window start states come from a replay of states visited by a separate data collector that runs the current policy, the collector’s resets are sampled by return-based bins after BeyondMimic [40], and a critic $V _ { \phi }$ fit with TD(�) bootstraps the window return. ## 5.1 Per-sample median-relative clipping

The extreme exact gradients of Section 4 live in individual samples: a few environments near contact events carry action cotangents orders of magnitude above the rest and dominate a batch-mean gradient. A global norm clip, as in SHAC, rescales the whole batch and destroys the learning signal of every other sample. ∇Mimic instead clips the action cotangent of each sample, the gradient of the window return with respect to the action $a _ { t }$ of one environment at one step, to � times the batch median norm. This happens at every step of the backward pass, before the cotangent flows into the policy and from there into earlier states, and again on the window’s action gradients; the cotangent that flows directly through the dynamics from one state to the previous one is left to the engine’s rules for unbounded derivatives (Section 6):

$$
g _ {i} \leftarrow g _ {i} \cdot \min \left(1, \frac {\kappa \cdot \operatorname{median} _ {j} \| g _ {j} \|}{\| g _ {i} \|}\right), \quad \kappa = 3.\tag{4}
$$

The median is the reference because it is the one statistic the explosion never moves: in Fig. 2b the median and the 99.9th percentile stay flat through the update that halts SHAC, and only the maximum leaves. A median-relative threshold therefore follows the gradient scale over training, needs no absolute value to tune, and never cuts the backward chain. Clipping touches 9–21% of the samples, i.e. of the (step, environment) pairs, per update, removes the tail of Fig. 2a and leaves the rest of the batch unchanged. The tail does not go away as training proceeds: in an instrumented ∇Mimic run, the largest per-sample action gradient is typically $1 0 ^ { 4 }$ to $\bar { 1 } 0 ^ { 5 }$ times the batch median at every stage, and up to $1 . 9 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 }$ times; over about 92,000 updates (3 seeds) no gradient was non-finite. Clipping is not free: the gradients of the clipped samples are biased. It trades this bias for stability.

Without clipping, one of three seeds diverges after 0.54 h and the other two reach 0.562, against 0.855 for the control (Fig. 4a). Clipping is what makes exact gradients usable at all.

## 5.2 Multistep regression with gradient-derived action targets

Every exact gradient is expensive: it costs a simulated window and its adjoint. A world-model method can aford to use each imagined window for one gradient step and discard it; we want to extract as much as possible from each simulated window. Taking several gradient steps on the same window, however, would move the policy beyond the region in which that gradient describes the return, and the gradient’s scale, which varies by orders of magnitude over training and across the batch, would set the step size. We therefore adopt policy optimization by diferentiable simulation (PODS) [50]: the gradient defines targets in action space, and the policy is fit to them by several steps of regression. ∇Mimic turns the clipped action gradient $g _ { t }$ of each window step into a target action

## 5.3 Clipping and multistep regression together: bounded targets

The two components bound the update in complementary ways. Let �<sub>�</sub> be the clipped action gradient of sample � (one step of one window, $d = 2 9 { \mathrm { j o i n t s } } ) ,$ � the batch median of $\| g _ { i } \|$ and � the number of samples. The target of sample � moves its setpoints by $\delta { g _ { i } } / \mathrm { R M S } ( g )$ (Eq. (5)), where the RMS runs over all samples and joints, so the per-joint RMS change of sample � is

$$
\frac {\delta \left\| g _ {i} \right\|}{\sqrt {d} \operatorname{RMS} (g)}.\tag{6}
$$

With clipping, $\| g _ { i } \| \le \kappa m$ , and at least half of the samples have $\left\| g _ { i } \right\| \ge m ,$ , so $\operatorname { R M S } ( g ) \geq m / { \sqrt { 2 d } } .$ . Every sample’s per-joint RMS change is then at most $\sqrt { 2 } \kappa \delta \left( 0 . 1 3 \right.$ rad with our settings), whatever the scale of the gradients: each sample’s target stays within a bounded distance of its current action. The bound holds for the targets the policy is regressed onto, and the regression inherits it as a regularizer. In practice the policy moves far less: after the regression steps of an update, the typical environment’s setpoints change by 0.002 to 0.01 rad (per-joint RMS), and the largest change in any environment over a whole training run is 0.11 rad. Without clipping, a single sample with norm � far above the others gives $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { R M S } ( g ) \approx G / \sqrt { N d } ; } \end{array}$ that sample’s target moves by about $\delta \sqrt { N }$ (about 15 rad for our batch size), and the targets of all other samples barely move. The step-size budget of the whole batch goes to one sample, and the learning signal of the rest is lost. The multistep regression fixes the size of the step; clipping keeps any single sample from taking it over.

## 5.4 Window length

The window length sets the horizon trade-of of Section 4.2: longer windows rely less on the critic, shorter ones accumulate fewer extreme gradients (Fig. 3). Halving the window to � = 32 lowers the return to 0.818 (−0.036) at matched wall clock and by 0.043 at matched updates (3 seeds; Fig. 4c). For tracking the longer window is better. We attribute this to the critic: with clipping bounding the extreme gradients of longer chains, relying less on the critic’s biased estimate outweighs the extra tail, although we have not measured the critic’s error directly.

## 5.5 Residual actions

The policy outputs an ofset to the reference joint pose; the PD setpoint is reference plus ofset. An untrained policy then already follows the reference pose, so learning refines a correction instead of reproducing the motion from scratch. The action-rate penalty is applied to the commanded setpoints, so the reward, and hence the task, is identical to predicting absolute setpoints; only the parameterization changes. Residual actions learn faster (Fig. 4d) and plateau higher: ∇Mimic reaches 0.82 in 0.34 h instead of 0.50 h with absolute actions, and 0.874 in 1.8 h, after which the return stays on this plateau (Table 4).

## 6 NablaEngine

NablaEngine is a diferentiable simulation engine for the Unitree G1 that makes exact gradients cheap. It computes the dynamics of MuJoCo [74] for this robot, including the full contact model with all 502 contact pairs and the Newton constraint solver, and their analytic derivatives. Its design rests on three ideas.

One world per warp, a megakernel for the adjoint. NablaEngine specializes the step to one robot and maps each world to one warp. Within a kernel, the world’s state stays in shared memory and registers. The forward step runs as two fused kernels per substep, 8 physics kernels per 20 ms control step. The adjoint of a whole control step is a single megakernel: one warp per world reads the tape and runs the derivatives of all four substeps in reverse without leaving the kernel. (The forward step is split into two kernels per substep for occupancy; Section B.)

A hand-derived adjoint. The backward pass is derived by hand for every stage: kinematics, collision, the constraint solve and integration. The constraint solve is diferentiated through its optimality conditions, not through the solver’s iterations, with the discrete decisions (contact set, active constraints, actuator clamps) held fixed. The forward pass writes a compact tape, and the adjoint replays it in reverse. The result is the derivative of the discrete step: it matches float64 finite diferences of MuJoCo to 10<sup>−5</sup> in median relative error, and the forward step matches float64 MuJoCo to $8 . 5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 7 }$ (Section C); the forward pass is bitwise deterministic.

Bounded where the derivative is not. Near two non-diferentiable events of the G1’s geometry the exact derivative is unbounded (Section 4): self-contact at vanishing distance and nearly parallel capsules. For training, NablaEngine freezes the corresponding geometric derivatives below a small threshold (1 mm, about 2<sup>◦</sup>), and on force-saturated actuators it keeps the actuator damping term, which the exact derivative drops; making the latter exact does not improve learning (Section 4). Away from these events the training gradient equals the exact gradient bit for bit. Section B gives the details.

Speed. Table 3 compares NablaEngine with MuJoCo Warp [21], the GPU implementation of the same dynamics. A forward step is 4.8× faster on an RTX PRO 6000; a step with its gradient is 11.5× faster than the open pull request that adds an adjoint to MuJoCo Warp [13]. On NablaEngine the gradient costs less than another forward step: a step with its gradient takes 1.78 ms against 1.21 ms for the step alone, so the entire adjoint, contact solve included, adds 0.57 ms. One RTX PRO 6000 simulates 3.39 M control steps per second, about 18.8 h of robot motion per second of wall clock. Other engines and GPUs are in Section B.

## 7.2 Main result: minutes instead of hours

∇Mimic reaches SuperMimic’s LAFAN1 score of 0.82 in 20 min on one GPU (Fig. 1a). SuperMimic reports reaching it within 1.2 h on one H100, and PPO-Sim does not reach it within 18 h on 1, 4 or 8 GPUs [39, §4.1]; on an H100 the absolute-action version reaches 0.82 in 0.41 h (one seed, Section H). SuperMimic states no final score, so the time to 0.82 is the only comparison its paper allows.

Training does not stop there: 0.874 in 1.8 h, no seed falling on any clip from 1.5 h on, and three seeds that agree to a standard deviation below $1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ at 2 and 3 h.

The other gradient sources on the same engine are far behind. PPO with BeyondMimic’s configuration reaches 0.64 after 3 h and 0.70 after 6 h, even though NablaEngine makes each PPO iteration 2.9× faster than stock MuJoCo Warp (0.123 vs 0.355 s): cheaper samples do not close the gap, the gradient does. SHAC stops on its gradient guard at 0.06 h; with the guard relaxed so that exploding updates are clipped instead of halting training, it keeps learning but stays just below 0.82 for 3 h (0.816; single seeds cross it after 2.4 to 3.1 h). Table 4 gives the numbers; all curves and the cost to reach 0.82 are in Section H.

## 7.3 Same algorithm, two simulators

Is the speed-up the engine’s or the algorithm’s? Training the absolute-action version of ∇Mimic on the MuJoCo Warp adjoint, with NablaEngine’s rules for unbounded derivatives ported to it, answers this: per update the two curves overlap (Fig. 5a); per hour NablaEngine is 4.7× ahead (2.03 h vs 0.43 h to 4k updates; 4.4× per update, 1.63 vs 0.37 s). The engine changes speed, not learning.

## 8 Limitations and future work

NablaEngine currently supports one robot, the Unitree G1, on flat ground; other robots need their own specialized kernels, and uneven terrain needs height fields and further collision types. Our results are on LAFAN1 in simulation: we have not yet trained on larger motion libraries or deployed the policies on hardware. The gradient holds the discrete decisions of the contact solve fixed, so it is the exact derivative of the step almost everywhere rather than a smoothed gradient across contact changes. Per-sample clipping biases the clipped gradients, and we have not quantified this bias. The PPO baselines and the MuJoCo Warp run of ∇Mimic are single seeds. SuperMimic’s implementation is not public, so we compare with the numbers its paper states, on a diferent GPU; speed comparisons between simulators are made within one GPU type. Extending NablaEngine to more robots and terrains, scaling ∇Mimic to large motion libraries and transferring the policies to real robots are the natural next steps.

## 9 Conclusion

First-order policy learning has long promised far more informative updates than zeroth-order methods, but on contact-rich whole-body control two things held it back: rare unbounded samples in the gradient, and the cost of computing it. NablaEngine removes the second for the Unitree G1: it computes the full MuJoCo dynamics of the robot, all 502 contact pairs included, with analytic gradients that match float64 finite diferences, and its adjoint costs less than another forward step. ∇Mimic removes the first: per-sample median-relative clipping bounds the rare extreme samples, and multistep regression onto gradient-derived action targets reuses each costly sample with action targets whose size does not depend on their scale. Together they train a single policy that tracks all 40 LAFAN1 clips and reaches the score of the world-model state of the art in 20 minutes on one GPU.

Cutting the cost of a tracking policy from hours to minutes makes training on far larger and more diverse motion corpora practical, and can thereby accelerate the scaling of humanoid foundation models.

## A Task details

Data. The 40 LAFAN1 clips [23] are the Unitree G1 retarget of the LAFAN1 Retargeting Dataset [43] (its g1/ folder), used without modification. At load time the 30 FPS clips are resampled to the 50 Hz control rate (linear interpolation for root position and joint angles, spherical linear interpolation for the root orientation), and body poses and velocities are computed by forward kinematics on the robot model.

Reward. The reward follows BeyondMimic [40] with the weights and widths of SuperMimic [39, Table A.2]. Tracking terms have the form � $\exp ( - e / \sigma ^ { 2 } )$ ), with � the squared error (orientation: squared rotation angle), averaged over the bodies for the body terms. The body terms are evaluated over 14 bodies, with the reference re-anchored to the robot’s torso in horizontal position and yaw; the anchor terms use the torso itself.

Observations. The actor sees a history of the last 10 proprioception vectors (93 dimensions each): 29 joint positions relative to the default pose, 29 joint velocities, base angular velocity, gravity direction in the base frame and the previous action. Uniform noise of SuperMimic’s Table A.1 is added during training. The goal consists of the next 10 reference frames, each with 68 features expressed in the reference’s own frame (joint positions and velocities, gravity direction, root angular velocity, root linear velocity in the heading frame, root height), plus the reference anchor’s orientation relative to the robot’s anchor (6 dimensions, two rotation-matrix columns), as in BeyondMimic’s variant without state estimation. The critic is privileged: it sees noise-free proprioception, the goal, root errors and the randomization parameters.

Domain randomization. Per episode, following SuperMimic’s Table A.3: foot friction U [0.3, 1.2], joint encoder bias U[−0.01, 0.01] rad and a torso center-of-mass ofset. None is applied at evaluation.

## B Engine details

Model. NablaEngine simulates the mjlab Unitree G1 model [86]: a free base and 29 actuated hinge joints, 31 bodies, and 502 contact pairs among capsules, spheres and the floor, self-collision included. The dynamics follow MuJoCo [74]:

• recursive Newton–Euler and composite rigid-body algorithms;

• MuJoCo’s soft-constraint contact model (solref/solimp) with pyramidal friction cones;

• joint limits, dof friction loss and actuator force limits;

• Newton’s method with exact line search, 10 iterations, tolerance $1 0 ^ { - 8 }$ ;

• the implicitfast integrator, 4 substeps of 5 ms per 20 ms control step, with the control held;

• PD position actuators.

Per-world foot friction and torso center-of-mass ofsets support domain randomization.

Kernel structure. One world maps to one thread block of 32 threads (one warp). Within each kernel the world’s state stays in shared memory and registers (6.9 kB and 8.2 kB per world for the two forward kernels), and the model’s constants sit in GPU constant memory. Each substep runs two forward kernels:

• the first computes kinematics, the bias and mass terms, actuator forces, collision and the constraint rows;

• the second runs the Newton solve and the integration.

A control step is therefore 8 physics kernels for all worlds; the handof between the two kernels (mass matrix, forces, constraint rows and contact Jacobians, about 33 kB per world) and the state between substeps pass through global memory. Up to 11–12 worlds are resident per SM on the RTX PRO 6000 and the H100. The whole control step can also run as one kernel, but it needs the full per-world state (31.9 kB) in shared memory, fits fewer worlds per SM, and is slower (forward with tape, 4096 worlds, kernel time: 2.76 vs 1.23 ms on the RTX PRO 6000, 4.37 vs 1.76 ms on the H100, 6.79 vs 3.13 ms on the RTX 3090, 10.88 vs 5.98 ms on the V100). The adjoint of a control step is one kernel: one warp per world reads the tape and runs the derivatives of all four substeps in reverse, with the per-world adjoint state in shared memory.

Tape and adjoint. For the gradient, the forward pass also writes a tape of 1,472 float32 values per world and substep: 23.6 kB per world and control step, or 96.5 MB at 4096 worlds. The adjoint replays the contact set, constraint rows and solver state from it and runs every stage in reverse:

• integration;

• the constraint solve, diferentiated through its optimality conditions with the discrete decisions (contact set, active constraints, actuator clamps) held fixed;

• collision;

• kinematics and dynamics.

Gradient modes. Exact mode (clamp\_kv = 1, normal = 0) is the derivative of the discrete step. The training default (clamp\_kv = 0, normal = 3) keeps the actuator damping term on force-clamped actuators, freezes the derivative of a self-contact normal when the two geoms’ core distance (between sphere centers or capsule axes) is below 1 mm, and holds the segment parameters of near-parallel capsule pairs (below about 2<sup>◦</sup>) fixed. On held-out states the two modes have the same median gradient errors on groups G0, G5 and G6; on G4, the states with clamped actuators, the truth has no clamp $\mathbf { k } \mathbf { v } = 0$ counterpart.

Unbounded derivatives. Two geometric events of the G1 make the exact derivative of the step unbounded.

• Self-contact at vanishing distance. The derivative of the contact normal scales as the inverse of the distance between the two geoms’ cores. One state with nearly touching feet had a step Jacobian norm of about $3 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 }$ On 24 training windows of $H = 6 4$ (2048 environments each), the gradient grew by more than $1 0 ^ { 6 }$ through 5 windows, by up to $4 . 9 \times 1 0 ^ { 2 1 }$ , and 32 rows were not finite. With the normal’s derivative frozen below 1 mm, 1 of 24 windows grew by more than $1 0 ^ { 6 }$ (at most $2 . 2 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 } )$ and no row was non-finite.

• Nearly parallel capsules. The closest points of two capsule segments are ill-conditioned, roughly as $1 / \sin ^ { 2 } \theta .$ On 172 constructed foot-crossing states, the exact rule amplified a vector–Jacobian product by up to $3 . 6 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 }$ per control step (median 29); repeated over 64 steps, 40 of the 172 states overflowed to non-finite values. With the segment parameters held fixed below about $2 ^ { \circ }$ , the amplification was at most 109 and no state overflowed; ordinary held-out states reach at most 273.

Other events (contact on or of, a constraint row at the edge of its zone, actuator saturation, the solver at its iteration limit) make the derivative jump rather than diverge. The training default removes the two unbounded terms and, on force-saturated actuators, keeps the actuator damping term (Section B, gradient modes); on held-out states without these events it equals the exact gradient bit for bit.

Determinism. Each world’s whole step runs in one warp with a fixed assignment of work to lanes, and worlds never interact. Reductions are warp shufles in a fixed order, with no floating-point atomics. The operations the adjoint replays from the tape use explicitly rounded arithmetic, so the replay equals the forward pass bit for bit. Outputs and gradients are bitwise identical from run to run: across three processes on 8192 held-out states (V100), and across repeated calls within a process in the verification suite (V100, H100).

Timing protocol. Times are per 20 ms control step for 4096 worlds, on held-out states. Each value is the median over three processes. Every simulator runs in its own standard configuration, and speed-ups are always within one GPU. Isaac Lab rows use Isaac Lab’s own G1 asset and task defaults; Isaac Sim requires an RT-core GPU, so they are not available on the H100 and V100. The MuJoCo C reference runs on one AMD EPYC 9555 (16 threads) with MuJoCo’s own thread pool. The MuJoCo Warp gradient times are those of the open adjoint pull request [13] as published; its public follow-up fixes [12], which the accuracy tables also report, change the cost by less than 2%. The MuJoCo Warp training run of Section 7.3 uses the pull request with these fixes and with NablaEngine’s training gradient rules ported to it.

## D Hyperparameters and baselines

Network architecture. The actor follows SuperMimic’s encoder–decoder design [39]. A motion encoder maps the next 10 reference frames (68 features each) through an MLP with hidden widths 2048–1024–512–256 to a 32-dimensional latent code with a tanh output. A decoder with the same hidden widths maps the latent code, the 10-frame proprioception history (93 features per frame) and the 6-dimensional anchor orientation to the 29 joint ofsets. The critic is an MLP with hidden widths 256–128 on privileged inputs (Section A).

The design encodes the reference motion into a compact latent space and lets the decoder act as a general motor policy conditioned on that code. This is the structure along which tracking policies are scaled toward foundation models for humanoid control: the latent code is the interface through which motion generators, teleoperation or downstream policies command the tracker, and the same decoder serves all motions [9, 40, 42]. We keep it, rather than a monolithic MLP, so that a policy trained with ∇Mimic can be used the same way.

## Baselines.

• PPO, BeyondMimic configuration [40, 63]: on NablaEngine (forward only), 4096 environments × 24 steps per iteration, 5 epochs × 4 minibatches, lr 10<sup>−3</sup> (adaptive, KL 0.01), clip 0.2, entropy 0.005, � = 0.99, � = 0.95, MLP 512–256–128, BeyondMimic’s adaptive sampling; 6 h budget.

• PPO, SuperMimic actor and reset sampling: as above with 8192 environments, SuperMimic’s encoder–decoder actor (2048–1024–512–256) and critic (256–128), and return-based reset sampling; 6 h budget.

• SHAC [81]: see Section F.

All baselines use the same environment, observation and evaluation reward as ∇Mimic. The smooth termination penalty (Eq. (3)) is part of our recipe and does not account for the gaps: removing all of fall handling, the penalty included, changes the return of ∇Mimic by less than 0.01 at matched wall clock (3 seeds, both recipes).

## E SuperMimic reproduction and cost estimate

Reproduction. The gradient analysis of Section 4 uses a public reproduction of SuperMimic (https://github.com/ CuboiLeo/SuperMimic-Reproduced) with the hyperparameters stated in its paper: windows of 32 imagined steps for 4096 rollouts per update, an update-to-data ratio of 0.4, and a world model that is an MLP with three hidden layers of 2048 units and SiLU activations, predicting the change of the canonicalized state from the state, the action and the simulation context. Its policy observes the anchor orientation of the reference as ours does. We trained one run on an RTX PRO 6000 and evaluated its policy with our protocol (Section G); it reaches a full-clip return of 0.789 after 2.88 h. It is used only for the gradient measurements, not as a baseline, and its checkpoints at 0.44, 1.14, 1.83 and 2.88 h provide the policies and world models of Section 4.

Cost estimate. SuperMimic states 320,000 training iterations and that the world model and the policy are updated in every iteration. Its BONES-SEED training curve, with a checkpoint every 4,000 iterations, ends at 9.37 h on one H100, which gives about 34,000 iterations per hour, or about 0.1 s per iteration; we assume that LAFAN1 iterations cost the same. Reaching 0.82 within 1.2 h then corresponds to about 41,000 iterations. If one instead counts policy updates at the stated update-to-data ratio of 0.4, as the public reproduction does, these become about 16,000 updates of about 0.26 s each, and ∇Mimic’s 3,235 updates to 0.82 are about one fifth of them rather than one thirteenth

## F SHAC details

Both SHAC variants use the same environment, actor, critic, 4096 environments, learning-rate schedule and window noise as ∇Mimic. SHAC’s algorithm:

• � = 32 windows continued across updates, with no replay starts and no collector;

• the actor’s own window gradient, one Adam step per window;

• no per-sample clipping and no termination penalty;

• a TD(� = 0.95) critic, empirical observation normalization and uniform reset sampling.

SHAC (published guard) clips the actor gradient to global norm 1.0 and stops training at the first update whose norm before clipping is not finite or exceeds 10<sup>6</sup>, as in the published code. SHAC (changed guards) instead clips to global norm 1.0 and then element-wise to 0.1, as SuperMimic does, and skips non-finite updates instead of stopping. Both run on NablaEngine; the published-guard variant also runs on MuJoCo Warp with the engine’s gradient semantics. They difer from ∇Mimic in several components at once, so they are baselines, not single-component ablations.

## G Evaluation protocol

Each of the 40 LAFAN1 clips is played once from its first reference frame. Playback uses MuJoCo Warp with 10 solver iterations, a deterministic actor (the mean action), no domain randomization and no observation or action noise. The clip’s return is the sum of the per-step reward over its steps until the clip ends or the first termination (height error above 0.25 m or tilt above 0.8). The per-step reward is the six tracking terms minus the action-rate and joint-limit penalties. The sum is divided by the clip’s maximum attainable tracking reward, and the score is the unweighted mean over the 40 clips. A clip with a fall therefore keeps the reward earned before the fall and scores zero afterwards. This matches the normalized return defined by Liao et al. [39]. Failed clips are clips with a fall.

Every checkpoint of the first training hour is evaluated (every 250 updates), then every 1000 updates, plus the last. Training wall clock excludes start-up compilation and all evaluation. Times to a score are first crossings of the 3-seed mean curve, linearly interpolated between evaluations.
