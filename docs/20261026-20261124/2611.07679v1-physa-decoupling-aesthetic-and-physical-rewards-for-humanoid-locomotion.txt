# PhySA: Decoupling Aesthetic and Physical Rewards for Humanoid Locomotion

Abstract: Humanoid locomotion policies trained without an explicit smoothness objective tend to produce jerky, high-energy gaits that stress actuators. Visionlanguage models (VLMs) offer an appealing reward source: given a rendered rollout, a VLM returns an aesthetic quality score on [0, 10] without task-specific feature engineering. We find, however, that aesthetic preference and physical smoothness point in opposite directions: across four production VLMs, clips with higher joint acceleration receive higher aesthetic scores (mean Pearson r=+0.66). We study PhySA (Physical Smoothness and Aesthetics), which keeps the two on separate, independently removable reward channels: a sparse, asynchronous VLM score and a phase-triggered correction channel that adjusts physical penalty weights from simulator state. Ablations attribute the smoothness gain to the correction channel. On MuJoCo Humanoid-v5 at 2.6M steps, PhySA lowers joint acceleration by 26 to 32% relative to vanilla PPO, Text2Reward, and Eureka (three seeds), at the cost of shorter episodes. In a single extended run it lowers joint acceleration by 59.5% relative to Prompt2Policy, while a three-seed replication shows a smaller, non-significant reduction (7.2%). The gain does not transfer to IsaacLab or the Unitree G1, even after rescaling the smoothness term.

Keywords: Humanoid Locomotion, Reinforcement Learning, Reward Engineering, Vision-Language Models, Reward Shaping

## 1 Introduction

Smooth motion is a practical requirement for deploying humanoid robots on hardware. High joint velocity accelerates actuator wear, increases energy draw, and produces trajectories that low-level servo controllers struggle to track faithfully [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Yet locomotion policies trained without an explicit smoothness objective consistently learn jerky, high-energy gaits. Encoding smoothness alongside forward progress in a single reward requires careful weight balancing: a large smoothness coefficient tends to destabilize early training, while a small one leaves joint acceleration ∥q¨∥ uncontrolled.

Vision-language models (VLMs) trained on human motion video offer an appealing alternative reward source: given a rendered rollout, a VLM returns a continuous aesthetic quality score on [0, 10] without task-specific feature engineering [6, 7, 8, 9]. Prompt2Policy [10] and related work [11] demonstrate this in practice by querying a VLM at every training update and incorporating the score directly into the per-step reward. LLM-synthesized reward functions [12, 13] take a different route: reward code is written once from a natural-language description, avoiding in-loop queries, but the resulting structure is fixed and cannot respond to failure modes that only emerge during training. Smoothness-specific regularizers [14, 15] encode the objective directly but require per-robot weight tuning and can destabilize early training if applied too aggressively.

One might hope that a VLM aesthetic score could serve as a jerk proxy, sidestepping hand-crafted penalties altogether. The data say otherwise. Across four production VLMs, jerkier, more energetic motion consistently scored higher; these models appear to read large joint excursions as confident locomotion and smooth, low-amplitude motion as stiff or hesitant. Using the aesthetic score as a smoothness signal would therefore push the policy toward higher joint acceleration, not lower. VLMs can, in principle, judge gait qualities such as knee flexion timing and swing symmetry that per-step losses do not encode, so the aesthetic signal may still be worth keeping, but not as a proxy for smoothness. Keeping aesthetic scoring and physical smoothness on separate channels lets each be measured and adjusted without disturbing the other.

We propose PhySA (Physical Smoothness and Aesthetics), a reward engineering framework for humanoid locomotion built around two separate channels: an asynchronous VLM aesthetic scorer and a phase-triggered physical correction module. The channels share no reward terms, so each can be ablated or tuned without touching the other. In a single extended training run on MuJoCo Humanoidv5 [16, 17], PhySA reduces joint acceleration ∥q¨∥<sub>2</sub> by 59.5% relative to Prompt2Policy [10], with 43% lower mechanical power and 57% longer episodes; a three-seed from-scratch replication shows a smaller, non-significant reduction (7.2%, p=0.45). Against vanilla PPO, Text2Reward, and Eureka at 2.6M steps (three seeds), PhySA lowers joint acceleration by 26 to 32% while producing shorter episodes. The VLM channel in isolation raises joint acceleration by 136%, and ablations attribute the smoothness gain to the correction channel. The smoothness gain does not carry over to IsaacLab or the Unitree G1, even after rescaling the smoothness term; a timestep experiment within MuJoCo points to the numerical integration scheme [18, 19, 20] as a major source of this gap.

Our main contributions are:

• A finding that VLM aesthetic scores and joint-acceleration roughness are positively correlated across four production VLMs (mean Pearson r=+0.66), so a VLM aesthetic score should not be used as a proxy for physical smoothness.

• PhySA, a reward design in which an asynchronous VLM aesthetic scorer and a phasetriggered physical correction module operate on two separate reward channels, each removable without affecting the other; ablations show the smoothness gain comes from the correction channel, and a timestep experiment points to physics integration as the main obstacle to cross-simulator transfer.

## 2 Related Work

Vision-language rewards for policy learning. LIV [21] and RoboCLIP [22] build shared embedding spaces between video and language, computing dense per-step shaping rewards by projecting rollout frames into a joint vision-language space; these methods target task completion and goal alignment but do not monitor physical smoothness. Prompt2Policy [10] and related work [11] query GPT-4-class VLMs on rendered rollouts at every policy update, incorporating the returned score directly into the per-step reward. This synchronous design blocks the training loop at each update and incurs API costs proportional to training length (roughly 1,220 calls per 10M steps). PhySA instead dispatches scoring calls asynchronously at one call per 200k steps (50 per 10M steps, 24× fewer), and separates aesthetic scoring from smoothness correction into distinct reward terms so that each can be ablated independently.

LLM-synthesized reward functions. Eureka [12] uses iterative LLM prompting [23] to synthesize reward code from natural-language task descriptions, then evaluates and refines candidates over multiple rounds; once the best candidate is selected, the reward structure is frozen for the duration of training and cannot be revised in response to unexpected policy behaviors that emerge. Text2Reward [13] follows a similar single-shot synthesis approach with the same structural limitation. PhySA addresses this by monitoring training statistics at phase boundaries and making targeted edits to individual reward terms when stagnation is detected, adapting the reward structure to what the policy is actually doing.

Smoothness in humanoid reinforcement learning. Action-rate penalties [14, 15] add fixed regularization terms to discourage rapid action changes; gradient-surgery projections [24] project conflicting gradients into a feasible subspace at optimization time; multi-objective formulations [25, 26, 27, 28] treat smoothness and task performance as separate objectives with a fixed Pareto weighting; imitation-based motion priors [29, 30] impose smoothness implicitly by training the policy to match pre-recorded reference trajectories. All of these approaches commit to a fixed reward structure or motion prior before training begins and are unable to adjust their objectives once training is underway. Applied too early, a fixed jerk penalty tends to destabilize learning; applied too late, it fails to dislodge an already entrenched high-jerk gait. PhySA instead monitors training statistics at phase boundaries and edits individual reward weights in response to what the policy is actually doing (Sec. 4.3).

VLM evaluation biases. VLMs used as evaluators carry well-documented preference biases that can diverge from the intended target [31, 32, 33, 34]. In text-generation settings these biases (length preference, self-consistency, positional effects) are well characterized; in robotics they are rarely measured. We find that across four production VLMs, jerkier motion clips receive higher aesthetic scores (mean Pearson r=+0.66, ∼100 paired episodes per model; Spearman inter-model agreement [+0.49, +0.94], mean +0.76). The VLMs appear to read energetic limb motion as confident locomotion, the same heuristic that makes human raters prefer action in sports video. Since this bias runs opposite to physical smoothness, using the VLM score as a jerk proxy produces the wrong gradient. The two objectives need separate channels, not a single conflated score.

## 3 Method

VLM-based reward has become a near-default choice in embodied AI, yet what a vision-language model evaluates from a rendered clip is appearance, not dynamics. A single frame contains no acceleration term; a VLM’s notion of “effort” is therefore inherited from human video, where large limb excursions read as confident, purposeful motion. This learned prior is misaligned with physical smoothness, and the misalignment is not idiosyncratic to one model: all four production VLMs we evaluate rate jerkier rollouts more favorably (Sec. 1). The implication for reward design is a clean separation of responsibility. Properties legible within a single frame (posture, limb coordination, bilateral symmetry) are well suited to VLM scoring. Properties defined by the trajectory’s timederivatives, such as joint acceleration, are not observable from a still frame and must instead be computed from simulator state; conflating the two into a single scalar would let the appearancebased signal dominate, since only that signal is actually supervised by the VLM.

A further complication is that the physical-side weight cannot be set a priori. Its effective scale is coupled to the simulator’s integration timestep: within MuJoCo alone, varying $\Delta t$ shifts the same joint-acceleration statistic by close to 5× (Sec. 4.4), and it also shifts with the policy’s competence over the course of training, since a penalty that is negligible early on can come to dominate once the policy’s control becomes more energetic, or the reverse. This is not a matter of insufficient tuning effort: a weight suitable at initialization has no guarantee of remaining suitable at convergence, so no offline sweep can be expected to identify one setting that holds throughout training. We instead leave this weight unspecified in advance and adjust it online at phase boundaries by monitoring training statistics (Sec. 3.3), while the VLM channel is held at a fixed weight throughout, responsible only for the appearance-level signal it can reliably supervise.

PhySA runs two reward channels on top of a standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [35] setup implementing this division; Fig. 2 shows the complete system. The per-step reward is

$$
r _ {\text { total }} (t) = r _ {\text { task }} (t) + \alpha r _ {\text { VLM }} (t) + r _ {\text { phys }} (t) + r _ {\text { GMP }} (t),\tag{1}
$$

where $r _ { \mathrm { t a s k } }$ is a hand-crafted locomotion reward (forward velocity, alive bonus, height penalty, actuator cost), $\alpha r _ { \mathrm { V L M } }$ is the aesthetic channel, $r _ { \mathrm { p h y s } }$ is the physical correction channel, and r is a Gaussian motion prior fit to motion-capture data [36], held constant across all conditions. r<sub>task</sub> covers the basic locomotion objective; $r _ { \mathrm { V L M } }$ is meant to reflect holistic gait qualities (knee use, swing symmetry, postural stability) judged from rendered clips (in its current form a global scalar, Sec. 3.2); $r _ { \mathrm { p h y s } }$ enforces joint-acceleration smoothness, which VLM scoring actively opposes; and r<sub>GMP</sub> keeps the gait in a naturalistic range. Routing the aesthetic and correction signals through separate terms means either can be switched off cleanly (Sec. 4.3).

## 3.1 Task reward

To isolate the effects of the two added channels, we carry over Prompt2Policy’s [10] task reward without modification: a Gaussian velocity-tracking term at $v ^ { * } { = } 0 . 5 \mathrm { m } / \mathrm { s } .$ , an alive bonus conditioned on torso height remaining within the standing band, a quadratic height penalty, and an L2 actuator cost. Any gait difference between PhySA and the baseline therefore originates in the two added channels, not in task-reward changes.

## 3.2 Aesthetic reward channel

Forward velocity and energy cost are straightforward to encode as per-step scalar penalties. Gait appearance qualities such as knee flexion timing, swing symmetry, and postural stability are harder to capture this way, as they depend on the trajectory as a whole rather than any single timestep. A VLM trained on large-scale human video can assess these holistically from a rendered clip, without task-specific feature engineering. The aesthetic channel (amber path in Fig. 2) dispatches all scoring calls asynchronously on a background thread, so the training loop never blocks.

Every 200k steps the trainer renders a 12-frame clip and queries GPT-4o-2024-05-13 [37] (temperature=0). The prompt elicits a [0, 10] rating on three sub-axes (knee use, swing symmetry, center-of-mass tracking) plus an overall scalar; only overall enters the reward. Fifty calls over 10M steps is sufficient for the EMA to converge while keeping API spend under \$1. At ∼2500 steps/s on a single RTX 4090, each 200k-step interval spans roughly 80 s of wall time, comfortably longer than the 1–3 s API round-trip, so no rollout is ever delayed.

The aesthetic scalar is smoothed via an EMA and recentered,

$$
\operatorname{vlm} _ {\mathrm{ema}} ^ {(t)} = 0. 8 \operatorname{vlm} _ {\mathrm{ema}} ^ {(t - 1)} + 0. 2 s _ {t}, \quad r _ {\mathrm{VLM}} = \operatorname{vlm} _ {\mathrm{ema}} / 1 0 - 0. 5 \in [ - 0. 5, + 0. 5 ],
$$

and enters Eq. (1) weighted by α. The EMA keeps the per-step signal continuous between sparse queries and limits the impact of any single missed call. VLM scores agree across the four models we tested (Spearman correlation between model pairs: [+0.49, +0.94], mean +0.76), though what the score measures semantically remains an open question. Appendix B.3 confirms that smoothness improvements are driven by the correction channel, not the VLM signal. A policy trained specifically to maximize posture cues without a smoothness constraint does not outscore PhySA on this evaluator (Appendix E), so the VLM is not simply rewarding jerk for its own sake. We fix $\alpha { = } 0 . 5 ;$ sensitivity across $\alpha \in [ 0 . 2 , 0 . 8 ]$ is in Appendix B.3.

What this channel can and cannot do. Because r<sub>VLM</sub> is updated once every 200k steps and added unchanged to every step until the next query, it does not depend on the current state or action. For example, a score of 6 gives every step in the next interval the same +0.05 (with α=0.5), whatever the robot’s posture. Under the Humanoid-v5 termination rule, a constant per-step term changes only how much the policy values survival, much like an adjustment to the alive bonus; it cannot reward a specific posture or gait directly.

## 3.3 Physical reward channel

The physical correction channel (teal path in Fig. 2) applies phase-triggered edits to penalty terms in $r _ { \mathrm { p h y s } }$ , targeting joint acceleration ∥q¨∥ . Training is divided into four sequential phases of 2.5M steps, each warm-started from the previous checkpoint. At each phase boundary, a criterion checks recent training logs across three axes (postural stability, energy efficiency, and episode reward) and fires a targeted reward edit when any axis regresses without a compensating gain elsewhere, or when all three stagnate simultaneously. A trigger raises actuator cost coefficients on high-velocity joints, removes a counterproductive penalty term, or leaves the reward unchanged; across both production runs, the criterion triggered at 3 of 8 phase boundaries (37.5%). Phase-level triggering is preferred over step-level monitoring because short-window statistics primarily reflect gradient noise; the 2.5M-step interval gives the policy sufficient time to adapt before the next evaluation. Full implementation details are in Appendix A.1.

## 4 Experiments

The experiments address three questions. RQ1: Does PhySA improve smoothness and task performance jointly, and what is the mechanism? RQ2: Which channel drives the smoothness gain, and can either be removed without loss? RQ3: Does the reward transfer across simulators and robot bodies, and if not, what breaks?

## 4.1 Setup

All conditions use Stable Baselines 3 (SB3) [38] PPO with identical hyperparameters (Appendix A.3) on MuJoCo Humanoid-v5 [39]; Prompt2Policy [10] is the primary baseline. Each condition runs $N \in \{ 3 , 5 , 8 , 1 0 , 3 0 \}$ seeds; cells with $N { < } 8$ are directional only. Paired t-tests use false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini-Hochberg) [40, 41] at $\alpha { = } 0 . 0 5 ;$ effect sizes are reported as standardized mean differences [42]. Physical smoothness is measured as mean joint acceleration $\| \ddot { \mathbf { q } } \| _ { 2 }$ , finite-differenced from joint position. Comparisons across evaluation protocols use ∆% only.

## 4.2 MuJoCo Humanoid-v5: smoother motion across all metrics (RQ1)

PhySA reduces mean joint acceleration $\| \ddot { \mathbf { q } } \| _ { 2 }$ by 59.5% over Prompt2Policy in a single training chain (Table 1). In this chain each method continues training from its own 10M checkpoint for four 2.5M-step phases; a from-scratch replication over three seeds shows a smaller, non-significant gap $( - 7 . 2 \% , p { = } 0 . 4 5 )$ , so the headline magnitude should be read as a single-run result. Relative to Prompt2Policy, the smoothness gain does not come at the cost of episode length: episodes are 57% longer, per-step mechanical power drops 43.1%, and per-episode work falls 13.1% even after accounting for the longer episode duration. Against vanilla PPO, Text2Reward, and Eureka, however, PhySA trades shorter episodes for lower joint acceleration (Appendix B.4). Episodes are also short in absolute terms (123.5 steps, about $1 . 9 \ \mathrm { s } ,$ for PhySA), so these measurements cover early locomotion rather than long-horizon gait quality.

Mechanism. The correction module raises penalty coefficients on high-velocity joints; the policy responds by concentrating movement into a smaller set ofjoints and holding the rest nearly still. Smoothness is thus achieved through gait specialization rather than uniform deceleration, which explains why torque rises while velocity falls.

## 4.3 Module isolation: identifying the source of the smoothness gain (RQ2)

Running each channel in isolation confirms that the two pull in opposite directions on the smoothness axis. The VLM aesthetic channel alone increases roughness by 136% while scoring higher on all four VLM evaluation dimensions (N=8; Appendix B.6). The correction module alone reduces roughness by 29.8% but earns lower aesthetic scores (N=50). The combined PhySA result does not lie on the line connecting these two points (Fig. 4): neither channel can substitute for the other. ## 4.4 Diagnosing the transfer gap (RQ3)

Per-platform reward tuning is well-established in simulation-to-real transfer [44, 45]. The experiments here are not about demonstrating successful transfer; they are about identifying which component ofthe reward breaks and why, a diagnosis that points at what must be fixed before hardware deployment. The short answer is that the per-step scale of the smoothness term shifts across simulators with the integration timestep, while the reward direction stays intact.

Isaac simulator (physics engine change only). To isolate the effect of the physics engine while holding everything else constant, we transfer the PhySA policy to IsaacLab [46, 47] with the same humanoid task specification and the same robot body. Direct transfer gives a −2.2% smoothness gap, within noise. Empirically, reweighting the smoothness term by 50×–60× (chosen empirically to match the per-step magnitude of the smoothness term) keeps the gap at −1.0% to −1.1% (p ∈ {0.91, 0.87}, N=5; Fig. 5). This is a controlled experiment: only the physics engine changes. The Unitree G1 experiment below changes both simulator and embodiment simultaneously, so its null result cannot be attributed to either factor alone.

Isolating the integrator. To test whether the integration step is a major cause of the smoothness gap, we retrain in MuJoCo with ∆t forced to $1 / 6 0 \mathrm { ~ s ~ } ( N { = } 3 )$ while holding all other settings fixed. Joint acceleration worsens by nearly 5×. At the standard MuJoCo timestep (0.015 s), each physics step internally integrates over five substeps, averaging out contact-spike noise; at the coarser step size those spikes are unsmoothed and dominate the $| | \ddot { \mathbf { q } } | | _ { 2 }$ measure. The policy’s control frequency (set by frame skip) is unchanged across both conditions: the policy receives the same number of action decisions per simulated second, and only the physics engine’s internal integration resolution differs. This within-MuJoCo swap points to the integration scheme as a major source of the crosssimulator gap and confirms that the reward direction itself remains intact (Appendix F).

Unitree G1 robot. Unlike the Isaac experiment, the G1 experiment changes both the simulator and the robot body simultaneously (a different physics engine and a different 23-joint embodiment), so any outcome cannot be attributed to either factor alone. One concrete miscalibration illustrates the scope of the problem: the Humanoid-v5 alive bonus fires while z ≥1.1 m, a threshold that never triggers on the G1 at its standing height of 0.793 m, effectively inverting the survival signal. After fixing this threshold and running a 38-condition hyperparameter search, the smoothness improvement remains non-significant (mean −1.7%, best −4.8%; Appendix G). A G1 experiment inside MuJoCo, holding the simulator fixed and changing only the embodiment, would cleanly separate the two factors but was not run; this remains the most direct next step for attributing the G1 null result.

## 5 Conclusion

PhySA separates VLM aesthetic scoring from physical smoothness correction into two independent reward channels. The two objectives conflict: VLMs score jerkier motion higher, the VLM channel alone raises joint acceleration by 136%, and the correction module alone reduces it by 29.8%; in the combined stack the gain comes from the correction channel. A VLM aesthetic score should therefore not serve as a smoothness proxy; smoothness needs its own channel.

Limitations and future work. Our largest effect (59.5%) comes from a single training run; a threeseed replication shows a non-significant 7.2%. Episodes are short (Sec. 4.2), and the VLM channel acts only through the value of survival (Sec. 3.2), so a clip-level or state-conditioned VLM reward is a natural next step. The gain does not carry over to other simulators even after rescaling, and on the G1 a height threshold copied from Humanoid-v5 kept the alive bonus from firing until fixed (Appendix G); a G1 run inside MuJoCo is the natural next step.

## A Implementation Details and Reproducibility

## A.1 Aesthetic reward channel, physical reward channel, and training stabilization

Every 200k env steps the trainer renders a 12-frame single-view MP4 (MPEG-4 video, 0.4 s at 30 fps, written via imageio) and dispatches a GPT-4o-2024-05-13 scoring call [37] (temperature=0, seed=42) on a background ThreadPoolExecutor; the main PPO loop never blocks (measured wall-clock overhead −4.1% at 1M, Appendix B.7). The VLM returns a JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) response with three per-dimension aesthetic scores (knee redundancy, swing symmetry, COM tracking) and an overall scalar in [0, 10]; only the overall feeds the reward channel via an EMA, vlm $\mathrm {  ~ l ~ } _ { \mathrm { e m a } } ^ { ( t ) } = 0 . 8 \mathrm { v l m } _ { \mathrm { e m a } } ^ { ( t - 1 ) } + 0 . 2 s _ { t }$ , mapped to $r _ { \mathrm { V L M } } = \mathrm { v l m } _ { \mathrm { e m a } } / 1 0 - 0 . 5 \in$ $[ - 0 . 5 , + 0 . 5 ]$

Phase-triggered modification. At each 2.5M-step phase boundary the selection criterion (multicriteria check: has any training axis regressed without a compensating gain on another?) reads the last 30 PPO log rows and computes ∆stability, ∆energy, ∆ep mean. It triggers a targeted reward edit under either a stability regression (∆stability $< - 0 . 0 4 )$ or full stagnation (no metric improves by $\geq + 0 . 0 1 ) ;$ otherwise it proceeds to the next phase without modification. Across the two production runs described in Sec. 4, the criterion triggered at 3 of 8 phase boundaries (37.5%).

## A.1.1 Axis-to-reward-term mapping

The correction module identifies the underperforming control axis from recent training metrics via the multi-criteria stagnation detection procedure described in Appendix A.1, returning a label ∈ {knee, swing, com, none}. Depending on the identified axis, targeted modifications are applied to specific reward terms: a knee verdict raises the L2 actuator cost at the two knee indices (ctrl cost, indices 6 and 10), while com instead zeros the upright-balance penalty (upright). A swing verdict removes the asymmetric swing penalty (swing trap) rather than adjusting joint costs. If the criterion returns none, no term is modified and the currently active reward terms remain frozen for the subsequent phase.

The per-phase diagnostic logs record what the criterion computed at each boundary (selection criterion output, stagnation flag, axis decision). In the PhySA trace, the targeted reward term shifts from a knee-velocity group at phase 0 to a single hip-velocity axis by phases 2–3; the set of active modifications narrows over training as the reward edits take effect.

Stabilization patches. Three additional components further stabilize the two-channel reward: EMA smoothing on $r _ { \mathrm { V L M } } ~ ( \alpha { = } 0 . 2$ , variance ratio 0.816 at 10M); an early-training guard that scales $\alpha _ { \mathrm { e f f } } { = } 0 . 2 \alpha$ when the mean episode length falls below 100 steps (fires $0 / 4 0 0$ times at 10M, 3/10 times at 1M); and axis-restricted reward edits within $r _ { \mathrm { p h y s } }$ indexed by the selection criterion’s identified weak axis ∈ {knee, swing, com, none}. Each component is active in a different regime, so removing any single one leaves the others functional.

Design constraints behind the cadence and EMA. The scoring cadence of one call per 200k environment steps reflects two constraints: at our throughput (∼ 2500 FPS (environment steps per second) on a single RTX 4090), 200k steps span roughly 80 s of wall-clock, long enough to amortize the $1 { - } 3 \ \mathrm { s } \ A \mathrm { P I }$ round-trip without blocking the rollout queue; and at the 10M-step horizon this gives 50 score updates per run, enough for the EMA to settle and few enough to keep API spend below \$1. The EMA constant $\alpha { = } 0 . 2$ on r<sub>VLM</sub> keeps inter-call swings to ±0.2 on a stable training run, so an isolated rate-limit fallback (counted as a missed score, not a zero) does not drive a visible perturbation; smaller values over-smooth the early-training signal, where the policy is still trading falling-jerk for steady-state jerk and the score range is wide. The $\alpha _ { \mathrm { e f f } } { = } 0 . 2 \alpha$ early-training guard was added after an $\alpha { = } 0 . 8$ 8 pilot at 1M steps in which the VLM, asked to grade a humanoid that was still falling, returned near-zero scores that the policy chased into a sit.

## A.2 Auxiliary modules: SAGE and GMP

SAGE (Structured Agent for Goal Elicitation) is a three-stage LLM agent (intent elicitor → reward author → judge) that generates the initial $r _ { \mathrm { t a s k } }$ template later adjusted by the correction module; its outputs are evaluated separately as single-shot benchmarks and do not affect the headline numbers. GMP (Gaussian Motion Prior) is a per-step reward fit to eight motion-capture categories [36] (walk slow, . . ., stairs, $K { = } 2 { - } 6$ components, covariance $\scriptstyle \mathtt { t y p e } = " \mathtt { t u l l } ^ { \mathfrak { n } } ;$ sklearn.mixture.GaussianMixture [49]); it appears as one cell of the 5-way ablation (Section B.2).

The smoothness claims in Section 4.2 rest on physical measurements (joint acceleration, mechanical power, per-joint power), not on the VLM channel; the −43% power reduction is independent of the VLM aesthetic scores.

## B.7 Cost audit: VLM-in-loop spend

A co-scheduled 1M-step head-to-head comparing P2P against PhySA with the VLM scoring channel and stabilization patches only (no correction module; referred to as PhySA-VLM-only in Table 8; N=1, RTX 4090, seed 42) records the full API spend and system-level throughput. ## C Scope and Boundary Conditions

This section provides additional detail on the scope discussion in Section 5. Beyond the simulator and embodiment transfer experiments in Section 4.4, we evaluated push recovery: 50–200 N impulses applied to the torso [58, 59, 60, 61], 480 episodes per condition; no significant smoothness improvement emerged, consistent with the other out-of-distribution results. The full boundarycondition table is in Table 10.

The simulator-transfer failures are localized. On Isaac (different physics engine, same humanoid task specification): a 50–60× rescaling of the smoothness reward term raises it from 0.0016 to 0.094 per step, but the smoothness gap stays $\mathrm { a t - 1 . 0 0 \% , } p \mathrm { = } 0 . 9 1$ . We attribute this gap mainly to differences in physics integration between the two engines (Sec. 4.4) [44, 45, 62]. The 2.47× jointacceleration reduction of our main run therefore holds for MuJoCo Humanoid-v5 only. The G1 null result is in Appendix $\mathrm { G } ;$ that experiment changes both simulator and robot body simultaneously, so its outcome cannot be attributed to either factor alone.

What would extend the scope. The three boundary results are not symmetric in what they isolate. A G1 experiment inside MuJoCo, holding the simulator fixed and changing only the embodiment, would cleanly test portability across robot bodies without the confounding simulator change that plagues the current G1 result. A 10M-step Isaac experiment run at MuJoCo’s integration timestep would directly test whether the integrator alone accounts for the cross-simulator gap. A pushrecovery experiment at N=30 paired seeds would distinguish a true null from an underpowered effect. Each would extend the present work in a cleanly isolating direction.

## G G1 robot replication: null result and diagnosis

We run PhySA on the Unitree G1 (23-joint humanoid) at 10M paired steps, N=3 seeds, with a 38-setting hyperparameter search. One caveat before the results: unlike the Isaac experiment, which holds the task specification fixed and varies only the physics engine, the G1 experiment changes both the simulator and the robot body simultaneously. Any null result is therefore not diagnostic on its own; it cannot isolate which factor is responsible. The headline outcome is a non-significant +3.4% roughness gap in the wrong direction, which we trace partly to a hardcoded alive-bonus threshold carried over from Humanoid-v5.

The bug. Humanoid-v5’s alive bonus fires while z ≥ 1.1 m, matching its standing height; the same threshold does not work on G1, whose standing height is $z _ { \mathrm { G 1 } } ^ { * } = 0 . 7 9 3 \ \mathrm { m }$ . Before the fix the bonus never fires, so the policy receives a −10/step alive penalty for the entire rollout, inverting the reward signal. The combined scope table (Table 10) reports the before/after rows: alive-bonus firing goes from 0.0% to 94.1%, but the smoothness gap remains not statistically significant.

The fix and its reach. A one-line patch, STANDING BONUS Z = standing height × 0.769, calibrates the alive bonus per robot body (ratio 1.1/1.43 from Humanoid-v5, giving 0.610 m for G1). With the patch, the alive bonus fires correctly and training is no longer degenerate, but the smoothness improvement still does not replicate: the gap on G1 remains a non-significant −1.7%. The reward code carries over; the calibration constants do not. A 38-setting hyperparameter search on G1 (Table 11) recovered no gain along any of four dimensions.

Since actuator wear scales with q˙ and contact transients rather than static torque, PhySA delivers the same total torque with slower joint motion. Per-episode work W drops by only −13% because the +57% longer episode offsets part of the per-step saving, but the integrated result is still net favorable.

The +6% torque rise. PhySA does not reduce roughness by reducing actuator effort: the total actuator torque norm actually rises +6.0% against P2P (Tab. ${ \dot { 1 } } 2 , \quad$ paired t=+4.21, p=0.004). The per-joint action bandwidth tells the same story (Appendix B.2): mean per-joint action standard deviation is 0.144 vs. 0.353 for a vanilla baseline, and per-joint action standard deviation correlates with the joint acceleration roughness at r= + 0.949 across four module ablation conditions. The policy uses only a few joints actively, holding the rest approximately still. Smoothness is achieved by restricting which joints move and slowing down those joints’ movement, not by reducing actuator effort overall.
