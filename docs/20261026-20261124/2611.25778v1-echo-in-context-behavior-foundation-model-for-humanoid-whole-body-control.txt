# ECHO: In-Context Behavior foundation model for Humanoid Whole-Body Control

Xingyi Wang<sup>1,2,∗</sup>, Bin Li<sup>2,3,∗</sup>, Jiyuan Shi<sup>2</sup>, Chenyun Zhang<sup>2</sup>, Weiji Xie<sup>2,4</sup>, Xiangyu Miao<sup>2,4</sup>, Junsong Wu<sup>2,4</sup>, Wei Song<sup>1</sup>, Shiqiang Zhu<sup>1,†</sup>, Chenjia Bai<sup>2,5,†</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Zhejiang University, <sup>2</sup>Institute of Artificial Intelligence (TeleAI), China Telecom, <sup>3</sup>ShanghaiTech University, <sup>4</sup>Shanghai Jiao Tong University, <sup>5</sup>Gamma Robotics <sup>∗</sup>Equal contribution (co-first authors), <sup>†</sup>Corresponding authors

Humanoid behavior foundation models (BFMs) acquire diverse skills through large-scale motion imitation and massively parallel reinforcement learning, yet broad behavioral capability does not ensure precise execution across physical conditions. Without distinguishing the underlying dynamics, policies may favor conservative compromises over environment-specific precision. We introduce ECHO, an in-context BFM that turns interaction experience into environment-specific control, separating general behavioral capability from dynamics-specific execution. Mixed and Annealed Sampling first establishes a behavior prior in a single pretraining stage by balancing targeted practice with broad motion coverage. Building on this prior, Context Representation and Residual Adaptation learns structured dynamics representations from interaction histories to guide residual corrections to the frozen prior, enabling adaptation without test-time parameter updates. Extensive experiments show that ECHO outperforms all baseline methods across diverse motions and physical conditions. Moreover, deployment on the Unitree G1 demonstrates whole-body teleoperation spanning extreme acrobatics and terrain interaction, alongside payload adaptation in manipulation and whole-body control. ## 1 Introduction

For the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them.

Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics

Humanoid robots are expected to execute diverse whole-body behaviors accurately, responsively, and robustly in human-centric environments. Large-scale motion imitation and massively parallel reinforcement learning (RL) have advanced this goal through behavior foundation models (BFMs) (Luo et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2025; Qi et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026; RoboParty Lab Team, 2026; Liu et al., 2026a; Cheng et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2026b). Yet a broad behavior repertoire alone does not ensure precise execution across physical conditions: the same reference motion may require diferent control responses as payload, friction, or actuator dynamics vary. Without adequately distinguishing these dynamics, a policy trained across environments may learn conservative compromises that favor broad reliability over environment-specific tracking precision (Mehta et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2026b). The key challenge is therefore to enable a BFM to understand the underlying dynamics across environments and adapt to diferent physical conditions.

Recent work in robotic manipulation has demonstrated in-context adaptation through human demonstrations (Shah et al., 2025) or retrieved interaction experience (Chen et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2025a), without updating the model weights at test time. These advances motivate equipping humanoid BFMs with the ability to learn environmental dynamics from interaction context and tailor whole-body actions accordingly. As a BFM executes diverse behaviors, its actions and the resulting state transitions provide evidence about how the underlying dynamics shape its response. By encoding this evidence into structured dynamics representations, the model can learn control-relevant properties of the current environment and use them to guide subsequent actions. Interaction histories thus serve not merely as records of past execution, but as context for environment-specific action corrections. This allows a shared behavior prior to pursue the same objective with control responses tailored to diferent physical conditions, adapting its execution without test-time parameter updates.

To this end, we introduce ECHO, an in-context behavior foundation model for humanoid whole-body contro that separates general behavioral capability from dynamics-specific execution. Specifically, we first train a motion-tracking prior, ECHO-PRE, using Mixed and Annealed Sampling (MIAS). By balancing targeted practice with broad motion coverage, MIAS enables a single policy to jointly learn everyday motions, highly dynamic skills (Wang et al., 2026c), contact-rich floor behaviors, and terrain motions within one pretraining stage (Table 1). we introduce Context Representation and Residual Adaptation (CRA), which learns structured representations of the underlying environmental dynamics from the prior’s interaction histories. CRA combines multi-step dynamics prediction with physical neighborhood soft alignment to capture control-relevant physical information across behaviors. A context-conditioned residual policy then uses these representations to generate action corrections tailored to the learned dynamics, adapting the frozen prior’s execution.

We evaluate ECHO from four fundamental perspectives: (1) tracking fidelity, covering challenging highdynamic skills (Figure 1 (a)) and terrain motion (Figure 1 (b)); (2) in-context adaptation, assessing the benefit of interaction context under nominal and heavily randomized dynamics; (3) representation quality, examining whether representations distinguish physical environments across motions; and (4) real-world applicability, demonstrating whole-body teleoperation and payload adaptation on the Unitree G1 (Figure 1 (c)). Extensive experimental results show that MIAS strengthens the general behavior prior, while CRA learns physically informative context that supports more precise execution across varying dynamics without test-time parameter updates.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

• We introduce ECHO, an in-context behavior foundation model that separates general behavioral competence from environment-specific execution, enabling a single whole-body behavior prior to retain broad capabilities while adapting to varying physical dynamics.

• We develop two complementary components: Mixed and Annealed Sampling (MIAS) for acquiring a broad whole-body repertoire in a single pretraining stage, and Context Representation and Residual Adaptation (CRA) for turning interaction histories into physically grounded context that guides dynamics-specific corrections to the frozen prior.

• Extensive experiments show that ECHO improves tracking under substantial dynamics variation, learns context representations that remain physically informative across motions, and transfers to real-world whole-body teleoperation and payload adaptation.

## 2 Related Work

Behavior Foundation Models. Behavior foundation models learn reusable whole-body control priors. BFM Zeng et al. (2025) supports diverse control interfaces through generative behavioral representations, while BFM-Zero Li et al. (2026) uses unsupervised RL to unify motion-, goal-, and reward-conditioned control in a shared latent space. SONIC Luo et al. (2026), Scaling BFM Zeng et al. (2026), Humanoid-GPT Q et al. (2026), and HoloMotion-1 Chen et al. (2026b) explore complementary data, model, and training scaling strategies to improve tracking and generalization, while Mimiclite RoboParty Lab Team (2026) emphasizes eficient generalized motion tracking. Beyond tracking, related systems support reactive motion planning (Chen et al., 2026c), terrain-aware execution (Wang et al., 2026e), and environmental interaction (Cheng et al., 2026). HEFT Liu et al. (2026a) and Extreme-RGMT Ma et al. (2026) further extend capabilities to heavy-payload teleoperation and highly dynamic skills, respectively. Our ECHO complements these advances by combining broad skill acquisition via MIAS with dynamics-specific residual adaptation through CRA.

In-Context Learning and Dynamics Adaptation. Algorithm Distillation Laskin et al. (2022) and Prompt-DT Xu et al. (2022) demonstrate adaptation through learning histories and demonstration prompts, respectively, without test-time parameter updates. In robotics, earlier work combines universal policies with online system identification to adapt control to unknown dynamics (Yu et al., 2017). RMA Kumar et al. (2021) and dual-history policies Li et al. (2025) exploit proprioceptive histories for dynamics adaptation, while DWL Gu et al. (2024) uses denoising reconstruction for implicit system identification. LocoFormer Liu et al. (2025) leverages cross-trial Transformer memory to adapt locomotion to unseen morphologies and dynamics. For humanoid control, Any2Track Zhang et al. (2025b) combines world-model-based dynamics representations with layer-wise adapters in a frozen tracker, while HoRD Wang et al. (2026a) integrates history-conditioned RL with online distillation. FADA Xie et al. (2026) separates planning from inverse dynamics and adapts the latter using target-domain rollouts. In contrast, ECHO combines multi-step dynamics prediction with cross-motion physical neighborhood soft alignment to learn structured context representations, then trains an RL-based residual policy (Johannink et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2026d) conditioned on the learned in-context representation with both the encoder and behavior prior frozen.

## 3 Method

As illustrated in Figure 2, we first formulate In-Context BFM under unobserved environmental dynamics (Sec. 3.1). We then introduce MIAS to pretrain ECHO-PRE, balancing broad motion coverage with targeted practice on dificult segments (Sec. 3.2). Building on this frozen prior, CRA enables efective in-context adaptation by inferring environmental dynamics from interaction history and translating the resulting context into action corrections tailored to the current environment (Sec. 3.3).

## 3.1 Problem Formulation

We formulate humanoid control under varying physical conditions as a family of Markov decision processes (MDPs) indexed by an environmental configuration $c \in { \mathcal { C } } \colon$

$$
\mathcal {M} _ {c} = \langle \mathcal {S}, \mathcal {A}, \mathcal {T} _ {c}, \mathcal {R}, \gamma \rangle ,\tag{1}
$$

where c specifies properties such as mass distribution, friction, and payload, and $\mathcal { T } _ { c }$ is the corresponding transition dynamics. The configuration c is not directly observable at deployment.

Following prior work (Zeng et al., 2026), we use humanoid whole-body motion tracking as the proxy task for BFM pretraining. A conventional domain-randomized BFM $\pi ( a _ { t } \mid s _ { t } ^ { p } , m _ { t } )$ generates actions $a _ { t }$ from proprioceptive states $s _ { t } ^ { p }$ and reference-motion goals $m _ { t }$ using a policy shared across heterogeneous dynamics, which can compromise environment-specific performance. To this end, we introduce In-Context BFM, which learns the underlying environmental dynamics from interaction context $\mathcal { H } _ { t }$ and enables adaptive control under varying environmental configurations c.

We formally define the interaction context for humanoid control as the history of proprioceptive states and actions:

$$
\mathcal {H} _ {t} \triangleq \left(s _ {0} ^ {p}, a _ {0}, s _ {1} ^ {p}, a _ {1}, \ldots , s _ {t - 1} ^ {p}, a _ {t - 1}, s _ {t} ^ {p}\right).\tag{2}
$$

This history captures the humanoid’s responses to past actions and thus provides evidence about the environmental configuration c governing its dynamics. We learn a context encoder $E _ { \varphi }$ to extract a latent representation of the environmental configuration c from interaction history $\mathcal { H } _ { t } \mathrm { : }$

$$
z _ {t} = E _ {\varphi} (\mathcal {H} _ {t}) \approx z _ {c} \triangleq g (c),\tag{3}
$$

Here, $g : { \mathcal { C } }  { \mathcal { Z } }$ maps each environmental configuration to a dynamics-relevant latent representation $z _ { c } ,$ and $z _ { t }$ denotes its context-dependent estimate. The resulting In-Context BFM acts according to $\pi ( a _ { t } \mid s _ { t } ^ { p } , m _ { t } , z _ { t } )$ ， enabling adaptation without direct access to privileged parameters.

With the learned encoder $E _ { \varphi }$ held fixed, our objective is to maximize the expected discounted return over a distribution $p ( c )$ of environmental configurations:

$$
\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\substack{c\sim p(c)\\ \tau \sim p_{\pi}(\tau |c)}}\left[\sum_{t = 0}^{T - 1}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\right],\tag{4}
$$

where $r _ { t }$ denotes the motion-tracking reward and $p _ { \pi } ( \tau \mid c )$ is the distribution of length-T rollouts induced by the In-Context BFM π under $\mathcal { T } _ { c }$

## 3.2 Learning a General Behavior Prior via MIAS

In-context adaptation across diverse whole-body behaviors requires a suficiently general behavior prior. We first train the behavior prior $\pi _ { \boldsymbol { \theta } } .$ , termed ECHO-PRE, in a single stage with MIAS, using motion tracking as the proxy task. MIAS combines failure rewind, uniform exploration, and failure-adaptive sampling to balance broad motion coverage with targeted practice on dificult segments, and gradually reduces sampling concentration to consolidate the learned repertoire.

Adaptive weight. We partition reference motions into temporal bins and estimate their dificulty using empirical failure rates $f _ { i }$ (Luo et al., 2026; Liao et al., 2026). We prioritize bins with observed failures through a length-weighted, temperature-scaled distribution:

$$
\widetilde {p} _ {i} \propto \ell_ {i} \exp (f _ {i} / T) \mathbf {1} \{f _ {i} > 0 \},\tag{5}
$$

where $\ell _ { i }$ is the number of valid frames in bin i and $T > 0$ controls sampling sharpness. To prevent excessive concentration on individual segments or motions, we cap and redistribute probability mass at both levels, yielding the adaptive distribution $p ^ { \mathrm { { A } } }$ . Counting rules, the no-failure fallback, and probability caps are detailed in Appendix $\mathrm { A } .$

Mixed and annealed sampling. Dificult segments warrant targeted practice without indefinitely restricting motion coverage. MIAS therefore mixes three sampling branches and progressively relaxes the concentration of the adaptive branch.

Mixed sampling. Let $\mathcal { P } _ { \mathrm { R } } , \mathcal { P } _ { \mathrm { U } }$ , and $\mathcal { P } _ { \mathrm { A } }$ denote the distributions over bins induced by rewind, uniform, and adaptive sampling, respectively. We sample bins from the failure-conditioned mixture:

$$
\mathcal {P} _ {d} = d \rho_ {\mathrm{R}} \mathcal {P} _ {\mathrm{R}} + (1 - d \rho_ {\mathrm{R}}) \big [ \rho_ {\mathrm{U}} \mathcal {P} _ {\mathrm{U}} + (1 - \rho_ {\mathrm{U}}) \mathcal {P} _ {\mathrm{A}} \big ],\tag{6}
$$

where $d \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ indicates whether the preceding trajectory ended in a tracking failure, and $\rho _ { \mathrm { R } } , \rho _ { \mathrm { U } } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ specify the rewind probability conditional on failure and the uniform probability conditional on not rewinding, respectively. Rewind restarts the failed motion at the beginning of its failure bin. Uniform and adaptive sampling select a bin uniformly from $\mathcal { T }$ or according to $p ^ { \mathrm { { A } } }$ , respectively, and then sample a valid frame uniformly within that bin.

Annealed concentration. To gradually broaden practice, we increase the sampling temperature $T$ and decrease the probability cap $C$ using a shared annealing function:

$$
\theta_ {k} = \mathcal {A} (k; \theta_ {\mathrm{start}}, \theta_ {\mathrm{end}}, K _ {\theta}), \qquad \theta \in \{T, C \},\tag{7}
$$

where k is the training iterations and $\mathcal { A }$ performs cosine interpolation with independent horizons $K _ { T }$ and $K _ { C }$ as specified in Appendix A.

## 3.3 In-Context Adaptation via CRA

CRA first learns a context encoder from the prior’s rollouts using dynamics prediction and physically grounded representation objectives, then freezes the context encoder and trains a residual policy to translate context into action corrections.

Interaction context. Let $s _ { t } ^ { p }$ denote noisy proprioception and $a _ { t }$ the applied action. At decision time $t ,$ the context $\mathcal { H } _ { t }$ contains only completed, valid transitions preceding that decision. We instantiate the context with short- and long-term histories:

$$
\mathcal {H} _ {t} = (\mathcal {S} _ {t}, \mathcal {M} _ {t}), \qquad z _ {t} = E _ {\varphi} (\mathcal {H} _ {t}),\tag{8}
$$

The short-term history $S _ { t }$ preserves fine-grained recent responses, while the long-term memory $\mathcal { M } _ { t }$ provides complementary dynamics evidence from earlier interactions across motions. Complete chunks are retained in $\mathcal { M } _ { t }$ across reference-motion changes and episode resets within the same physical session, whereas $S _ { t }$ is cleared at these boundaries. The physical context remains fixed within a session, and a new session clears all memory.

Predictive in-context dynamics. To encourage the context latent to encode control-relevant dynamics, we jointly train $E _ { \varphi }$ and a forward predictor $D _ { \psi }$ on rollouts collected by the frozen tracker. Let $y _ { t }$ contain the privileged robot state, foot kinematics, contact forces, and binary contact indicators, and let $a _ { t }$ denote the applied action. Conditioned on the context $z _ { t }$ , the predictor estimates future states over a horizon of $K _ { \mathrm { p } }$ steps:

$$
\hat {y} _ {t + k} = D _ {\psi} (\mathcal {H} _ {t + k - 1}, a _ {t + k - 1}; z _ {t}),\tag{9}
$$

where $\mathscr { H } _ { t + k - 1 }$ denotes the predictor’s rolling input history, initialized from the observed trajectory and updated with recorded states or model predictions during training. The context $z _ { t }$ is held fixed throughout the prediction horizon, and all predictions use the recorded applied target sequence. We minimize the multi-step prediction objective:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{pred}} = \mathbb {E} _ {\underset {\mathbf {H} \sim \nu (\cdot | \tau)} {\tau \sim \mathcal {D}}} \left[ \frac {1}{K _ {\mathrm{p}}} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K _ {\mathrm{p}}} \ell_ {\mathrm{pred}} (\hat {y} _ {t + k}, y _ {t + k}) \right],\tag{10}
$$

where $\mathcal { D }$ is the rollout-segment distribution and $\nu ( \cdot \mid \tau )$ is the weighted distribution over rolling-history sequences $\mathbf { H } = ( \mathcal { H } _ { t } , \dots , \mathcal { H } _ { t + K _ { \mathrm { p } } - 1 } )$ .

Physically grounded context geometry. We complement predictive supervision with physical neighborhood soft alignment to preserve neighborhoods among physical conditions. Specifically, we align cross-motion context neighborhoods P with physical-parameter neighborhoods $Q$ :

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{soft}} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} D _ {\mathrm{KL}} (Q _ {i:} \parallel P _ {i:}),\tag{11}
$$

where n is the number of eligible randomized systems. More details are in Appendix A.

Then, we jointly optimize the context encoder and forward predictor with

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{ctx}} = \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{pred}} + \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{soft}}.\tag{12}
$$

Privileged states and physical parameters are used only during training.

In-context residual adaptation. The learned context representation provides evidence about environmental dynamics, which the residual policy translates into environment-specific action corrections. We freeze both the context encoder $E _ { \varphi }$ and $\operatorname { E C H O - P R E } \pi _ { \theta }$ and train a residual actor $\pi _ { \Delta }$ . The predictor is not used in this stage or at deployment. The final action combines the prior’s command $a _ { t } ^ { \theta }$ with a context-conditioned correction $a _ { t } ^ { \Delta }$

$$
a _ {t} ^ {\theta} = \pi_ {\theta} (s _ {t} ^ {p}), \qquad a _ {t} ^ {\Delta} = \pi_ {\Delta} (s _ {t} ^ {p}, m _ {t}, z _ {t}, a _ {t} ^ {\theta}), \qquad a _ {t} = a _ {t} ^ {\theta} + a _ {t} ^ {\Delta},\tag{13}
$$

where $a _ { t }$ is the final applied action. Each completed valid transition updates the interaction history through Eq. equation 8, closing the interaction–context–control loop.

## 4 Experiments

## 4.1 Set up

We use the Unitree G1 for both training and evaluation. We evaluate ECHO extensively in simulation, including tracking fidelity (Section 4.2), in-context adaptation (Section 4.3), and in-context representation analysis (Section 4.4). We further validate ECHO on the real robot in Section 4.5.

To enable fair comparison with existing BFMs while evaluating in-context ability under broader dynamics variations, we design two physical randomization settings. Necessary Domain Randomization (NDR) contains the minimum set of DR required for sim-to-real transfer and is designed to remain close to the settings commonly adopted by existing baselines. We pretrain ECHO-PRE only under NDR to acquire the general behavior prior.

We further introduce Heavy Domain Randomization (HDR), which extends NDR with substantially larger dynamics variations, including additional payloads on the wrists and lower legs. Starting from the same frozen ECHO-PRE backbone, we train CRA under NDR and HDR, resulting in ECHO-N and ECHO-H, respectively. The detailed randomization ranges of Nominal environment, NDR and HDR are summarized in Table 7.

We train ECHO using PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) in Mjlab (Zakka et al., 2026) for behavior pretraining and in-context adaptation, leveraging more than 1,200 hours of filtered high-quality motion data. The composition of the training dataset is illustrated in Figure 5.

## 4.2 Evaluation of Motion Tracking Fidelity

We evaluate the contributions of MIAS and CRA to motion tracking. To assess MIAS, we compare ECHO-PRE with HEFT (Liu et al., 2026a), SONIC v1.1 (Luo et al., 2026), and ECHO-PRE w/o MIAS, which uses uniform motion sampling. To assess CRA, we compare ECHO-N against its pretrained ECHO-PRE backbone. Our evaluation covers two complementary benchmarks: AMASS-2000-Random tests generalization to everyday motions from AMASS, which is excluded from ECHO’s training data, while BONES-SEED-100-HighDynamic tests challenging motions within the training distribution. All evaluations use nominal dynamics without domain randomization. We report success rate (SR) and motion-tracking errors, with dataset construction and metric definitions detailed in Appendix C.

As shown in Table 2, MIAS improves tracking on both benchmarks relative to uniform sampling, and ECHO-PRE compares favorably with the external baselines. CRA further reduces tracking errors, showing that interaction context improves execution precision even under nominal dynamics.

## 4.3 Evaluating the ability of using context

In this section, we compare ECHO with representative adaptation methods for legged robot control. We consider three baselines. RMA (Kumar et al., 2021) learns a privileged teacher conditioned on ground-truth dynamics parameters and subsequently distills its adaptation capability into a student policy; we report both the RMA-Teacher and RMA-Student for comparison. Any2Track Zhang et al. (2025b) learns a latent dynamics representation from interaction history and regularizes the representation with a world-model prediction objective. We further design a Vanilla baseline, which uses the same residual-policy architecture but receives only the observations available to ECHO-PRE, without any latent dynamics representation.

For a fair comparison, all methods share the same frozen ECHO-PRE backbone and residual-policy architecture, and are trained under the same HDR setting. We evaluate all methods on AMASS-2000-Random under both the nominal environment and the HDR environment. The evaluation metrics are identical to those used in Section 4.2.

As shown in Table 3, ECHO-H achieves performance comparable to RMA-Teacher in the nominal environment, without requiring privileged dynamics parameters at inference. Under HDR, ECHO-H achieves the best results on four of the five metrics, improving SR by 0.95 percentage points and reducing global MPJPE by 3.0% relative to RMA-Teacher. ECHO-H also outperforms RMA-Student across all HDR metrics and consistently improves over Vanilla in both environments. In contrast, the Any2Track baseline provides no consistent gains over Vanilla, suggesting that introducing a history-derived latent alone does not guarantee efective adaptation in this setting. Together, these results suggest that ECHO learns control-relevant representations of environmental dynamics that the residual policy can efectively exploit to improve tracking under substantia dynamics variation.

## 4.4 Analyzing the learned context representations

We examine whether CRA learns environment representations that remain consistent across motions while retaining physically meaningful information. We collect trajectories by rolling out ECHO-PRE across 40 LAFAN motions in 1,024 HDR environments and evaluate all variants on 325,193 shared valid history windows. We evaluate environment identification, cross-motion retrieval, separation, and linear readout of payload mass and friction. See Appendix D for details.

## 4.5 Real-world experiments

## 5 Conclusion

We presented ECHO, an in-context behavior foundation model that separates general behavioral capability from dynamics-specific execution. MIAS acquires a broad whole-body repertoire, while CRA learns physical dynamics from interaction context to guide residual corrections to the frozen behavior prior. Experiments and real-world deployment on the Unitree G1 demonstrate improved tracking, whole-body teleoperation, and adaptation to added payloads. These results show that interaction context enables a shared behavior prior to adapt to its physical environment, combining behavioral breadth with dynamics-specific precision without test-time parameter updates.

## A Details of Methods

Adaptive sampling. Let I denote the set of valid temporal bins, where $i = ( m , b )$ identifies bin b of motion m. For each bin, we maintain completed-visit and failure counts, $V _ { i }$ and $F _ { i }$ , and estimate its failure rate as $f _ { i } = F _ { i } / V _ { i }$ for $V _ { i } > 0$ . A visit is completed when a trajectory leaves the bin or terminates. We compute the preliminary sampling distribution as

$$
s _ {i} = \ell_ {i} \exp \left(\frac {f _ {i} - f _ {\max}}{T}\right) \mathbf {1} \{f _ {i} > 0 \},
$$

$$
\widetilde {p} _ {i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {s _ {i}}{\sum_ {j \in \mathcal {I}} s _ {j}}, & \sum_ {j \in \mathcal {I}} s _ {j} > 0, \\ \frac {\ell_ {i}}{\sum_ {j \in \mathcal {I}} \ell_ {j}}, & \text {otherwise}, \end{array} \right.\tag{14}
$$

where $f _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } = \operatorname* { m a x } _ { j \in \mathcal { T } } f _ { j }$ is subtracted for numerical stability. Length weighting accounts for partially filled bins and provides a length-proportional fallback when no failures have been observed. We then cap and redistribute probability mass to obtain $p ^ { \mathrm { { A } } }$ satisfying

$$
\sum_ {i \in \mathcal {I}} p _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{A}} = 1, \qquad 0 \leq p _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{A}} \leq \frac {C}{N}, \qquad \sum_ {i \in \mathcal {I} _ {m}} p _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{A}} \leq \frac {C}{M} \quad \forall m,\tag{15}
$$

where $N = | \mathcal { T } |$ , M is the number of motions, and $\mathcal { I } _ { m }$ contains the bins of motion $m$ . The multiplier $C \geq 1$ is chosen so that the constraints are jointly feasible, and redistribution preserves relative weights wherever capacity permits.

Mixed sampling. In practice, we set $\rho _ { \mathrm { R } } = 1 / 3$ and $\rho _ { \mathrm { U } } = 1 / 2$ in Eq. equation 6, yielding

$$
\mathcal {P} _ {d} = \frac {d}{3} \mathcal {P} _ {\mathrm{R}} + \frac {3 - d}{6} \big (\mathcal {P} _ {\mathrm{U}} + \mathcal {P} _ {\mathrm{A}} \big).\tag{16}
$$

After a tracking failure $( d = 1 )$ , all three components receive equal weight $1 / 3$ . After a non-failure timeout or normal motion completion $( d = 0 )$ , rewind is disabled, and uniform and adaptive sampling each receive weight $1 / 2$ . A tracking failure coinciding with a timeout is assigned $d = 1$

The rewind component $\mathcal { P } _ { \mathrm { R } }$ selects the failure bin of the preceding motion and restarts at the beginning of that bin. For each valid bin $i \in \mathcal { T } ,$ , the uniform and adaptive components satisfy $\mathcal { P } _ { \mathrm { U } } ( i ) = 1 / | \mathcal { I } |$ and $\mathcal { P } _ { \mathrm { A } } ( i ) = p _ { i } ^ { \mathrm { A } }$ respectively. Both components then sample a valid frame τ uniformly within the selected bin. Uniform sampling initializes at τ, whereas adaptive sampling additionally applies a random backward ofset to include the lead-up to dificult segments:

$$
t _ {0} = \max \{0, \tau - u \}, \quad u \sim \operatorname{Uniform} \{0, \dots , W - 1 \},\tag{17}
$$

where $\tau$ and $t _ { 0 }$ are frame indices within the selected motion, and $W$ is a positive integer specifying the backward-ofset window in frames.

Annealing schedule. The annealing function in Eq. equation 7 interpolates from an initial value a to a final value b over a horizon of K iterations:

$$
\mathcal {A} (k; a, b, K) = a + \frac {b - a}{2} \left[ 1 - \cos (\pi \min \{k / K, 1 \}) \right],\tag{18}
$$

where $k \geq 0$ and $K > 0$ . For the sampling temperature, we use $( a , b , K ) = ( T _ { \mathrm { s t a r t } } , T _ { \mathrm { e n d } } , K _ { T } )$ , with $T _ { \mathrm { e n d } } >$ $T _ { \mathrm { s t a r t } } > 0$ . For the cap multiplier, we use $( a , b , K ) = ( C _ { \mathrm { s t a r t } } , C _ { \mathrm { e n d } } , K _ { C } )$ , with $C _ { \mathrm { s t a r t } } > C _ { \mathrm { e n d } } \geq 1$ . Each schedule reaches its final value at the corresponding horizon and remains constant thereafter. All scheduled cap values are chosen to keep the constraints in Eq. equation 15 jointly feasible.

Prediction loss. The per-step prediction loss is

$$
\ell_ {\mathrm{pred}} (\hat {y}, y) = \sum_ {g \in \mathcal {G}} \alpha_ {g} \operatorname{Huber} (e _ {g} (\hat {y}, y)) + \alpha_ {\mathrm{bin}} \operatorname{BCE} (\hat {y} ^ {\mathrm{bin}}, y ^ {\mathrm{bin}}).\tag{19}
$$

Here, $\mathcal { G }$ comprises root position and orientation, linear and angular velocity, joint position and velocity, foot kinematics, and contact force. Each $e _ { g }$ is a scaled component-wise error, with rotation-vector errors for orientation and Huber losses (Huber, 1992) averaged over coordinates. The binary cross entropy (Bengio et al., 2017) term supervises contact occurrence.

Physical neighborhood soft alignment. For n eligible randomized systems in a minibatch, let $\xi _ { i }$ denote the range-normalized, group-balanced physical-parameter vector of system $i ,$ with $d _ { i j } = \lVert { \boldsymbol { \xi } } _ { i } - { \boldsymbol { \xi } } _ { j } \rVert _ { 2 }$ . Let $\bar { z } _ { i }$ and $\bar { z } _ { i } ^ { \prime }$ denote the mean context embeddings from its current and archived histories, respectively. A current history contains the completed state–action–next-state transitions available at the corresponding training sample’s time; “current” is relative to that sample and does not imply recently collected data. An archived history is an earlier, previously stored interaction window from the same environment instance and the same physica session, during which the physical parameters remain unchanged. The paired current and archived windows correspond to diferent reference motions and do not overlap in time. Only systems with an eligible history pair are included in the neighborhood loss. Our neighborhood-distribution matching is related to probabilistic knowledge transfer (Passalis and Tefas, 2018), but uses physical-parameter distances to supervise cross-motion context similarities. We construct the physical and latent neighborhood distributions as

$$
\begin{array}{l} {Q _ {i j} = \mathrm{softmax} _ {j} \left(- (\log 2) \left(\frac {d _ {i j}}{h _ {c}}\right) ^ {2}\right),} \\ {P _ {i j} = \mathrm{softmax} _ {j} \left(\frac {\bar {z} _ {i} ^ {\top} \bar {z} _ {j} ^ {\prime}}{\tau_ {z}}\right),} \end{array}\tag{20}
$$

where softmax normalizes each row over all n systems, $h _ { c } > 0$ controls the physical-neighborhood bandwidth, and $\tau _ { z } > 0$ is the latent similarity temperature. The neighborhood loss in Eq. equation 11 is the average row-wise KL divergence:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{soft}} = \frac {1}{n} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {i j} \log \frac {Q _ {i j}}{P _ {i j}}.\tag{21}
$$

Matching neighborhoods across diferent motions encourages the learned relationships to reflect physical conditions rather than motion identity.

Joint optimization. We jointly optimize the context encoder $E _ { \varphi }$ and forward predictor $D _ { \psi }$ using the total objective in $\operatorname { E q . }$ equation 12. Each loss term is averaged over its eligible samples. Privileged states and physical parameters are used only during training; the context encoder receives interaction history alone.

## C EVALUATION OF MOTION TRACKING FIDELITY

Benchmark construction. We construct two benchmarks to assess complementary aspects of motion tracking. AMASS-2000-Random contains 2,000 randomly sampled motions from AMASS (Mahmood et al., 2019). Since AMASS is excluded from ECHO’s training data, this benchmark evaluates ECHO’s out-of-distribution generalization to diverse everyday behaviors. BONES-SEED-100-HighDynamic contains 100 highly dynamic motions sampled from BONES-SEED (Studio): 33 ground-interaction motions, 33 extreme motions, and 34 dance motions. These motions belong to the training distribution and assess the policy’s mastery of challenging behaviors.

Evaluation metrics and simulator. Following Luo et al. (2023, 2026), we report success rate (SR) and four discrepancy metrics between executed and reference trajectories: global MPJPE $\left( E _ { \mathrm { g - M P J P E } } , \ \mathrm { m m } \right)$ , rootrelative MPJPE $( E _ { \mathrm { M P J P E } } , \mathrm { m m } )$ , acceleration error $( E _ { \mathrm { a c c } } , \mathrm { m m } / \mathrm { f r a m e ^ { 2 } } )$ , and velocity error $( E _ { \mathrm { v e l } } , \mathrm { m m / f r a m e } )$ Higher SR and lower tracking errors indicate better performance. All policies are evaluated using the CPU implementation of MuJoCo (Todorov et al., 2012) under nominal dynamics, without domain randomization during evaluation.

## D Context Representation Evaluation

Sampling and splits. Each environment–motion pair contributes up to eight independent sessions, with one latent extracted after 500 warm-up steps. ECHO variants are evaluated after 5,000 training iterations; the default encoder produces a 64-dimensional context from at most 350 completed interactions. Following grouped evaluation (Roberts et al., 2017), we split motions into five folds, keeping each motion’s windows together. For classification, three folds fit the probe and one selects hyperparameters; the probe is then refitted on all four non-test folds. Retrieval uses these four folds as the gallery. Physical readout additionally splits environments into five folds, yielding 25 tests that exclude both test environments and test motions from probe fitting and validation. Preprocessing uses training data only. LAFAN motions belong to the encoder’s pretraining corpus.

Metrics. We report frozen-encoder linear classification accuracy (Chen et al., 2020), full-gallery mAP (Manning et al., 2008), and the Silhouette score (Rousseeuw, 1987) using environment identities. Retrieval treats windows from the same environment as positives; both retrieval and Silhouette use Euclidean distances between original latent vectors without additional normalization. Ridge probes (Hoerl and Kennard, 1970) predict payload mass and friction. Probe scores pool out-of-fold predictions across valid windows; mass $R ^ { 2 }$ averages the four limb-wise scores, while friction $R ^ { 2 }$ evaluates a single coeficient.

Visualization. Both panels use the same windows and t-SNE settings: perplexity 5, PCA initialization and 1,500 iterations. The Any2Track comparison is qualitative because inputs, capacity, history length, and training setup difer. To further evaluate out-of-distribution generalization, we visualize the learned context representations on the AMASS dataset using t-SNE.

## E Real world experiments

Broad capability versus contextual adaptation. Figure 6 demonstrates that ECHO-PRE supports a broad repertoire of whole-body behaviors with a single pretrained policy, ranging from ground and terrain interactions to highly dynamic aerial motions. The robot seamlessly handles substantial variations in posture, support conditions, and motion dynamics, including floor-level transitions, platform traversal, and aerial flips, highlighting the breadth and reusability of the learned behavioral capabilities.

Figure 7 further demonstrates that ECHO can adapt these capabilities to changing physical contexts through in-context interaction. Under additional wrist and limb payloads, the robot maintains precise manipulation and diverse whole-body behaviors, including balance, squatting, sitting, and posture transitions. Crucially, when the payload is attached or removed, ECHO adjusts its control response based on the resulting interaction history without updating model parameters at test time. This shows that ECHO complements broad pretrained capabilities with context-dependent dynamics adaptation, enabling precise behavior under previously unseen physical conditions.

## F Limitations

Despite its broad whole-body capabilities and efective in-context dynamics adaptation, ECHO has severa limitations. First, context learning relies on simulated rollouts and privileged supervision, and generalization to physical efects beyond the training randomization requires further evaluation. Second, our memory mechanism assumes fixed physical parameters within each session and clears the history at session boundaries. Adaptation to unannounced changes within a session, where accumulated context may become outdated, remains insuficiently explored. Finally, our evaluation focuses on the Unitree G1 and reference-conditioned whole-body control; transfer across embodiments and integration with autonomous task planning remain untested. We leave broader physical generalization, change-aware context memory, and extensions across embodiments and tasks to future work.
