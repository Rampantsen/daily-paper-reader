# FORGE: Feasible Online Reduced-Order Reference Generation and Whole-Body Execution for Agile Humanoid Locomotion

Adrian B. Ghansah<sup>1</sup>, Yujin An<sup>2</sup>, Jeeseop Kim<sup>3</sup>, Soon-Jo Chung<sup>1</sup>

Abstract— This paper presents a two-stage learning framework for versatile and agile humanoid locomotion that couples online reduced-order model (ROM) reference generation with whole-body tracking control. In the first stage, we train a command-conditioned whole-body reference generator using single rigid body dynamics combined with full humanoid kinematics. In the second stage, the frozen ROM policy is rolled out online to generate references for training a whole-body tracking policy on the full-order dynamics model. Learning a kinodynamically consistent reference generator in the simplified ROM space enables us to generate references over a broad command space and over multiple locomotion strategies using a single unified framework. We demonstrate the approach on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, achieving longitudinal velocities from −2.0 m/s to 2.8 m/s, lateral velocities up to ±1.0 m/s, angular velocities up to ±3.0 rad/s, one-legged hopping, and continuous transitions across the commands, all using one unified reference policy and one whole-body tracking policy.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Recent advances have enabled humanoid robots to perform individual dynamic behaviors with impressive reliability. However, unifying these behaviors within a single control framework that is both agile and versatile remains challenging due to the high-dimensional, underactuated, and nonlinear hybrid dynamics of humanoids.

Model-based controllers often address this challenge by decomposing locomotion into two subproblems: generating reference trajectories and whole-body tracking. For reference generation, full-order model (FOM) trajectory optimization employs the complete dynamics, yielding trajectories that are dynamically consistent with the full-order system. However, using the complete dynamics may make the optimization problem computationally expensive [1]–[3]. Many frameworks therefore perform trajectory optimization offline or update the planner at a lower rate than the tracking controller [1], [4], which may limit their applicability in online settings.

Reduced-order models (ROMs) offer an alternative approach in which the robot is represented by a simplified model capturing its dominant dynamics. Because this lowdimensional representation involves fewer states and constraints, the associated optimization is less expensive, enabling ROM-based planners to generate references online, making them attractive for real-time planning [5], [6]. However, a trajectory that is feasible for the ROM may not be feasible for the full-order system, as the low-dimensional representation does not account for full-body kinematic constraints and limb dynamics [6], [7].

Learning-based controllers provide an alternative approach for handling the complex dynamics of humanoid locomotion. Rather than relying on an explicit trajectory optimizer or a ROM planner to compute control actions, reinforcement learning (RL) trains policies that map observations directly to actions by maximizing a reward function. Massively parallel simulation allows these policies to accumulate large amounts of experience through training, and end-to-end policies trained in this manner have demonstrated remarkable robustness and task performance [8]–[10]. Locomotion frameworks that learn distinct skills via separately trained policies have been explored [11], yet, training a single policy that covers multiple tasks or broad command ranges remains difficult.

Beyond task rewards alone, a complementary strategy is to supply reference trajectories during training, providing structured objectives for RL training [12], [13]. Wholebody tracking policies trained in this way have demonstrated highly agile behaviors [13]–[15]. However, this agility depends on high-quality reference trajectories [13]–[15], which remain challenging to obtain over a broad command space, let alone generate online [12], [13].

This work addresses the challenge of generating and tracking high-quality whole-body reference trajectories online for versatile and agile humanoid locomotion over broad command spaces. To this end, we propose a two-stage framework. In the first stage, we train a command-conditioned whole-body reference generator based on single rigid body (SRB) dynamics combined with full-order humanoid kinematics. In the second stage, we integrate this reference generator into the training of a whole-body tracking policy.

We show that the proposed framework generates highquality whole-body reference trajectories over a broad command space and leverages them to train a whole-body tracking policy. We further validate the framework on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Our contributions are:

• A command-conditioned whole-body reference generator that combine SRB dynamics with full-order humanoid kinematics to produce dynamically informed references over a broad command space.

• A two-stage training framework that integrates the online reference generator into the training of a wholebody tracking policy.

• A hardware demonstration of the proposed framework, achieving longitudinal velocities from $- 2 . 0 \mathrm { ~ m } / \mathrm { s }$ to 2.8 m/s, lateral velocities up to ±1.0 m/s, angular velocities up to ±3.0 rad/s, one-legged hopping, and continuous transitions across the commands.

## II. RELATED WORK

## A. Reduced-Order Models for Humanoid Locomotion

ROMs are attractive for humanoid locomotion control because they provide simplified representations of the complex full-order humanoid dynamics. These models are commonly employed to plan foothold locations and to generate the ground reaction forces and moments (GRFMs) required to stabilize the robot via real-time control. One of the canonical ROMs is the Linear Inverted Pendulum (LIP) model [16], [17], which represents the robot as a point-mass moving in the horizontal plane at a fixed center of mass (COM) height, rotating about a passive pivot point at the foot. Related ROMs capture additional features of different locomotion behaviors: the Hybrid Linear Inverted Pendulum (HLIP) model [6] incorporates the hybrid nature of legged locomotion, the Angular Linear Inverted Pendulum (ALIP) model [18], [19] accounts for angular momentum about the stance foot, and the Spring-Loaded Inverted Pendulum (SLIP) model [20] permits variation in vertical velocity. These pendulum models, however, are primarily concerned with the regulation of step length and step timing; centroidal models instead incorporate both linear and angular momentum dynamics, providing a richer representation of the robot system. One such model is the SRB model [21], which is commonly coupled with Raibert-style footstep planners [22], and to which model predictive control (MPC) is often applied to compute GRFMs that are then mapped to feedforward torques through the contact Jacobian [7], [21], [23].

ROMs have also been employed to guide the training of RL policies, often through objectives derived from ROM outputs, such as step-length targets. In [24], the LIP model was used to generate rewards based on desired footstep targets, whereas in [25], [26], the ALIP model was used to generate step-length rewards that regulate the angular momentum for traversing constrained footholds. The HLIP model was employed in [27] to generate step-length rewards for achieving multigait locomotion and high-speed running. Common to these works is that the ROMs guide the policy during training rather than generating whole-body reference trajectories for the robot to track. Offline-generated ROM trajectories have also been explored for policy training. In [28], gait libraries based on the SLIP model were generated offline via trajectory optimization, and the resulting references were used during training to provide COM velocity and foot location rewards. Similarly, in [29], trajectory libraries based on the SRB model were generated offline via optimization for various dynamic maneuvers and were subsequently used as expert references during policy training to generate COM linear velocity, angular momentum, and foot position rewards. The direction most closely related to the proposed work is [30], in which LIP-based whole-body references are generated online. However, whereas [30] focuses on wholebody reference generation for various terrain setups, our work proposes a two-stage framework that broadens the command space, enabling whole-body locomotion that ranges from agile maneuvers to high-speed running while also accommodating seamless transitions between commands.

## B. Humanoid Reference Generation

In humanoid locomotion, providing motion references, such as kinematic and joint-torque trajectories, as a prior for imitation-based RL has proven crucial for robust policy learning of online tracking controllers. Model-based trajectory optimization serves as a foundation of such reference generation by formulating motion synthesis as a constrained optimization problem that enforces physically grounded dynamics to produce high-quality, dynamically feasible trajectories. These optimization-based approaches range from formulations built on ROMs [29] to those built on full-order-dynamics [31]. However, classical optimization pipelines typically rely on precomputed gait libraries and separate per-task policies, which limits versatility and hinders transitions between behaviors. Human motion data offers an alternative source for the reference generation, yet retargeting motions from human to robot morphologies often yields kinematically or dynamically infeasible references. Consequently, obtaining high-quality references for diverse downstream tasks remains an important open problem [12], [13], [15].

## C. Whole-Body Motion Tracking

Whole-body motion tracking for humanoids aims to transform kinematic references into dynamically feasible motions, enabling humanoids to operate in real environments through imitation-based RL. The control policy is trained to minimize the discrepancy between the reference motion and the executed motion, which allows the humanoid to reproduce diverse behaviors while satisfying physical constraints. Reference motions are typically drawn from large-scale human datasets. This paradigm was first demonstrated for humanoid character animation in physical simulation [32], [33], and has since been extended to teach real humanoid robots skills ranging from various locomotion behaviors [12] to wholebody scene interactions [34]. Real-time human imitation via teleoperation further broadens deployment to a wide range of environments [35]–[37]. To move beyond skills confined to fixed environments and toward real-time control in dynamic settings, a generator–tracker hierarchy has recently been proposed [38]–[40], where a diffusion-based motion generator produces future whole-body trajectories conditioned on task inputs and a tracker converts them into physically executable actions. However, all of these paradigms fundamentally rely on large-scale human motion datasets, which remain scarce and expensive to acquire.

## III. BACKGROUND

## A. Single Rigid Body Dynamics

The SRB model can effectively capture both the linear and rotational dynamics of a floating base system by ignoring the limbs and treating the whole system as a lumped mass with fixed inertia. Using Newton-Euler dynamics, we can write the SRB dynamics as:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} \dot {\mathbf {v}} _ {\mathrm{B}} \\ \dot {\boldsymbol {\omega}} _ {\mathrm{B}} \end{array} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \mathbf {G} \\ - \mathbf {I} _ {\mathrm{B}} ^ {- 1} \left(\boldsymbol {\omega} _ {\mathrm{B}} ^ {\times} \mathbf {I} _ {\mathrm{B}} \boldsymbol {\omega} _ {\mathrm{B}}\right) \end{array} \right] + \left[ \begin{array}{c c} \frac {1}{m} \boldsymbol {I} _ {3 \times 3} & \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} \\ \mathbf {0} _ {3 \times 3} & \mathbf {I} _ {\mathrm{B}} ^ {- 1} \end{array} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} \mathbf {F} \\ \mathbf {M} \end{array} \right],\tag{1}
$$

where $\mathbf { v } _ { \mathrm { B } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 3 }$ and $\boldsymbol { \omega _ { \mathrm { B } } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 3 }$ represent the linear and angular velocity of the body, $\mathbf { G } = [ 0 \bar { \textbf { 0 } } - g ] ^ { \top }$ with gravitational acceleration $^ { g , }$ and $\textbf { F } \in \ \mathbb { R } ^ { 3 }$ and $\mathbf { M } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 3 }$ represent the sum of external forces and moments acting on the system. Finally, we have that $m \in \mathbb { R }$ and $\mathbf { I } _ { \mathrm { B } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \bar { 3 } \times 3 }$ are the mass and moment of inertia around COM. The full SRB state can therefore be expressed as:

$$
\mathbf {x} _ {\mathrm{SRB}} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c c} \mathbf {p} _ {\mathrm{B}} & \boldsymbol {\Theta} _ {\mathrm{B}} & \mathbf {v} _ {\mathrm{B}} & \boldsymbol {\omega} _ {\mathrm{B}} \end{array} \right],\tag{2}
$$

where $\mathbf { p } _ { \mathrm { B } } ~ \in ~ \mathbb { R } ^ { 3 }$ and $\Theta _ { \mathrm { B } } ~ \in ~ \mathrm { { \bf S O } ( 3 ) }$ represent the body’s position and orientation relative to the inertial frame.

Let the GRFMs for the left and right feet be given by:

$$
\boldsymbol {\lambda} _ {\mathrm{F}} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c c} \mathbf {F} _ {\mathrm{LF}} ^ {\top} & \mathbf {F} _ {\mathrm{RF}} ^ {\top} & \mathbf {M} _ {\mathrm{LF}} ^ {\top} & \mathbf {M} _ {\mathrm{RF}} ^ {\top} \end{array} \right] ^ {\top} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {1 2}.\tag{3}
$$

Then the external forces in (1) are given by: $\mathbf { F } = \textstyle \sum _ { i } \mathbf { F } _ { i }$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { M } = \sum _ { i } ( \mathbf { r } _ { i } ^ { \times } \mathbf { F } _ { i } + \mathbf { M } _ { i } ) } \end{array}$ , where $i \in \{ \mathrm { L F } , \mathrm { R F } \}$ and ${ \bf r } _ { \mathrm { L F } }$ and r<sub>RF</sub> is the vector from the base to left and right foot.

## B. Reinforcement Learning

RL formulates control as the search for an optimal policy $\pi ^ { * }$ that maximizes the expected discounted return $\begin{array} { r } { \dot { \mathbb { E } } _ { \mathcal { T } \sim p _ { \pi } ( \mathcal { T } ) } \left[ \sum _ { t = 0 } ^ { N _ { \mathrm { e p } } } \gamma ^ { t } r \big ( \mathbf { s } _ { t } , \mathbf { a } _ { t } \big ) \right] } \end{array}$ , where $\mathbf { s } _ { t }$ is the full environment state, $\mathbf { \bar { a } } _ { t }$ the agent action, and r the reward at each time step t. The episode terminates at step $N _ { \mathrm { e p } } , \gamma \in [ 0 , 1 )$ discounts future rewards, and $\tau$ denotes a trajectory induced by π. The problem is modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), in which $\mathbf { s } _ { t }$ governing the dynamics is not fully observable from humanoid onboard sensing. The policy instead acts on an observation $\mathbf { o } _ { t } = \Omega ( \mathbf { s } _ { t } )$ where Ω is the observation function producing a partial and noisy projection of $\mathbf { s } _ { t }$ from proprioception. Since a single observation often is insufficient to disambiguate $\mathbf { s } _ { t } ,$ , the policy is normally conditioned on a history of length h comprising past observations and actions, together with a user control command $\mathbf { o } _ { \mathrm { c m d } } , \ \mathrm { g i v i n g } \ \pi \left( \mathbf { a } _ { t } \ \middle \vert \ \mathbf { o } _ { t - h : t } , \ \mathbf { a } _ { t - h - 1 : t - 1 } , \ \mathbf { o } _ { \mathrm { c m d } } \right)$

## IV. WHOLE-BODY REFERENCE GENERATION

## A. Overview

The objective of the Stage 1 illustrated in Fig. 2, is to develop a whole-body reference generator capable of realizing a broad range of locomotion commands through a single policy. Learning multiple skills within one policy while ensuring smooth transitioning remains challenging. We therefore employ the SRB dynamics in (1) and incorporate the full humanoid kinematics information into the training process, ensuring that all generated motions respect the kinematic capabilities of the robot. The Stage 1 training is conducted in a numerical ROM simulator, as described in Section IV-B. The output of the Stage 1 is a commandconditioned whole-body reference generator, which is subsequently employed by the Stage 2 whole-body tracker.

## B. Training Environment

Stage 1 training is conducted in a proposed numerical ROM simulation environment that combines SRB dynamics with full humanoid kinematics using simplified contact kinematics. Because the environment is formulated directly at the reduced-order level, it exposes the quantities that ROMbased control acts upon, specifically the end-effector states governed by task-space accelerations and contact timings as well as applied GRFMs. This formulation therefore provides immediate access to the command inputs without mediation by an external physics or contact engine. The environment instead advances the system states through numerical evaluation that encompasses both contact and collision detection, with MuJoCo [41] employed solely for visualization.

1) Kinematics and Dynamics: In our simulator, only the robot base is modeled dynamically, while the end effectors are represented kinematically. The inertial properties of the physical robot are lumped into a single rigid body and the simulated physics evolves according to the SRB dynamics in (1). The external forces and moments acting on the system are computed such that the GRFMs are included only when the corresponding foot is in contact with the ground. The end effectors of the ROM comprise the feet poses and twists together with the hand positions and velocities, and they are driven kinematically through end effector task space accelerations. Given the ROM end effector poses and the SRB pose, the generalized coordinates of the full robot, $\mathbf { q } _ { \mathrm { F O M } } = \left[ \mathbf { p } _ { \mathrm { B } } \ \Theta _ { \mathrm { B } } \ \mathbf { q } _ { \mathrm { j } } \right]$ , are obtained via inverse kinematics, where $\mathbf { q } _ { \mathrm { i } } ~ \in ~ \mathbb { R } ^ { n _ { j } }$ are the joint positions of the robot and $n _ { j }$ denotes the number of joints. The joint velocities and accelerations are subsequently computed through numerical differentiation of the joint positions. Although the ROM assumes massless limbs, the leg joint torques, $\tau _ { \mathrm { l e g s } }$ , are approximated using the contact Jacobian, $\mathbf { J } _ { \mathrm { c } } ,$ together with the applied GRFMs as $\tau _ { \mathrm { l e g s } } = \mathbf { J } _ { \mathrm { c } } ^ { \top } \lambda _ { \mathrm { F } }$

2) Contact Kinematics: Each foot is modeled as an ankle with four contact points, two at the front and two at the back of the foot, matching the geometry of the robot’s foot. When a foot contact point is active, it is fixed in space, while the foot remains free to rotate about that point. We choose to constrain the foot roll to zero rotation to avoid brittle side contacts. As a result, the two heel contact points are always in the same contact state, and the same for the two toe contact states. Consequently, a foot in contact cannot yaw, however it is free to pitch around the toes and heels. Figure 3(a) shows the possible contact states for a foot in our simulator.

3) Gait Phasing: Foot contacts are gated via a phasing variable $\phi \in [ 0 , 1 )$ which increases monotonically from 0 to 1 over a full gait cycle before resetting to 0 (see Fig. 3(b)). For the left foot (LF), the phasing variable evolves as

$$
\phi_ {\mathrm{LF}, t + \Delta t} = \mathrm{mod} \Bigl (\phi_ {\mathrm{LF}, t} + \frac {\Delta t}{\alpha_ {T} T}, 1 \Bigr),\tag{4}
$$

where $T \in \mathbb { R } ^ { + }$ is the nominal gait period over two steps and $\alpha _ { T } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { + }$ is a scaling factor of the step period that allows us to slow down or speed up the gait. To ensure that the left and right feet remain exactly out of phase, the phasing variable of the right foot (RF) is defined as a function of that of the left foot with a phase offset of 0.5, that is,

$$
\phi_ {\mathrm{RF}, t + \Delta t} = \mathrm{mod} (\phi_ {\mathrm{LF}, t + \Delta t} + 0. 5, 1).\tag{5}
$$

When the phasing variable exceeds a take-off threshold $\phi _ { \mathrm { t o } } \in$ $( 0 , 1 )$ , the corresponding foot is considered to be in the swing phase. Otherwise, the foot is considered to be in the stance phase, meaning that it is in contact with the ground. The swing foot phasing variables $\phi _ { \mathrm { L F } } ^ { \mathrm { s w f } } , \phi _ { \mathrm { R F } } ^ { \mathrm { s w f } } \in [ 0 , 1 ) $ denote the progress of the swing feet and are given by:

$$
\phi_ {\mathrm{LF/RF}} ^ {\mathrm{swf}} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 0, & \phi_ {\mathrm{LF/RF}} <   \phi_ {\mathrm{to}} \\ \frac {\phi_ {\mathrm{LF/RF}} - \phi_ {\mathrm{to}}}{1 - \phi_ {\mathrm{to}}}, & \phi_ {\mathrm{LF/RF}} \geq \phi_ {\mathrm{to}} \end{array} \right.\tag{6}
$$

## C. ROM MDP Formulation

We now present the MDP formulation of the ROM training. Specifically, we describe the states of the environment, the observations, the action space, and the reward structure.

1) State: The full state of the environment is defined as a combination of the SRB states, the end effector poses and twists, and the phasing variables:

$$
\mathbf {s} ^ {\mathrm{ROM}} = \left[ \begin{array}{l l l l l l l l} \mathbf {x} _ {\mathrm{SRB}} & \mathbf {p} _ {\mathrm{F}} & \mathbf {p} _ {\mathrm{H}} & \boldsymbol {\Theta} _ {\mathrm{F}} & \mathbf {v} _ {\mathrm{F}} & \mathbf {v} _ {\mathrm{H}} & \boldsymbol {\omega} _ {\mathrm{F}} & \boldsymbol {\Phi} \end{array} \right],\tag{7}
$$

where $\mathbf { p } _ { \mathrm { F } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 6 }$ and $\mathbf { p } _ { \mathrm { H } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 6 }$ denote the two foot positions and hand positions in the world frame, and ${ \bf v } _ { \mathrm { F } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 6 }$ and $\mathbf { v } _ { \mathrm { H } } ~ \in ~ \mathbb { R } ^ { 6 }$ denote the linear velocities. Furthermore, we have that $\Theta _ { \mathrm { F , y z } } ~ = ~ [ \theta _ { \mathrm { L F } } ~ \theta _ { \mathrm { R F } }$ ψ<sub>LF</sub> $\psi _ { \mathrm { R F } } ] ~ \in ~ \mathbb { R } ^ { 4 }$ denotes the left and right foot pitch and yaw in the world frame, and $\omega _ { \mathrm { F , y z } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \bar { 4 } }$ the feet angular y and z velocities. The vector $\begin{array} { r } { \Phi = [ \phi _ { \mathrm { L F } } ~ \phi _ { \mathrm { R F } } ~ \phi _ { \mathrm { L F } } ^ { \mathrm { s w f } } ~ \phi _ { \mathrm { R F } } ^ { \mathrm { s w f } } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \bar { 4 } } } \end{array}$ collects the phasing variables of the left and right feet with the corresponding swing foot phasing variables.

2) Observation: The ROM policies receives an observation consisting of the following groups:

$$
\mathbf {o} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{ROM}} = \left[ \mathbf {o} _ {\text {base}} \mathbf {o} _ {\text {ee}} \mathbf {o} _ {\text {phase}} \mathbf {o} _ {\text {cmd}} \mathbf {o} _ {\text {hist}} \right].\tag{8}
$$

The base observation is given by $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { o } _ { \mathrm { b a s e } } = \left[ \mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { u p r } } \ \mathbf { v } _ { \mathrm { B } } \ \omega _ { \mathrm { B } } \right] \in \left[ \begin{array} { l } { \begin{array} { r } \end{array} } \end{array} \right] , } \end{array}$ $\mathbb { R } ^ { 9 }$ , where we define the upright base pose states as $\mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { u p r } } =$ $[ \Delta p _ { \mathrm { B , z } } \ \varphi _ { \mathrm { B } } \ \theta _ { \mathrm { B } } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 3 }$ . Here $\Delta p _ { \mathrm { B , z } }$ is the base height relative to a nominal desired height. The base pose states enable the robot to track a upright posture, while the base twist states facilitate the tracking of twist commands and help mitigate velocity oscillations.

To enable the direct control of the end effector poses and twists via task space acceleration, the end effector observations $\mathbf { o } _ { \mathrm { e e } } = [ \mathbf { p } _ { \mathrm { F } }$ p<sub>H</sub> ${ \bf v } _ { \mathrm { F } } \ { \bf v } _ { \mathrm { H } } \ \Theta _ { \mathrm { F , y z } } \ \omega _ { \mathrm { F , y z } } ] \ \in \ \mathbb { R } ^ { 3 2 }$ include the positions and linear velocities of the feet and hands relative to the base, expressed in the base control frame, together with the foot orientations in the base control frame and the foot angular velocities in the body frame. The phase observations $\mathbf { o } _ { \mathrm { p h a s e } } = [ \mathbf { c } _ { \mathrm { F } }$ sin (2πΦ) cos $( 2 \pi \Phi ) ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \mathrm { i 0 } }$ complement the continuous motion information with the discrete foot-ground interaction, consisting of binary contact indicators for the left and right feet, $\mathbf { c } _ { \mathrm { F } } \in \{ 0 , 1 \} ^ { 2 }$ , and sines and cosines of the foot phasing variables, which locate the robot within the gait cycle. The desired behavior itself is specified by the command observations ${ \bf o } _ { \mathrm { c m d } } = \left[ { \bf v } _ { x , y } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } } ~ \omega _ { z } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } } ~ \bar { { \bf h } _ { \mathrm { F } } } \right] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 7 }$ , which contain the commanded base twist and hopping commands, $\mathbf { h } _ { \mathrm { F } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 4 }$ , two for each foot. The twist command defines the velocity to be tracked, whereas two hopping commands per foot indicate when a foot should be in the air, with one applied immediately, and the other applied after a short delay preceding the deactivation of ground contact. The history observation $\mathbf { o } _ { \mathrm { h i s t } } = [ \mathbf { a } _ { t - 1 } ~ \mathbf { L } _ { \mathrm { p r e v } } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 3 2 }$ comprise the previous action $\mathbf { a } _ { t - 1 } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 2 8 }$ and the most recently executed step lengths in the x and y directions, $\mathbf { L } _ { \mathrm { p r e v } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 4 }$ . This promotes smoother behavior with reduced action chattering.

3) Action Space and Action Realization: The actions are designed to regulate the ROM inputs associated with the endeffector states, the step timing, and the GRFMs. The action space of the ROM is defined as

$$
\mathbf {a} ^ {\mathrm{ROM}} = \left[ \ddot {\mathbf {p}} _ {\mathrm{ee}} \boldsymbol {\Theta} _ {\mathrm{F,yz}} \lambda_ {\mathrm{F}} \alpha_ {\mathrm{T}} \phi_ {\mathrm{to}} \right] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {2 8},\tag{9}
$$

where, $\ddot { \mathbf { p } } _ { \mathrm { e e } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 1 0 }$ contains the desired end-effector linear accelerations expressed in the body frame, except for the feet linear z acceleration, which is instead governed by phasebased trajectories. $\Theta _ { \mathrm { F , y z } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 4 }$ contains the pitch and yaw angles of the feet which are commanded directly in the base control frame. Analogously, $\lambda _ { \mathrm { F } } ~ \in ~ \mathbb { R } ^ { 1 2 }$ contains the desired GRFMs for the left and right feet, expressed in the respective control frames of the feet. $\alpha _ { \mathrm { T } }$ and $\phi _ { \mathrm { t o } }$ are gait phasing variables. The actions are first clipped $\mathrm { t o } \pm 1 . 0$ and subsequently mapped through linear interpolation onto the admissible ROM ranges.

## D. Reward Structure

The set of rewards used to train the policy can broadly be divided into the following categories: task rewards, locomotion rewards, gait shaping rewards, and regularization rewards. The full overview is presented in Table I.

The task rewards consists of the linear x/y tracking rewards and an angular velocity z tracking reward. These rewards use exponential kernels with σ denoting the per-term Gaussian width in order to incentivize accurate tracking of the user commanded twist signals $( v _ { x } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } } , v _ { y } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } } , \omega _ { z } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } } )$ . As the main goal of the controller is to be able to track these commands, we give a relatively large weight to these terms.

The locomotion rewards are used to control the step timing of the robot, that is when a foot should be in contact with the ground and when to be in the air. The standing contact reward encourages full ground support when the velocity command is near zero. $\rho _ { \mathrm { d s } } ~ \in ~ \{ 0 , 0 . 5 , 1 . 0 \}$ represents contact with zero feet, one foot, or both feet, respectively. $1 _ { \mathrm { s t } } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ is 1 when the commanded velocity is close to zero and otherwise 0. The step-period deviation term penalizes the policy for deviating from the nominal step period, driving the step-period scaling factor $\alpha _ { T }$ toward 1. The contact phase threshold reward penalizes the policy for deviating from the nominal takeoff-threshold.

Whereas the locomotion rewards are concerned with the step timing of the robot, the gait-shaping rewards determine the spatial characteristics of the gait. The base pose reward is used to keep the roll and pitch of the base close to upright, and the base height close to a nominal desired base height. The stance foot flat term rewards the robot for keeping the foot pitch, $\Theta _ { \mathrm { F , y } } ~ \in ~ \mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ , close to zero and is only active when the twist command is zero. For the hands, we reward the policy for tracking nominal hand trajectories, $\mathbf { p } _ { \mathrm { H } } ^ { \mathrm { d } }$ , that are a function of $v _ { \mathrm { x } } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } }$ . Although the hand positions have minimal effect on the SRB dynamics, as we do not model limb inertia, we find that the simple arm motions aid in the Stage 2 training by providing references that are close to natural human arm swinging.

The remaining set of rewards are regularization rewards. In Table. I we define the base twist as $\pmb { \xi } \triangleq \left[ \mathbf { v } _ { \mathrm { B } } ~ \omega _ { \mathrm { B } } \right] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 6 }$ and $\zeta \ \triangleq \ \left\lceil \xi \ \dot { \xi } \right\rceil \ \in \ \mathbb { R } ^ { 1 2 }$ . For the normalized leg torque regularization we scale each torque value by the maximum torque of the respective joint. We use a power of 6 to mainly penalize joint torques that are close to the max-joint torques.

## V. WHOLE-BODY TRACKING

In the second stage, we train a whole-body tracking policy that follows the command-conditioned reference produced online by the Stage-1 ROM-based reference generator. Our whole-body tracking frameworks builds on the Beyond-Mimic setup [12].

We use the following group of observations:

$$
\mathbf {o} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{FOM}} = \left[ \mathbf {o} _ {\mathrm{mimic}} \mathbf {o} _ {\mathrm{phase}} ^ {\mathrm{H}} \mathbf {o} _ {\mathrm{cmd}} ^ {\mathrm{H}} \right],\tag{10}
$$

where $\mathbf { o } _ { \mathrm { m i m i c } }$ is adopted from BeyondMimic unchanged, which include the whole-body kinematic reference, proprioception, and the previous action. We extend the observations with $\mathbf { o } _ { \mathrm { p h a s e } }$ and $\mathbf { o } _ { \mathrm { c m d } }$ which are the phase and command observations from (8), supplied by the online ROM planner. The ROM observations are stacked over a history of $\mathrm { H } = 3$ control steps so that the policy observes the recent evolution of the planner state rather than a single snapshot.

The reward terms are adopted from BeyondMimic [12]. In addition, we augment the rewards with a twist-command tracking reward given by:

$$
r _ {\mathrm{twist}} = w _ {\mathrm{twist}} \sum_ {i \in \{v _ {x}, v _ {y}, \omega_ {z} \}} \exp (- (i - i ^ {\mathrm{cmd}}) ^ {2} / \sigma_ {\mathrm{twist}} ^ {2}),\tag{11}
$$

where $w _ { \mathrm { t w i s t } } = 0 . 5$ and $\sigma _ { \mathrm { t w i s t } } = 0 . 2$ . Although the ROM supplies a high-quality whole-body reference for the humanoid to imitate, a dynamics discrepancy between the reducedorder reference and the true full-body dynamics exists. This term helps the whole-body tracking policy in following the commanded twist. To reduce the joint torques, we also include the normalized leg torque penalty from the ROM training (Table I), evaluated using the FOM leg joint torques.

## VI. RESULTS

We evaluate the versatility and agility of the proposed twostage framework, which couples online ROM-based reference generation with whole-body tracking control, through both simulation studies and hardware experiments on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Both the ROM reference generator and whole-body tracking controller operate at 50 Hz.

## A. Reference Generation and Tracking in Simulation

In simulation, we verify that the ROM-guided reference generator produces whole-body references that the Stage 2 policy can track while following the commanded velocity. Particular emphasis is placed on continuously time-varying longitudinal commands and concurrent hopping commands, both of which are handled by a single unified policy. Figure 4 depicts the commanded velocity $( v _ { x } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } } )$ , the velocity of the ROM reference $( v _ { r } ^ { \mathrm { R O M } } )$ , and the velocity of the tracked fullorder model $( v _ { x } ^ { \mathrm { F O M } } )$ as the command ramps up to $4 . 0 ~ \mathrm { m / s }$ for fast running, reverses to $- 2 . 0 \mathrm { m } / \mathrm { s }$ for backward running, and settles at 1.0 $\mathrm { m } / \mathrm { s }$ for walking before returning to rest. The ROM reference follows the command closely over the full velocity envelope, confirming that the Stage 1 generator produces feasible whole-body references across the entire command range. The FOM in turn tracks the whole-body reference, so that the realized robot velocity matches the command throughout these rapid transitions. In the shaded interval, a right-foot air (hopping) command $h _ { \mathrm { R F } }$ is issued while the forward velocity command is held at $1 . 0 \mathrm { \ m } / \mathrm { s } ;$ velocity tracking is retained through this contact-schedule change, demonstrating that the framework composes twist and hopping commands rather than treating them as separate modes. The synchronized snapshots render this flow visible at both high speed and during hopping: the ROM state drives the generation of a whole-body reference, which the Stage 2 policy tracks to realize the corresponding robot motion.

## B. Hardware Experiments $f o r$ Versatile Command Tracking

We demonstrate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of our unified policy under time-varying velocity commands. Figure 5 shows the tracking performance for the lateral $( v _ { y } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } } )$ and angular $( \omega _ { z } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } } )$ velocity commands, which are varied online, exercising yaw rates up to ±3.0 rad/s and lateral velocities up $\mathrm { 1 o ~ \pm 1 . 0 ~ \ m / s }$ on hardware. The velocities of Unitree G1 are measured from its onboard IMU and odometry estimator. The measured velocities follow the commanded signals closely across both channels, and the robot transitions continuously between commands, including sign reversals through zero, without switching between separately trained skills. We emphasize that all results are produced by a single policy with no per-task tuning, no gait-specific gain scheduling, and no mode-switching logic: lateral stepping and in-place turning emerge from the same ROM-guided reference generator that produces forward and backward walking. These results indicate that the whole-body references supplied by the Stage 1 generator remain consistently trackable by the Stage 2 policy across a broad command space, and that this property is preserved under transfer to hardware without additional tuning.

## C. Diverse Real-World Locomotion

To further evaluate robustness and dynamic behaviors of our unified policy, we conducted additional real-world tests with the same unified policy. In Fig. 6(a) the robot performs high-speed running on uneven, sloped grassy terrain. In Fig. 6(b) the robot is commanded to walk over sandy terrain, transitioned from walking to highly dynamic single-leg hopping, and subsequently resume walking. Although the policy was trained only on flat ground and had not encountered any of these terrain variations in training, the robot achieved stable locomotion in all cases. These results indicate that a single policy trained within the proposed framework generalizes beyond the nominal training conditions, handling diverse locomotion tasks in the real world. We also performed highspeed running experiments to validate the top speed achieved on hardware. Figure 6(c) shows an experiment where the robot is commanded to run at $v _ { \mathrm { x } } ^ { \mathrm { c m d } } ~ = ~ 3 . 0 ~ \mathrm { m / s }$ . As the odometry data were noisy for high-velocity motions, we instead measured the average running speed over a distance of 15 feet (indicated by the yellow lines), and the robot was able to run at approximately $2 . 8 ~ \mathrm { m / s } .$ It is noted that the results for fast running at 4.0m/s and sustained hopping are demonstrated only in simulation, as these motions approach the actuation capabilities of the physical robot and are therefore beyond the scope of the current hardware validation.

## VII. CONCLUSION

In this work we presented a framework for realizing versatile and agile locomotion on humanoid robots. We trained a command-conditioned whole-body reference generator based on ROM control, where SRB dynamics were combined with the full humanoid kinematics to synthesize the reference motions. Integrating this reference generator into a wholebody tracking framework enabled a single policy to realize a broad range of twist commands and multiple modes of locomotion. We demonstrated the proposed approach on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, achieving longitudinal speeds from −2.0 m/s to 2.8 m/s, lateral speeds up to ±1.0 m/s, turning rates up to ±3.0 rad/s, and the ability to hop on one leg. Future work will include ablation studies, comparisons with baseline controllers, and tracking performance analysis.
