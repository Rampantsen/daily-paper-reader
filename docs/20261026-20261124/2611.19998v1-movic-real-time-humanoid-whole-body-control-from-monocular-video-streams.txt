# MoViC: Real-Time Humanoid Whole-Body Control from Monocular Video Streams

Abstract—Real-time whole-body imitation from monocular video requires both a robust universal tracker and high-quality real-time reconstruction. We present MoViC, a framework that uses live RGB video as a control signal for humanoid robots without wearable tracking devices. The framework combines monocular human motion reconstruction, robot retargeting, and a transformer-based behavior foundation model. The policy receives proprioceptive history, a single explicit next-step reference, and a compact frequency-domain motion representation that supplies temporal context without explicitly stacking future reference frames, which reduces the input dimension and network size. During deployment, our extrapolation method bridges the 10-Hz visual estimation with 50-Hz controller, enabling asynchronous visual processing and policy execution. Hardware experiments further demonstrate highly dynamic motion tracking and device-free teleoperation of bimanual and single-arm manipulation tasks from live monocular RGB input. Project page: https://movicproject.github.io/ .

Index Terms—Humanoid robots, Whole-body Control, Motion Representation, Vision-based Teleoperation

## I. INTRODUCTION

As humanoid robots increasingly enter everyday human environments, their behaviors are expected to become not only functional but also natural and human-like. Learning from large-scale human motion data provides a promising way to acquire such behavioral characteristics. However, achieving human-like whole-body control (WBC) on full-sized humanoid robots remains challenging. Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) approaches typically train task-specific policies with carefully designed rewards, but these policies transfer poorly across skills. In contrast, extracting motion patterns from human motion data and learning a unified behavior foundation model (BFM) through motion tracking has emerged as an effective approach for humanoid WBC, enabling humanoid robots to acquire diverse whole-body behaviors directly from reference human motions.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) for natural language processing and vision-language-action (VLA) models suggest that data scale-up has become a key driver behind the emergence of generalist models with strong zeroshot generalization ability. Humanoid whole-body control can also benefit from the same principle. Imitation learning framework provide a promising way to leverage large-scale datasets for humanoid WBC [1]–[4]. However, some existing approaches, such as ASAP [5] and BeyondMimic [1], have limited generalization capacity across various motion modalities due to their model architectures or the limited scale and quality of their training data. These methods can reproduce highly dynamic in-domain motions, but fail to transfer zeroshot to unseen movements. In contrast, some methods, such as the TWIST series [6], [7] and UniTracker [8], demonstrate stronger generalization but tend to underfit highly dynamic behaviors. To solve this trade-off, recent studies have begun to adopt models that accommodate large-scale, diverse motion corpora and to scale up the datasets used for training, such as OmniH2O [9], and SONIC [10], which achieve substantial progress in improving the learned policies’ robustness in tracking diverse motions. Scaling the reference-motion dataset, however, raises a deeper question of how its statistics should be exploited. A large motion dataset—typically including motion capture (MoCap) data and video-reconstructed motions—defines a highly complex, multi-modal distribution over whole-body motions. Most prevailing methods imitate reference trajectories only implicitly through RL and treat the motion data as an unstructured collection of samples. Such an imitation framework clearly benefits from growing scale, but the rich structure of the underlying distribution, such as its behavioral modes and inter-motion relations, remains largely unexploited. Beyond enlarging the motion scale, therefore, modeling the mathematical structure of the motion distribution offers a meaningful complement to building stronger behavior foundation models. Grouping similar motions provides the tracker with additional information for distinguishing different types of motion.

Moreover, existing popular humanoid real-time whole-body tracking systems can be roughly categorized into two types according to the teleoperation devices they use: MoCapbased [6], [11], [12] and VR-based [7], [13], as well as combinations of these two [9], [10]. Both of these solutions require expensive external hardware or operator-sensitive calibration, which restricts their deployment. MoCap-based methods typically depend on expensive, non-portable motion capture setups. VR-based methods require prior calibration that makes instant deployment impossible, and prolonged use of head-mounted displays (HMDs) can cause physiological burdens, such as visually induced motion sickness (VIMS). Vision-based solutions provide a practical alternative that preserves precise tracking ability while removing expensive and cumbersome wearable devices. They require nothing beyond a low-cost RGB camera. As humanoid robots increasingly move into public life, daily interaction between humans and humanoids should not heavily rely on dedicated or specialized instruments. The visual interface should be an appropriate choice that lets whole-body control blend seamlessly into everyday human activities.

Video-driven WBC have been used, but mostly offline [14], [15]. The resulting policies do not follow a live operator. Video-based teleoperation instead requires online tracking of the operator’s motion as it is estimated from the video stream. Curretnly, existing systems show that this is feasible with a single RGB camera [9], [10], [16], [17]. However, they either track only sparse targets, which discards whole-body detail, or rely on additional sensing that complicates deployment.

Besides, their monocular pose estimation is noisy and degrades under occlusion. High-quality motion reconstruction from video is typically too slow to keep pace with real-time control. SAM 3D Body [18] targets robust full-body mesh recovery across diverse poses, but it runs at about 10 Hz, well below the control rate. To resolve this trade-off between reconstruction quality and speed, we propose an extrapolation method that bridges low-rate visual references and high-rate whole-body control, without requiring per-video training.

In summary, we propose MoViC framework. MoViC addresses the interface between visual motion estimation and whole-body control through the following three contributions:

1) We develop MoViC, a monocular-video interface for real-time humanoid whole-body control. Human motion reconstruction and retargeting convert live RGB input into motion commands for a pretrained tracker, without wearable tracking devices or per-video policy training.

2) We formulate a compact frequency-domain conditioning interface for a transformer-based behavior foundation model. A θ goal window supplies temporal motion context alongside proprioceptive history and a single explicit next-step reference, reducing the need for stacked future reference poses.

3) We construct an online reference generator that combines extrapolation method with motion increments anchored to the latest measurement. It supplies 50-Hz references between approximately 10-Hz visual updates and compensates for reconstruction offsets shared by the current and predicted decoded states.

## II. RELATED WORK

## A. Whole-Body Humanoid Teleoperation

Motion imitation enables physically simulated characters to acquire coordinated skills. PHC [19] progressively expands a control policy for diverse motion tracking and fall recovery. Neural Categorical Priors [20] learn reusable vector-quantized skills. MaskedMimic [21] distills a transformer tracker into a conditional variational controller for partial motion constraints, while CALM [22] and PULSE [23] learn motion-conditioned latent policies through adversarial imitation. But the physically simulated characters have different joint configurations and degree of freedom, compared with real humanoid robots. These methods have not yet been demonstrated feasible on real humanoid robots.

For physical humanoids, ASAP [5] incorporates a residual action model learned from real-world data into simulation to reduce dynamics mismatch during policy refinement. However, the high cost of collecting real-world data makes such dynamics adaptation difficult to scale across diverse motion trajectories, potentially restricting the robot to a limited repertoire of learned motions. To enable a policy to track a broader range of reference motions, several works adopt similar teacher–student design. OmniH2O [9], HOVER [24], and TWIST [6] transfer privileged tracking skills to students operating with sparse targets, masked command modes, or restricted future references. UniTracker [8] follows this paradigm while using a conditional variational student to model motion ambiguity under partial observations. However, these methods still fall short of achieving universal motion tracking ability.

To enable a single policy to track a broader range of reference motions, architectural improvements and temporal modeling of the input can enhance its representational capacity. Recent studies have made some progress toward universal motion tracking along these lines. HumanPlus [17] and TWIST2 [7] directly train history-conditioned tracking policies through reinforcement learning, using a decoderonly transformer and a convolutional encoder with an MLP, respectively. GMT [25] and HoloMotion-1 [26] increase representational capacity through mixtures of experts, implemented as softly combined expert policies and sparsely routed transformer layers, respectively. However, their performance is still bounded by how well the training data covers the space of motions. The out-of-distribution (OOD) motions are tracked poorly. Learned latent interfaces and generative models further broaden controller functionality. Exbody2 [27] extends wholebody tracking to expressive humanoid motion. SONIC [10] conditions control through shared latent representations and quantized codes, which unify heterogeneous motion commands. BeyondMimic [1] and Heracles [28] combine tracking with motion synthesis, using guided latent diffusion for skill composition and state-conditioned trajectory generation for disturbance recovery, respectively. However, many of them rely on multi-stage training pipelines, such as teacher–student distillation, or latent-space pretraining. These make training costly, and hard to reproduce.

Moreover, fully exploiting the representational capacity of these high-capacity models with large parameters amount demands vast high-quality motion data. Training data combine public motion-capture datasets with video-reconstructed motions and self-captured data. Dataset scales reach hundreds or thousands of hours [10], [26]. As motion libraries grow, their structure can guide learning beyond providing reference targets and imitation rewards. However, few work mentioned above have considered the dataset structure. Some recent works, Humanoid-GPT [11] and BumbleBee [29] group motions for expert training and transformer distillation using Harmonic Motion Embedding and learned motion–text representations, respectively. But these methods both assign a single embedding to an entire sequence, so sequences containing multiple behaviors collapse into a mixed descriptor. Moreover, neither of them can be reused by the policy at deployment. A complementary use of motion structure is to encode local temporal context directly for policy conditioning.

Frequency-domain representations offer a structured way to encode this context. DeepPhase [30] learns phase manifolds with a periodic autoencoder. FLD [31] extends this formulation with latent dynamics and multi-step consistency for periodic and quasi-periodic motion, enabling phase-based extrapolation. DFM [32] emphasizes local reconstruction and updated encodings to retain details in less periodic motions. This allows motions to be organized at the segment level rather than per sequence, and the same representation to be reused online for expert routing or continuous, adaptive sampling.

Video-driven deployment additionally requires handling reconstruction errors and the timing of reference updates. Several works learn humanoid behaviors from videos offline. Humanoid-X [14] builds over 20 million humanoid poses from internet videos, HDMI [15] trains humanoid–object interaction policies from monocular videos, and GenMimic [33] tracks motions reconstructed from generated videos. These methods process the videos before execution rather than following a live operator. Teleoperation instead requires online tracking, in which the robot imitates the operator’s whole-body motion while it is being estimated from the video stream. Several systems achieve this visual interface with a single RGB camera. Although H2O [16] teleoperates a full-sized humanoid from a webcam, the robot’s root velocity is still measured by a MoCap system during deployment. OmniH2O also supports an RGB camera, but tracks only the head and hand targets used for its VR interface [9]. The sparse reference specifies only part of the operator’s posture. VisualMimic [34] focuses on task-specific egocentric command generation, and requires a separate high-level policy, with its own reward design and privileged teacher, for each task. This task-dependent visual control is therefore unsuitable for vision-based teleoperation, which requires following arbitrary operator motions rather than executing a predefined task. HumanPlus [17] shadows body and hand motions on a humanoid with WHAM [35] and HaMeR [36]. SONIC [10] accepts human video as a real-time command source. These systems show that video-based wholebody teleoperation is feasible, but they also expose some limitations. Firstly. the tracking ability is poor on the OOD highly dynamic motion references. Besides, the monocular pose estimation fails under large occlusions [16], [17], and the WBC tracker has to tolerate the delayed reconstruction input [10]. The difficulties of pose estimations remain with the reconstruction models.

## B. Video Reconstruction

Monocular human reconstruction offers a way to obtain motion references without body-worn tracking devices. VIBE [37] regresses pose and shape over time with an adversarial motion prior learned from motion-capture sequences. HMR 2.0 [38] estimates meshes with a transformer for human tracking in video. HybrIK [39] solves joint swings from 3D keypoints and regresses only 1-DoF twists, yielding pixelaligned and kinematically valid meshes. But it is sensitive to keypoint errors. WHAM [35] and GVHMR [40] further recover world-grounded motion, using camera angular velocity with contact-aware refinement and gravity-view coordinates, respectively. However, such models are largely trained on laboratory captures or pseudo-labeled data and often fail on challenging poses and under severe occlusion. Moreover, they rely on temporal context rather than per-frame inference. GVHMR in particular reconstructs entire sequences offline, which incurs latency and suffers leads to severe degradation under strong camera jitter. SAM 3D Body [18] targets these failure cases. It is trained on annotations from a data engine that selects rare poses and imaging conditions, and represents the full body with the Momentum Human Rig, which decouples skeletal pose and body shape. It outperforms prior single-image methods and is competitive with video-based approaches. Because it requires only a single image, it can process a live stream frame by frame, which suits real-time teleoperation.

Our proposed MoViC combines temporal reference conditioning and online reference generation for whole-body control from live RGB video. MoViC implements SAM 3D Body for per-frame human reconstruction, and extrapolation for generating 50-Hz references from available visual estimates to align the reference trajectory sampling rate with the control rate. We combine existing reconstruction capabilities with structured motion conditioning, where compact motion representations are fed to a task-agnostic WBC tracker.

## III. SYSTEM OVERVIEW

As illustrated in Fig. 1, MoViC drives humanoid wholebody control from live monocular RGB video through an online video-to-motion module, SEG, which reconstructs human motion with SAM 3D Body [18] at approximately 10 Hz, and then retargets and extrapolates it to the robot into reference motions at 50 Hz. A transformer-based behavior foundation model then tracks these references by combining proprioceptive history with an explicit next-step reference and a frequency-domain θ goal.

## A. Data Curation

We use a combination of AMASS [41], LAFAN1 [42], Motion-X++ [43], OMOMO [44], and self-captured motion data to train a BFM as our robust tracking policy. Because these datasets contain human motion sequences, we retarget them to the robot to form the reference-motion library for training a motion tracker.

Motion Retargeting: When constructing the reference-motion library, the motions are converted to a common human-body representation [45], temporally resampled, and retargeted to the EngineAI PM01 through parallel differentiable kinematic optimization. Let ${ \bf y } _ { i , t }$ denote the position of human body keypoint i, and let $\mathbf { p } _ { i , t }$ denote the position of its corresponding robot link. We solve

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \min _ {\mathbf {q}, \mathbf {T}, s, \{s _ {i j} \}} & \lambda_ {\mathrm{g}} \sum_ {t, i} \| \mathbf {p} _ {i, t} - s \mathbf {y} _ {i, t} \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ & + \lambda_ {\mathrm{l}} \sum_ {t, (i, j) \in \mathcal {E}} \| s _ {i j} \Delta \mathbf {p} _ {i j, t} - s \Delta \mathbf {y} _ {i j, t} \| _ {2} ^ {2} \\ & + \lambda_ {\mathrm{r}} \sum_ {t, k \in \mathcal {K}} d _ {\mathrm{SO(3)}} ^ {2} (\mathbf {R} _ {k, t}, \mathbf {R} _ {k, t} ^ {\mathrm{h}}) + \mathcal {R}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $\Delta \mathbf { p } _ { i j , t } = \mathbf { p } _ { i , t } - \mathbf { p } _ { j , t }$ , with $\Delta \mathbf { y } _ { i j , t }$ defined analogously; $\mathcal { E }$ denotes the set of kinematically connected keypoint pairs. The set $\kappa$ contains links with orientation targets, ${ \bf R } _ { k , t }$ and $\mathbf { R } _ { k , t } ^ { \mathrm { h } }$ are the corresponding robot and human orientations, and $d _ { \mathrm { S O ( 3 ) } }$ denotes their distance on the rotation manifold. The global and pairwise scales s and $s _ { i j }$ account for morphology differences, while R contains regularization terms for jointposition and joint-velocity limits and temporal smoothness. The global term anchors the robot keypoints to the reference trajectory, the local term preserves the relative configuration of connected body parts, and the rotation term aligns the selected link orientations. We jointly optimize the root poses, joint configurations, and scale factors for batches of variable-length clips using GPU-parallel forward kinematics and automatic differentiation; padded frames are excluded from the objective by a validity mask.

## B. Universal Motion Tracker

1) Overview: In this section, a scalable training framework for a universal motion tracker is proposed. Unlike other trackers that use the explicitly stacking future reference frames, we innovatively integrate the future information into frequency-domain motion representations, and design the actor architecture accordingly. The proposed framework, MoViC, integrates two coupled components: (i) a pretrained motion encoder, built on latent dynamics, compresses the reference motions into a compact periodic parameterization $\theta$ and (ii) a tracking policy incorporates θ into a Transformer actor. The actor receives ten frames of proprioceptive history, an explicit next-step reference, and a θ goal window. During training, this θ window conveys information about ten future reference frames. The explicit target specifies the immediate tracking objective, while the frequency-domain representation supplies additional motion context.

Most motions are relatively simple and can be learned with the next reference frame only, while more challenging motions may benefit from a longer future context. We therefore augment the explicit next-frame reference with a compact θ token that summarizes the next ten reference frames. Compared with explicitly providing ten or more future reference frames, this formulation reduces the input dimensionality, and thus supports learning with a smaller network, enabling the policy to acquire a broad repertoire of motions spanning different levels of difficulty.

2) Problem Formulation: Whole-body motion tracking for the 24-DoF EngineAI PM01 is formulated as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP), $\mathcal { M } =$ $( \mathcal { S } , \mathcal { O } , \mathcal { A } , \mathcal { P } , \Omega , \mathcal { R } , \gamma )$ . Here, S, O, and A denote the state, observation, and action spaces; P and Ω are the transition and observation models; and R and $\gamma$ denote the reward function and discount factor. The underlying state includes the robot, environment, and reference-motion process, whereas the policy receives only proprioceptive measurements and reference-derived features.

We optimize the policy using proximal policy optimization (PPO) [47] to maximize the expected discounted return,

$$
J (\pi) = \mathbb {E} _ {\pi} \left[ \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {T - 1} \gamma^ {t} R _ {t} \right],\tag{2}
$$

where $T$ is the episode length, $\gamma = 0 . 9 9$ , and $R _ { t }$ is the tracking reward defined in Section III-B4.

3) Asynchronous Actor-Critic: Training uses an asymmetric actor-critic architecture. The transformer actor maps ${ \bf o } _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { a c t } }$ to an action distribution, whereas the critic estimates the discounted return from ten privileged state observations and ten future references:

$$
V _ {\psi} \left(\mathbf {o} _ {t - N + 1: t} ^ {\text { priv }}, \mathbf {r} _ {t} ^ {1, 2, \dots , 1 0}\right).\tag{3}
$$

Each privileged observation ${ \bf o } _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { p r i v } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 4 5 9 }$ contains the root position, linear velocity, orientation and angular velocity in the world frame, the body positions, orientations, linear and angular velocities in the robot heading frame, the joint positions and velocities, and the previous action. The critic is an MLP with four hidden layers of width 1024 and Exponential Linear Units (ELU) [48] as activations. It receives no θ features and is used only during training.

The actor is Transformer-based. At each control step, the actor receives

$$
\mathbf {o} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{act}} = \left[ \mathbf {o} _ {t - N + 1: t}; \mathbf {r} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{next}}; \mathbf {G} _ {t} \right] \in \mathbb {R} ^ {1 1 8 2},\tag{4}
$$

where $N = 1 0$ and ${ \bf o } _ { t } ~ \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 8 1 }$ contains joint positions and velocities, the previous action, and heading-frame root angular velocity and orientation. The history contributes 810 features, $\mathbf { r } _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { n e x t } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 5 2 }$ specifies the next-step reference, and $\mathbf { G } _ { t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 3 2 0 }$ is the reference generator’s output, θ goal window that is explained in Sec. III-C2. The actor architecture is illustrated in Sec. III-D.

4) Whole-Body Tracking Reward: We define the reward as the sum of a tracking part and a regularization part,

$$
r _ {t} = r ^ {\mathrm{track}} \big (s _ {t}, \hat {s} _ {t} \big) + r ^ {\mathrm{reg}} \big (s _ {t}, a _ {t}, a _ {t - 1} \big),\tag{5}
$$

where $r ^ { \mathrm { t r a c k } }$ minimizes body-link pose and velocity errors against the reference state $\hat { s } _ { t } ,$ and $r ^ { \mathrm { r e g } }$ penalizes jerky actions, joint-limit violations and unintended contacts. Table I lists the terms of both parts.

In Table I, p, q, v, ω represent link position, orientation, linear and angular velocity, respectively. Hatted variables denote the corresponding reference values. $\angle$ denotes the geodesic angular distance between the reference and actual orientations, measured in radians. B indicates the 12 tracked links $( | B | = 1 2 )$ , and $\boldsymbol { B } _ { \mathrm { a l l o w } }$ denotes the links allowed to make contact, namely the feet and forearms $( | B _ { \mathrm { a l l o w } } | = 4 )$

5) Adaptive Sampling: We combine randomized subdivision of long sequences with sampling weights based on completion history and tracking error. Weights decrease as motions are mastered, while poorly tracked segments receive higher priority. Normalizing these weights yields sampling probabilities that reduce repeated exposure to easy segments and emphasize motions requiring further improvement.

## C. Real-Time Monocular Video Processing Module (SEG)

1) Real-Time 3D Human Reconstruction from Video Streams: Prior humanoid whole-body teleoperation systems either track only sparse head and hand targets or still depend on MoCap. We reconstruct human motion from a monocular RGB stream and transmit SMPL parameters to the robot retargeting module. Visual estimation runs asynchronously at approximately 10 Hz.

Per-Frame Monocular Reconstruction: A TensorRT YOLO11-pose detector returns person boxes and 2D keypoints. Reconstruction proceeds only when exactly one detection exceeds a confidence threshold of 0.75.

SAM 3D Body processes the 512 × 512 person crop using a DINOv3 [49] backbone conditioned on camera rays derived from the intrinsics K. Intrinsics are obtained from fixedcamera calibration or per-frame MoGe-2 estimates [50]. A promptable decoder and Momentum Human Rig (MHR) [51] head estimate mesh vertices $\mathbf { V } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 1 8 4 3 9 \times 3 }$ , root translation t, and global orientation, followed by inverse-kinematics refinement. Hand decoding is omitted from the streamed output.

The MHR mesh is mapped to Skinned Multi-Person Linear Model (SMPL) [45] by a precomputed barycentric correspondence: SMPL vertex j is $\begin{array} { r } { \hat { \bf v } _ { j } ~ = ~ \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { 3 } b _ { j , k } { \bf V } _ { \mathrm { w } } [ f _ { j , k } ] . } \end{array}$ where $V _ { \mathrm { w } } = V + \mathbf { t }$ . A fixed subset of 1500 vertices, where $\mathbf { V } _ { \mathrm { w } } = \mathbf { V } + \mathbf { t } . \mathbf { A }$ fixed subset of 1500 vertices, centroid-centered and flattened to $\mathbb { R } ^ { 4 5 0 0 }$ , is fed to a small MLP that regresses a 6D global orientation, the 63-dimensional body pose (21 joints, axis-angle), and 10 shape coefficients. SMPL forward kinematics yields 24 joints, optionally denoised by a slidingwindow smoothing MLP. The output results of the per-Frame monocular reconstruction module are shown in Fig. 3.

For the fixed-camera setup, we use the calibrated gravity direction to express the reconstructed human motion in a gravity-aligned coordinate frame with an upward-pointing $z -$ axis.

2) Reference Motions Encoding and Frequency-Domain Representation: Reference motions contain temporal structure that can inspire WBC tracking. Most existing trackers treat the motion library as an unstructured collection of samples, so clips containing multiple behaviors collapse into a mixed descriptor. In contrast, we encode every short motion window, rather than an entire sequence, into per-channel sinusoidal latent parameters, namely a frequency, amplitude, and offset that describe the high-level features of the motion and a phase that indexes local time within it. Assuming that the frequency, amplitude, and offset remain quasi-constant over a short horizon, an encoder is trained with a multi-step forward-prediction loss, so that future states are predicted by advancing only the phase at the encoded frequency and decoding the result. These latent dynamics yield a compact, and temporally coherent representation. To reduce the impact of the local periodicity of this method and improve the tracking performance of less periodic motions such as dance, we remove the forward-prediction terms in order to train on local reconstruction alone. Its parameters are re-estimated from each new reference window rather than constrained to remain constant across a prediction horizon, so the frequency, amplitude, and offset can follow expressive, non-periodic changes in the motion. These two formulations thus serve complementary roles in MoViC. The local-reconstruction encoder provides an accurate, and timevarying description of the reference motion, which is used to construct the θ for policy conditioning and to characterize the reference motion library. Thanks to its motion prediction and genreation capacity, the predictive latent dynamics of the periodic representation are used during real-world deployment to generate reference motions that compensate the low-rate visual updates.

Motion Encoder: Each reference frame is a body state $\mathbf { s } _ { n } ~ \in ~ \mathbb { R } ^ { 5 7 }$ comprising base-frame linear and angular velocities, projected gravity, and the positions and velocities of the 24 actuated joints. We construct a trailing window $\mathbf S _ { n } = [ \mathbf s _ { n - H + 1 } , \dots , \mathbf s _ { n } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 5 7 \times H }$ with $H = 3 1$ , spanning one second. A one-dimensional convolutional encoder maps the window to $c = 6 4$ latent channels. At layer $\ell ,$ the temporal convolution is

$$
U _ {n, k, h} ^ {(\ell)} = d _ {k} ^ {(\ell)} + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {C _ {\ell - 1}} \sum_ {r = - 1 5} ^ {1 5} W _ {k, j, r} ^ {(\ell)} \widetilde {X} _ {n, j, h + r} ^ {(\ell - 1)}.\tag{6}
$$

where $C _ { \ell - 1 }$ is the input channel count, $W ^ { ( \ell ) }$ and $d ^ { ( \ell ) }$ are learned weights and biases, and $\widetilde { X } ^ { ( \ell - 1 ) }$ denotes the padded layer input, with $\mathbf { X } _ { n } ^ { ( 0 ) } ~ = ~ \mathbf { S } _ { n }$ . The sums aggregate input channels $j$ and temporal offsets r for output channel $k$ at position $h .$ The kernel length is 31; unit stride and same padding preserve the window length, yielding encoder output $\mathbf { Z } _ { n } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { c \times H }$

A differentiable fast Fourier Transform (FFT) estimates the frequency $f _ { n , k }$ , amplitude $\alpha _ { n , k }$ , and offset $b _ { n , k }$ of each channel k. A linear head followed by atan2 estimates the phase $\phi _ { n , k }$ . Here, α denotes the Fourier amplitude to distinguish it from the policy action. The latent signal is reconstructed as

$$
\widehat {z} _ {n, k} (\tau) = \alpha_ {n, k} \sin (2 \pi (f _ {n, k} \tau + \phi_ {n, k})) + b _ {n, k},\tag{7}
$$

where $\tau$ is the temporal coordinate within the window. A decoder maps the reconstructed latent sequence to $\widehat { \mathbf { S } } _ { n }$

The periodic and local representations share the same encoder and decoder design, but differ in the training objective. Let $\pmb { \eta } _ { n } = ( \mathbf { f } _ { n } , \pmb { \alpha } _ { n } , \mathbf { b } _ { n } )$ collect the frequencies, amplitudes, and offsets encoded from $\mathbf { S } _ { n } .$ , and let ${ \mathcal D } ( \phi , \pmb { \eta } ) = \mathrm { D e c } \left( \widehat { P } ( \phi , \pmb { \eta } ) \right)$ where $\widehat { P }$ evaluates the sinusoidal reconstruction of all latent channels. The two objectives are

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\text { periodic }} = \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {M - 1} \left| \left| \mathcal {D} (\phi_ {n} + i   \mathbf {f} _ {n} \Delta t, \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {n}) - \mathbf {S} _ {n + i} \right| \right| ^ {2},\tag{8a}
$$

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{local}} = \left\| \mathcal {D} (\boldsymbol {\phi} _ {n}, \boldsymbol {\eta} _ {n}) - \mathbf {S} _ {n} \right\| ^ {2},\tag{8b}
$$

where $\Delta t$ is the reference frame interval and M is the prediction horizon. In (8a), the parameters $\eta _ { n }$ encoded from one window must also reconstruct the following $M \ : - \ : 1$ windows once the phase is advanced, which acts as the reference generator and enforces the latent dynamics used for extrapolation. Eq. (8b) is the case $M = 1 \colon$ each window is reconstructed only from its own encoding, so $\eta _ { n }$ can change from one window to the next. We train the encoder for the $\theta$ goal window used for actor, with (8b). This encoder acts as the reference motion encoder. Eq. (8b) emphasizes local reconstruction without enforcing multi-step predictions from a fixed latent parameterization. The sinusoidal representation is retained, while its parameters are recomputed for each reference window to capture changes in the motion.

3) Latent-Dynamics Extrapolation of Low-Rate Visual $R e f -$ erences: High-quality visual reconstruction runs much slower than the humanoid control loop, and this mismatch has so far been handled by reconstructing motion offline before execution or by leaving the tracker to tolerate the delayed, lowrate estimates. Rather than trading reconstruction quality for speed, we close this gap using the periodic latent dynamics extrapolation method. The reconstructed poses are retargeted to the robot and supplied to the reference generator in Sec. III-C3. This module fills the intervals between low-rate updates by advancing the latent phase and decoding reference states at 50 Hz. Predictions are anchored to the latest accepted reference using (12). At deployment, our extrapolation methods generates intermediate references for 50-Hz policy execution from an approximately 10-Hz motion stream. The extrapolation network is trained using largely identical hyperparameters to those in Table II, except that it is trained with (8a) and $M \ : = \ : 1 5$ instead of (8b). Each accepted reference updates a rolling buffer of H states, which is encoded into latent phase, frequency, amplitude, and offset. Between updates, the latent phase is advanced and the resulting states are decoded. This generates reference estimates without access to future measurements.

## D. Transformer Actor Architecture

Existing universal trackers typically rely on teacher–student distillation or mixtures of experts to gain capacity, and on explicitly stacked future reference frames to obtain temporal context that inflates the policy input. Our actor is a single transformer conditioned on a compact θ goal window, which summarizes the upcoming reference motion in the frequency domain. The actor receives an 810-dimensional proprioceptive history, a 52-dimensional next-step reference, and a 320- dimensional θ goal window, for a total of 1182 input features. Input streams are normalized and embedded by MLPs into tokens of dimension $D = 7 6 8$ . A learnable query provides the policy readout. Let $\mathbf { G } _ { t , j }$ denote the blocks of the θ goal window:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \mathbf {u} ^ {q} = \mathbf {q} _ {\mathrm{qry}}, \\ \mathbf {u} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{prop}} = \mathrm{MLP} _ {p} (\mathbf {o} _ {t - N + 1: t}), \\ \mathbf {u} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{ref}} = \mathrm{MLP} _ {r} (\mathbf {r} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{next}}), \\ \mathbf {u} _ {t, j} ^ {\theta} = \mathrm{MLP} _ {\theta} (\mathbf {G} _ {t, j}), \quad j \in \{1, 2 \}, \end{array}\tag{13}
$$

where $N = 1 0$ , and $\mathbf { G } _ { t , j }$ is the j-th block of $\mathbf { G } _ { t } . \mathrm { \ M L P } _ { p } ,$ $\operatorname { M L P } _ { r } ,$ and ${ \mathrm { M L P } } _ { \theta }$ embed the proprioceptive history, the next-step reference, and the frequency-domain feature blocks, respectively, into D-dimensional tokens. These three networks have separate parameters, while ${ \mathrm { M L P } } _ { \theta }$ is shared across the two frequency-domain tokens. The query $\mathbf { q } _ { \mathrm { q r y } } \ \in \ \mathbb { R } ^ { D }$ is a learned parameter shared across control steps and environments; unlike the other tokens, it is not initialized from an observation or reference feature.

Each proprioceptive observation $\mathbf { o } _ { t } ~ \in ~ \mathbb { R } ^ { 8 1 }$ contains joint positions and velocities, the previous action, and headingframe root angular velocity and orientation. Orientation uses a six-dimensional tangent–normal representation. The next-step reference $\mathbf { r } _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { n e x t } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \bar { 5 } 2 }$ specifies the explicit tracking target at the next control timestamp. The θ features convey temporal context without explicitly stacking ten future reference frames in the proposed actor input.

Learned token-type embeddings and sinusoidal positional encodings are added to the sequence

$$
\mathbf {X} _ {t} = \left[ \mathbf {u} ^ {q}, \mathbf {u} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{prop}}, \mathbf {u} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{ref}}, \mathbf {u} _ {t, 1} ^ {\theta}, \mathbf {u} _ {t, 2} ^ {\theta} \right].\tag{14}
$$

The query, proprioceptive input, reference target, and frequency-domain inputs use four distinct token types. The two frequency-domain tokens share a type embedding and are distinguished by position. The sequence is processed by four post-normalization transformer encoder layers with full bidirectional self-attention. Each layer uses six attention heads of dimension 128 and a feed-forward network of width 1024 with ReLU activations and no dropout.

The encoder output at the query position is normalized and passed to the action head:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\mathbf {y} _ {t} = \mathrm{LN} ([ \mathrm{Enc} _ {T} (\mathbf {X} _ {t}) ] _ {0}),} \\ & {\pmb {\mu} _ {t} = \mathrm{tanh} (\mathrm{MLP} _ {\mathrm{head}} (\mathbf {y} _ {t})),} \\ & {\mathbf {a} _ {t} \sim \mathcal {N} \big (\pmb {\mu} _ {t}, \sigma_ {a} ^ {2} I _ {2 4} \big).} \end{array}\tag{15}
$$

The action head has three hidden layers of width 1024 with ELU activations.

Training Protocol: The controller is trained from scratch using proximal policy optimization (PPO), while the reference motion encoder pretrained with Eq. (8b) remains fixed. Let w and ψ denote the actor and critic parameters, respectively. For each rollout batch, the principal actor–critic objective is:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \underset {w, \psi} {\arg \min} \Bigl \{- \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{PPO}} ^ {\mathrm{clip}} (w; \mathbf {z} _ {t}) + \lambda_ {V} \mathcal {L} _ {V} ^ {\mathrm{clip}} (\psi) \Bigr \}, \\ \mathbf {z} _ {t} = \left[ \mathbf {o} _ {t - 9: t}; \mathbf {r} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{next}}; \mathrm{sg} [ \mathbf {G} _ {t} ] \right]. \end{array}\tag{16}
$$

where $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { P P O } } ^ { \mathrm { c l i p } }$ is the clipped policy surrogate evaluated over the rollout batch, $\mathcal { L } _ { V } ^ { \mathrm { c l i p } }$ is the clipped value loss, and $\lambda _ { V }$ weights the value loss. The stop-gradient operator sg preserves its input in the forward pass and blocks gradients in backpropagation.

We use a clipping threshold of $\epsilon = 0 . 1$ and generalized advantage estimation with $\gamma = 0 . 9 9$ and $\lambda = 0 . 9 5$ . Advantages and value targets are normalized. The Training is conducted on four A100 GPUs.

## C. Ablation Study

In order to demonstrate the effectiveness of the θ goal window, ablation experiments on the policy observation are designed. We investigate whether the frequency-domain representation can replace an explicit multi-frame reference preview for whole-body motion tracking. All five variants receive an 810-dimensional proprioceptive history comprising ten consecutive 81-dimensional observations. Each explicit reference frame contains 52 features, and the θ goal window input contributes 320 features. The proposed configuration, θ with 1 frame, combines the proprioceptive history with the explicit next-step reference and a θ goal window containing information about ten future reference frames. The θ with 10 frames variant additionally provides nine explicit future reference frames. The 1 future frame and 10 future frames variants omit the θ goal window and use only the proprioceptive history and the corresponding explicit reference inputs.

Table IV shows that temporal reference context improves tracking performance. Without θ, increasing the explicit preview from one to ten frames raises the success rate from 90% to 95%, while reducing MPJPE from 0.47 to 0.28 rad and MPJVE from 4.80 to 4.55 rad/s. The proposed θ with 1 frame configuration matches the highest success rate of 99% and achieves the lowest MPJPE and MPJVE, 0.25 rad and 4.30 rad/s, respectively. Compared with 10 future frames, it improves the success rate by 2 percentage points and reduces MPJPE and MPJVE by 10.7% and 5.5%, respectively. These results support using θ to convey the additional reference context while retaining only the next-step target explicitly.

The 90% success rate of the one-frame baseline indicates that the immediate target already supports successful tracking for much of the evaluated motion set. Additional context is therefore motivated by the remaining failures, particularly segments that may require anticipation beyond the next target. The increase from 90% to 95% with ten explicit frames represents five percentage points of additional success, but an explicit ten-frame preview incurs a larger input at every control step, including portions where local guidance is sufficient. This motivates encoding extended reference context compactly. The results show that θ reaches 99% success with one explicit target, exceeding the 95% obtained from ten explicit frames. The aggregate success rates support this trade-off, although they do not identify which individual trajectories account for the improvement.

The benefit of θ is also observed when ten explicit reference frames are retained. This configuration does not outperform θ with 1 frame on any reported metric: both achieve 99% success, while the single-frame configuration has lower position and velocity errors. Thus, providing nine additional explicit reference frames yields no observed improvement once the actor receives the frequency-domain representation, supporting the use of a single explicit next-step target alongside θ.

The proposed configuration also uses a more compact actor input. Its input dimension is 1182, compared with 1330 for 10 future frames and 1650 for θ with 10 frames, corresponding to reductions of 11.1% and 28.4%, respectively. The reference component contains 372 features, comprising the 320-dimensional θ code and the 52-dimensional next-step target, compared with 520 features for ten explicit frames. Relative to θ with 10 frames, reducing the explicit reference input from ten frames to one also decreases the number of weights in the reference embedding’s input layer when its hidden widths are held fixed. For a first-layer width of d, this removes 468d input weights. Smaller observation vectors also reduce rollout-storage requirements. The reduced input dimensionality therefore, accompanies equal or higher tracking success and lower tracking errors in these comparisons.

• Mean per-joint position error (MPJPE): The absolute joint-angle error $| q _ { s i m } - q _ { r e f } |$ is computed over 23 joints, first averaged across joints and then across all frames in the sequence.

• Mean per-joint velocity error (MPJVE): Computed in the same manner as MPJPE, but using joint angular velocities instead of joint angles.

• Mean per-keypoint position error (MPKPE): The mean Euclidean distance between the 14 tracked keypoints selected from the robot.

All three error metrics above are calculated using the robot motions reconstructed from the video that act as the input reference motions of each tracker, as the ground truth. For a fair comparison, we reproduce all three baseline methods using their publicly released codebases and adapt them to the EngineAI PM01 humanoid robot. Fig. 7 showcases the tracking performance from video input of different methods. Each baseline is trained on the same reference motion dataset used to train our method, ensuring that all methods are evaluated under a consistent embodiment. As TWIST does not have original video reconstruction module, we combine our reconstruction module with it.

## E. Real-robot Experiments

In order to further demonstrate the practical effectiveness of the proposed framework, we conduct highly dynamic realworld experiments on the physical PM01 platform. These experiments are designed to evaluate whether the learned controller can retain its motion-tracking capability beyond simulation and execute the reference behaviors reliably under real robot dynamics and sensing conditions. The results are shown in Fig. 9 and 10.

Tracking of Highly Dynamic Motions: We use Bencaogangmu (BCGM), a well-known dance, for the real-world deployment test. The RGB signal processing module first detects and extracts the human pose at 10 Hz using SAM 3D Body. GMR [46] maps the human motion to the robot configuration in real time. FLD then extrapolates the retargeted motion from 10 Hz to 50 Hz, which aligns the reference rate with the control rate. The performance of the RGB signal processing module is shown in Fig. 9-(a).

To relate the deployment motion to the structure of the reference motion library, we also record the θ values of this video-reconstructed motion sequence. The distribution of $\theta _ { B C G M }$ is showcased in Fig. 9-(b). It provides a qualitative view of this relationship rather than a quantitative measure of distribution alignment or tracking accuracy.

The θ values play an important role in the motion tracker. Fig. 9-(c) shows representative poses executed by the physical robot under the actions of our transformer-based BFM. These behaviors illustrate the reproduction of characteristic upperand lower-body configurations of the dance, including singleleg-support poses with the other leg lifted. Together, the three panels provide a qualitative demonstration of the complete video-to-robot pipeline, linking visual motion estimates and their latent representations to coordinated whole-body execution on the physical platform.

Fig. 10. Teleoperation. (a) Bi-manual pick, reorient, and place. (b) Single-arm pick, carry, and place in a 139-s continuous session. Insets show the operator, who wears no tracking device, as recorded by the third-person camera rather than the MoViC input camera.

In Fig. 10-(a), a box stands upright on a table in front of the robot, and another table stands on the robot’s left. The robot clamps the box between both hands, lifts and tilts it, turns toward the side table, and lays the box down on its side before releasing it. The clamp holds only while both hands keep pressing on opposite sides of the box, so the relative configuration of the two arms must be preserved as the torso turns. The box remains clamped throughout the transfer, indicating that the references produced by reconstruction, retargeting, and extrapolation preserve this bimanual coordination during the turn. When the box rests tilted against the edge of the side table, the operator adjusts the arms before pushing it flat. Such corrections rely on the operator’s visual feedback and on the policy following small corrective arm motions while the hands are in contact with the object.

Fig. 10-(b) shows a continuous session that a bag initially rests on a blacktop small table. The robot hooks one hand through the bag handles, lifts the bag while stepping, carries it beside the stand, and rests it on the near small table, and finally withdraws its hand from the handles. During this withdrawal, the tracked image position of the bag remains unchanged within one pixel, indicating that the hand is extracted from the handle loop without dragging the bag. Because the policy observes neither the object nor the contact forces, this load is an unmodeled disturbance that must be compensated through proprioceptive feedback alone. The robot remained balanced, and the bag stayed hooked on its hand.

Compared with the Bencaogangmu dance, these tasks involve slow, aperiodic, upper-body-dominated motions, while the lower body must stabilize the robot against object loads. Their completion suggests that the θ-conditioned tracker and the reference generation are not limited to rhythmic motions. Neither the visual reconstruction nor the policy observes the object, and monocular reconstruction provides limited depth cues for such fine alignment. Incorporating object perception or haptic feedback is therefore a natural extension of this interface.

## V. CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION

We presented MoViC, a framework for humanoid wholebody control from live monocular RGB video. The frequencydomain motion representations supply temporal context with fewer policy input features than normal design, while a single next-step target retains direct tracking guidance. This separation gives the transformer complementary information about the immediate tracking objective and the surrounding motion, providing a structured way to use temporal information in the reference library. Our extrapolation method enables 50-Hz reference generation between slower visual updates, and measurement anchoring compensates for shared reconstruction offsets. The asynchronous pipeline allows visual reconstruction and policy execution to operate at different rates. This avoids forcing the controller to wait for every visual update, while retaining an input interface based on a single RGB camera. Hardware deployment demonstrates that the framework can track highly dynamic dance motions from live video. The same tracking policy also accomplishes device-free teleoperation tasks, including bimanual and single-arm object manipulation, without task-specific training

These advantages come with trade-offs. Reference quality still depends on visual reconstruction and retargeting, and latent phase propagation cannot guarantee accurate predictions during prolonged occlusion or abrupt motion changes. Future work will focus on improving the accuracy and processing speed of video-based human motion reconstruction. We will also extend the current fixed-camera setup to support egocentric video from a camera mounted on the robot’s head, accounting for camera motion and viewpoint changes during robot movement. More motion data should also be incorporated to discover the scaling law of the frequencydomain representations. The frequency-domain representation summarizes local temporal structure in a shared feature space, providing a compact conditioning interface across diverse motion clips. This structure may facilitate the reuse of temporal features across clips. We hypothesize that such conditioning can help the tracking policy benefit more effectively from increases in the size and diversity of the motion dataset.
