# Anchor 1: Precise and Stable Humanoid Control for Whole-Body Manipulation

Archon Robotics

For detailed authorship, see Contributions section.

https://archon.tech/research/anchor1

## Abstract

Humanoid controllers have acquired increasingly diverse motor skills, yet translating these capabilities into precise and stable whole-body manipulation remains challenging. Manipulation tasks are sensitive to execution deviations – even small errors or unintended steps can cause failure. Introducing Anchor 1, a whole-body controller for precise and stable whole-body manipulation, together with a benchmark spanning diverse coordination demands. Anchor 1 adopts calibrated sparse motion references to preserve task-relevant human motion structure without online joint-space retargeting, leaving kinematic freedom for precise control. To guide this freedom towards stable, physically plausible whole-body coordination, we shape the training distribution through ofline curation of robot motions paired with these calibrated sparse references. We further introduce explicit error feedback decoupled from motion encoding, improving control precision while supporting flexible command interfaces. To achieve a systematic evaluation of these capabilities, a benchmark is proposed to assess precision, sustained execution stability, and reachability across diverse whole-body coordination demands. Evaluations in simulation and on hardware show stronger precision and stability across all evaluation tracks. Under the most strict real-world global scoring setting, Anchor 1 achieves a 30% to 80% decrease in tracking error with fewer look-ahead inputs, and without retargeting compared to all existing controllers. Downstream experiments demonstrate more eficient teleoperated data collection and higher task success using the same deployment pipeline as well. We believe Anchor 1 supports precise and stable whole-body control as a practical basis for humanoid task execution.

## 1 Introduction

Humanoid robots ofer the prospect of performing everyday tasks in environments designed around the human body [1]. Realizing this prospect requires accurate coordination of the hands, trunk, and feet as the robot moves and interacts with its surroundings. Reaching into a low shelf couples hand placement with changes in posture and balance; carrying a tray requires its pose to be maintained while the feet reposition. In both cases, small deviations at the end efectors can disrupt the task even when the robot remains upright, and an unintended step can shift the body and displace the hands from their targets. A controller for whole-body manipulation must therefore be precise, bringing the hands and feet to their intended targets, and stable, sustaining this accuracy throughout execution without falls, slips, or unintended steps. These capabilities underpin predictable teleoperation, successful demonstration collection [2, 3, 4], and reliable execution by autonomous policies [5].

Learning from human motion have enabled substantial progress in whole-body control [6, 7, 8]. Recent work demonstrates agile, natural movement and transfer across motion sources and embodiments [9, 10, 11], alongside general-purpose interfaces for interactive and autonomous control [12, 13, 14]. Task-oriented systems further demonstrate coordinated whole-body control for dynamic interactions [15, 16, 17]. In parallel, other approaches transfer egocentric human demonstrations to humanoid loco-manipulation through visual and action alignment [18, 19]. Bringing these advances to whole-body manipulation raises two fundamental requirements: task intent must be faithfully expressed in a control command, and this command must be realized through precise, stable execution. In teleoperation, the command must adapt the operator’s motion to the robot’s morphology while preserving task-relevant cues and a predictable correspondence. A common approach constructs the command as a robot-space reference through online retargeting [20, 21]; errors in this conversion can disrupt the intended correspondence or introduce discontinuities. Encoding human motion directly avoids this conversion [12], but a parametric body representation [22] that disregards the operator’s body proportions can distort task-relevant trajectory structure.

Even with a faithful command, close reproduction of the overall motion does not ensure sustained accuracy at task-relevant targets. The controller needs to correct deviations as posture and support change, without introducing unintended movements that disrupt the task. Existing controllers obtain the signal for this correction in diferent ways. Token-conditioned controllers [12] receive the intended motion as a learned token together with proprioception. This compact, structured representation can facilitate learning by higher-level policies. However, tracking-error feedback is implicit in this formulation: the policy must infer deviations from the token and proprioception, while compression obscures fine-grained target information and makes small residual errors harder to correct. By contrast, explicit tracking-error feedback provides a direct correction signal [2, 13, 23], but encoding it in the motion token would entangle intended behavior with transient execution errors, complicating token prediction and reuse by higher-level policies.

Progress toward such controllers also depends on evaluations that can verify these capabilities. Such evaluations must distinguish overall motion reproduction from sustained accuracy under manipulation-relevant conditions. Humanoid benchmarks now span whole-body task learning and the interplay between high-level policies and low-level trackers [24, 25]. Motion-tracking benchmarks have also broadened motion coverage and begun to assess contact quality and alignment with human preferences [26, 27]. Yet task success in task-level benchmarks also reflects perception and planning, whereas sequence-averaged tracking errors can obscure brief deviations. Complementary, controller-level evaluations are therefore needed to characterize how precisely targets can be reached, how consistently they can be maintained, and under which whole-body conditions execution becomes unreliable.

Here, we present Anchor 1, a humanoid controller for precise and stable whole-body manipulation. Anchor 1 constructs its commands through a structure-preserving calibration of human motion to robot scale, which retains task-relevant motion cues and a predictable correspondence without online joint-space retargeting. To focus control on the body parts that most directly shape manipulation and support, we restrict these commands to sparse motion references [13, 28, 29] that specify target poses only for the pelvis, both wrists, and both ankles. With only these poses specified, the policy can use the remaining kinematic redundancy to track them precisely. This freedom, however, also admits multiple whole-body realizations, leaving the training distribution to determine how it is used. We shape this distribution by pairing each human motion with an ofline-retargeted robot reference and curating these pairs through quality screening. The resulting data bias the learned coordination toward stable, physically plausible behavior.

To retain explicit error feedback for precise tracking while supporting flexible commands from higher-level policies, Anchor 1 accepts either sparse motion references or motion tokens [12] and decouples this feedback from motion encoding. Execution errors thus guide control without entering the motion tokens. When commands arrive as tokens, targets reconstructed from them provide the reference for this feedback. The reconstruction objective also ties each token to the intended motion, keeping the token space consistent for higher-level policies to learn.

To fairly assess these control capabilities of current whole-body controllers, we also introduce a benchmark across three complementary tracks, especially highlighting out-of-distribution whole-body manipulationrelated motions. The In-house track uses task-structured motion-capture sequences that combine upper-body actions with varied lower-body support and movement, probing coordination during reaching and hand stabilization. The Public track broadens the evaluation to diverse motion sources and behaviors, including dynamic whole-body movements. The Reachability track tests whether prescribed wrist targets can be reached and held across body configurations without foot slips or extra steps. Together, these tracks characterize sustained tracking accuracy, reachability, and stability under varied whole-body coordination demands.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we present Anchor 1, a whole-body controller that integrates structure-preserving sparse references, stability-oriented distribution curation, and decoupled error feedback for precise and stable whole-body manipulation. Second, we introduce a controller-level benchmark for diagnosing precision and stability under diverse conditions relevant to whole-body manipulation. Third, through simulation and hardware evaluations, we show that Anchor 1 improves tracking precision and execution stability over the evaluated baselines and yields more eficient teleoperated data collection and higher autonomous task success. Hardware demonstrations further cover fine end-efector contact and fas interception, as well as whole-body manipulation while kneeling, bending, or walking (Fig. 1).

## 2 Results

Anchor 1 supports accurate reaching, object-pose maintenance during body motion, and coordinated whole-body manipulation, as illustrated by the real-world demonstrations in Fig. 1.

A structured benchmark measures tracking precision, stability, and reachability through comparisons in simulation and on hardware (Secs. 2.1 to 2.3). Ablation studies and supporting experiments examine the reference representation and controller architecture (Sec. 2.4), followed by evaluations of teleoperated data collection and autonomous task execution (Sec. 2.5).

## 2.1 Benchmarking manipulation-centric whole-body control

Benchmarking whole-body manipulation requires evaluating positioning accuracy and support stability alongside general motion-tracking performance. The benchmark therefore comprises three tracks: In-house, Public, and Reachability. The In-house track systematically varies the coordination demands placed on the controller through structured motion-capture tasks. The Public track extends evaluation to external motion sources and behaviors beyond those emphasized by the in-house tasks. The Reachability track adds prescribed spatial targets and holding phases, allowing positioning performance to be examined alongside support stability. Together, these tracks assess controller performance across both diverse motion sequences and explicitly specified reaching conditions.

## 2.1.1 Benchmark composition and motion diversity

The In-house track contains 15,342 motion-capture sequences across 3,247 tasks designed to probe manipulation-related whole-body coordination. We specify torso configuration and arm-and-hand behavior separately from lower-body support and movement, then combine compatible action descriptions into motion-capture instructions. These tasks include unilateral and bimanual reaching from diferent postures, coordinated hand–foot motion, and hand stabilization during body movement. The task structure makes the intended body behavior and its lower-body context explicit, allowing related manipulation demands to be examined under diferent postural and locomotor conditions. The associated tags also allow results to be grouped by coordination demand. Tag coverage, action-tag distributions, and details of the spatial analysis are provided in Sec. S1.

The Public track contains 2,230 motion sequences selected from BONES-SEED [30], CIRCLE [31], OMOMO [32], and AMASS [33]. We select BONES-SEED and CIRCLE sequences by actor or subject identity, use the existing OMOMO test partition, and include a predefined AMASS test subset. These sequences introduce motion patterns from external datasets, including running and jumping, which are absent from the In-house track. The Public track therefore complements the manipulation-focused task composition of the In-house track by testing whether tracking capability extends to a wider range of motion sources and dynamic behaviors.

The Reachability track complements these motion-sequence evaluations with explicitly specified Cartesian wrist targets. We define fixed right-wrist targets in a world coordinate frame across five postures: standing, shallow squat, deep squat, staggered stance, and half kneeling. Each case pairs a target location with a posture. We generate base motions for each posture using Kimodo [34], then adapt them to the targets through constrained inverse kinematics subject to joint limits and prescribed support contacts. The resulting references include preparation, reaching, target holding, and return phases. Accepted references maintain a maximum wrist-position error below 1 cm relative to the prescribed target during the holding interval. After quality control and left–right mirroring, the track contains 3,210 reference motions covering reaching with either wrist. This construction provides target-conditioned references for evaluating sustained tracking across body configurations. Tracking success, foot-slip and extra-step events are recorded over the reach–hold–return task window, testing whether the controller can execute the motion while maintaining the intended support.

The coverage analyses further illustrate the complementary composition of the tracks. Joint projections of semantic and pose features provide a qualitative view of overlapping and distinct motion regions across all three tracks (Fig. 2B). Under the coordinate transformations used for the spatial analysis, the In-house references exhibit broader wrist-position coverage, particularly in the stationary subset, whereas the Public references span a larger region of pelvis displacement after initial alignment (Fig. 2C). These distributions characterize the spatial occupancy of the reference motions; the Reachability track separately specifies target locations across postures for controller assessment.

Feature-space distributions and spatial coverage of the three tracks are visualized in Fig. 2B and C. All benchmark sequences are held out from training.

## 2.1.2 Evaluation criteria

Benchmarking whole-body manipulation requires evaluation criteria that capture both tracking precision and the ability to sustain it throughout execution. Tracking precision is quantified by mean position errors, and sustained tracking by motion-level success rates (SR). Global scoring compares executed and reference trajectories after a fixed initial alignment of horizontal pelvis position and heading. Local scoring additionally removes each trajectory’s instantaneous horizontal pelvis translation while preserving height and heading diferences. Both scoring frames are independent of the controller’s input mode.

For the In-house and Public tracks, a motion is successful only if wrist and ankle position errors remain within 10 cm, their orientation errors within 1 rad, and all other tracked body-point position errors within 25 cm throughout the sequence. All motions, including failures, contribute equally to SR. Mean position errors are averaged within each motion and then across motions, using frames preceding the first position error exceeding 25 cm at any tracked body point. The resulting observation windows may difer across controllers.

For the Reachability track, task success requires global tracking without foot slips or extra steps throughout the reach–hold–return task window. Mean slip and extra-step counts per motion further characterize support stability. Full definitions, failure handling, and aggregation rules are provided in Sec. 4.4.1.

## 2.2 Tracking precision, stability, and reachability in simulation

Simulation experiments compare Anchor 1 with ScaleBFM XL [13], SONIC v1.1 [12], and MimicLite v1.1 [35] to assess tracking precision, sustained tracking success, and reachability across the three benchmark tracks (Fig. 3). Within each track, all configurations are tested on the same reference motions using the criteria in Sec. 2.1.2 and a common simulation and domain-randomization protocol (Sec. 4.4.2). References are precomputed for all tracks, with sparse robot-space references supplied to Anchor 1 on the Reachability track. The comparisons assess complete controller configurations. Controller look-ahead and online retargeting requirements are summarized in Table 1. Corresponding results in Isaac Sim are provided in Sec. S3.

On the In-house track, Anchor 1 in global mode achieves the highest local and global success rates (SR), at 88.4% and 88.7%, respectively, and the lowest mean horizontal root position error among the compared configurations, alongside low wrist and ankle position errors (Fig. 3A,B). The category-wise comparisons also show higher tracking success on household motions requiring whole-body coordination, including low-reaching and half-kneeling reaches (Fig. 3C). This advantage extends to manipulation during locomotion: during walking with asymmetric bimanual reaches, its local SR is 91.2%, compared with 57.0% for ScaleBFM in global mode, the strongest baseline in this setting.

The Public track extends evaluation to highly dynamic behaviors, including running and jumping, that are absent from the In-house track and less represented in training. The three baseline methods may have been exposed to portions of the public datasets during training. Although absolute success rates are lower on this track, Anchor 1 in global mode retains the highest local and global SR among the compared configurations (Fig. 3D). It also has the lowest horizontal root position error and lower ankle position error than all baselines (Fig. 3E). Success rates are particularly low across controllers on the AMASS test subset, indicating that its motions remain challenging under the sustained-tracking criterion (Fig. 3F). These results support whole-body tracking capability beyond the behaviors emphasized by the In-house track.

Across the In-house and Public tracks, Anchor 1 in re-locate mode retains local tracking performance close to that of global mode, with similar pelvis-relative wrist and ankle position errors (Fig. 3A,B,D,E). The preservation of local tracking accuracy supports its use in teleoperation, where re-locating references to the robot’s current root accommodates diferences between the operator’s and robot’s global trajectories.

On the Reachability track, Anchor 1 in global mode achieves the highest global task SR in most posture groups, including standing, squatting, and staggered stance (Fig. 3G). Mean foot-event counts are low, at 0.06 slips and 0.53 extra steps per motion (Fig. 3H).

## 2.3 Tracking accuracy and task success on the real robot

Real-world evaluation covers two complementary motion subsets drawn from the benchmark tracks (Sec. 2.1.1) The Reachability subset comprises standing reaches selected to broadly cover the workspace and assess end-efector tracking precision. The In-house subset contains motions selected using predefined action tags to examine performance across structured task categories.

Anchor 1 is compared with MimicLite v1.1 [35], the 5-point and 14-point configurations of ScaleBFM [13], and SONIC v1.1 [12] with G1 and human-body inputs. Both subsets report mean wrist and ankle position and orientation errors in global and local scoring frames. Body-point poses are reconstructed through forward kinematics from recorded joint configurations and motion-capture measurements of the pelvis pose. The error statistics include all valid recorded samples, without the 25 cm position-error truncation applied in simulation.

Global tracking error measures world-space deviations from the reference trajectories after a fixed initial alignment of horizontal pelvis position and heading. Local position error reduces the contribution of pelvis translation, providing a complementary assessment of limb tracking. Low local error alone does not establish accurate global execution, because some body-displacement errors are removed by the local scoring frame. Reporting both therefore distinguishes local tracking precision from accuracy along the world-space trajectory.

The In-house subset additionally reports motion-level success rate (SR), following the simulation success criteria. A motion succeeds only if the prescribed body-point position and end-efector orientation thresholds are satisfied throughout its valid recorded segment. SR is calculated within each task category as the fraction of successful motions among those with valid recordings.

## 2.3.1 Tracking precision on the Reachability subset

The Reachability subset comprises 51 standing-reach motions selected to broadly cover the wrist-target workspace and this evaluation focuses on reaching precision under stationary support.

Anchor 1 achieves the lowest global and local position and orientation errors for the right wrist and both ankles.(Table 1) Its global wrist and ankle position errors are 44.58 mm and 35.70 mm, respectively, compared with 53.59 mm and 50.40 mm for the strongest baseline, MimicLite v1.1. This improvement comes with a shorter reference look-ahead (0.06 s versus 0.08 s) and a diferent input requirement: Anchor 1 accepts calibrated sparse human references $H _ { 5 } ^ { \prime }$ directly, whereas MimicLite consumes robot motion references �, requiring online retargeting for live human input.

The comparison with SONIC-SMPL further highlights the tracking precision achievable without online retargeting. Although both controllers accept human-motion inputs without this conversion, Anchor 1 reduces global wrist and ankle position errors by 73.3% and 73.4%, respectively, while using one-third of SONIC-SMPL’s reference look-ahead. The low global errors at both the wrist and ankles are consistent with accurate reaching while maintaining stable foot placement, supporting the stable and precise whole-body coordination required for whole-body manipulation.

## 2.3.2 Task-structured evaluation on the In-house subset

The In-house subset comprises 72 motions selected using predefined action tags to represent seven task categories. ## 3 Discussion

## 3.1 Implications for humanoid whole-body manipulation

Precise and stable whole-body manipulation requires commands that preserve the intended motion and controllers that maintain accuracy as posture and support change. The reference comparisons and hardware experiments show how errors at either stage can disrupt execution. Sparse commands specify task-relevant body poses while leaving the remaining joints free to coordinate. This freedom makes the training distribution consequential: the same commanded poses can admit diferent whole-body configurations. In Anchor 1, calibrated sparse references preserve task-relevant human motion cues, while curated robot references guide whole-body coordination during training. This division retains a sparse deployment interface without requiring a complete robot joint trajectory.

The hardware comparisons show that conclusions about controller reliability depend on the precision demanded by the evaluation. Tighter position tolerances reveal diferences that permissive success criteria can obscure. Mean tracking error summarizes average accuracy but can obscure brief deviations; SR reports the fraction of motions that satisfy the prescribed criteria throughout the evaluated window. The three-track benchmark translates manipulation demands into explicit tests of precision and stability. Its task structure and prescribed spatial targets help identify the coordination conditions under which accuracy can be sustained, while the Public track tests a broader range of motion behaviors.

Feedback placement has consequences beyond tracking. Supplying explicit execution errors directly to the decoder, with feedback targets provided by sparse references or reconstructed from motion tokens, allows the same controller to correct deviations under both command interfaces. In the autonomous tasks, token commands with reconstructed-target feedback support successful execution, whereas the tested encoderfeedback variant fails when commanded through tokens. These results support separating motion commands from the explicit error signals used to execute them. These VLA-driven tasks demonstrate the use of this interface for object interaction involving locomotion and changes in posture. Precise and stable whole-body control can thus provide a common execution layer for human motion input and learned task commands.

## 3.2 Limitations

The present evaluation covers a single humanoid embodiment, leaving transfer across robot morphologies untested. Sparse kinematic targets do not explicitly describe object geometry, contact forces, or grasp stability, so accurate motion tracking alone does not ensure successful object interaction. The reconstruction branch also remains a source of tracking error, and further improvements are needed to narrow the precision gap between reconstructed and supplied feedback targets.

## 3.3 Future directions

Future work should focus on connecting whole-body tracking more directly to object interaction through object-relative targets and contact-aware control and evaluation. Testing across robot embodiments, payloads, and contact conditions would help establish the limits of transfer. For motion-token control, further work should examine how target-reconstruction errors and high-level prediction errors afect closed-loop execution, and how their impact on precision can be reduced, particularly during longer tasks with changing contacts.

## 4 Materials and methods

## 4.1 Overview of distribution curation and control

Anchor 1 combines curated motion references with a controller that separates motion encoding from explicit tracking-error feedback (Fig. 10). Human motion is geometrically calibrated to form $H ^ { \prime } .$ , from which five target poses $H _ { 5 } ^ { \prime }$ are extracted, and paired with an ofline-retargeted robot reference �. Quality screening retains corresponding pairs, shaping the robot behaviors available for learning while preserving sparse human references as the command interface.

The controller encodes the sparse references into motion tokens and combines these tokens with proprioception and tracking errors to generate joint actions. A reconstruction head supplies feedback targets when explicit pose references are unavailable, enabling a higher-level policy to command the same controller through motion tokens.

## 4.2.2 Quality control

All training motion sequences undergo automated quality control during data conversion, with original and left–right mirrored sequences screened individually. Checks on retargeted robot motions assess prolonged floating, joint discontinuities, self-collision, periodic joint jitter, and ground penetration using kinematics and collision information from the robot model. Temporal criteria account for each sequence’s sampling rate.

All in-house sequences additionally undergo human review of retargeted motion playback, with source human-skeleton animations consulted to assess motion quality and correspondence. In-house sequences are retained only if they pass both automated checks and human review; public sequences are retained if they pass automated checks.

## 4.3.3 Reachability track

We construct the Reachability track from fixed Cartesian wrist targets and corresponding whole-body reference motions across five postures: standing, shallow squat, deep squat, staggered stance, and half kneeling. Targets are specified in meters in a world coordinate frame with � forward, � leftward, and � upward. We first generate right-wrist references on a coarse 20-cm Cartesian grid and add 10-cm samples to extend spatial coverage. Each case pairs a target with a posture, so the same spatial target can occur in multiple posture groups.

We first generate base motions for each posture using Kimodo [34], then adapt these motions to the wrist targets using constrained inverse kinematics (IK), subject to joint limits and prescribed support contacts. The six wrist roll, pitch, and yaw joint angles are fixed at zero in the reference. Each reference contains 464 samples at 50 Hz and spans 0–9.26 s. The sequence includes posture preparation, a reach from 2.75 to 5 s, target holding from 5 to 7 s, and a return from 7 to 9.26 s. We require the maximum right-wrist position error relative to the target to remain below 1 cm over [5, 7) s. Tracking and foot-event evaluation use the broader task window [2.76, 9.26] s (Sec. 4.4.1).

All generated reference motions additionally undergo manual quality control (Sec. 4.2.2). We mirror the accepted references across the sagittal plane, exchanging left and right limbs and reflecting the targets to include reaching with either wrist. The final track contains 3,210 references, comprising 1,605 original motions and 1,605 mirrored counterparts.

## 4.4 Evaluation metrics and simulation settings

## 4.4.1 Metric definitions and aggregation

For the simulation tracks in Sec. 2.2, we report success rate (SR), mean position errors, and reachability foot-event counts. The global and local scoring rules, indexed by $f \in \{ \mathrm { g } , 1 \}$ , are independent of the controller’s input mode.

For one motion, let ${ \bf p } _ { b } ( t )$ be the executed position after fixed initial pelvis XY/yaw alignment and $\bar { \mathbf { p } } _ { b } ( t )$ the corresponding reference position. Here � denotes the pelvis and $t = 1 , \dots , T$ indexes control frames at $\Delta t = 0 . 0 2 \mathrm { ~ s ~ }$ . Both scores use the same reference trajectory; local error subtracts each trajectory’s current pelvis XY position when computing the diference. With $\Pi _ { x y } = \mathrm { d i a g } ( 1 , 1 , 0 )$ , the errors are

$$
\begin{array}{l} e _ {b} ^ {\mathrm{g}} (t) = \left\| \mathbf {p} _ {b} (t) - \bar {\mathbf {p}} _ {b} (t) \right\| _ {2}, \\ e _ {b} ^ {1} (t) = \left\| \left[ \mathbf {p} _ {b} (t) - \Pi_ {x y} \mathbf {p} _ {r} (t) \right] - \left[ \bar {\mathbf {p}} _ {b} (t) - \Pi_ {x y} \bar {\mathbf {p}} _ {r} (t) \right] \right\| _ {2}, \\ \theta_ {b} (t) = \arccos \left(\frac {\operatorname{tr} \left[ \bar {\mathbf {R}} _ {b} (t) ^ {\top} \mathbf {R} _ {b} (t) \right] - 1}{2}\right). \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

Here $\mathbf { R } _ { b }$ is the executed orientation after the same fixed yaw alignment and $\bar { \mathbf { R } } _ { b }$ the reference orientation; both scores use the same orientation error. Errors compare the robot state after each control step with the reference at the same time, preserving height and heading diferences.

Let B denote the tracked body points and $\varepsilon$ the two wrists and two ankles. For a set of control frames $\mathcal { U } .$ success requires all three threshold conditions:

$$

$$

$$
\mathbf {S R} ^ {f} = \frac {1 0 0}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} s _ {i} ^ {f} (\{1, \ldots , T _ {i} \})\tag{%) .}
$$

The indicator 1[·] is one when all enclosed conditions hold and zero otherwise; subscript � identifies a motion when aggregating over the dataset. Each of the � motions contributes equally, and failed motions remain in the denominator.

Mean position errors use a separate failure rule: any of the 14 tracked body points exceeding 25 cm ends the observation window, independently of the SR conditions. This more permissive criterion reduces premature truncation under the stricter SR conditions, retaining more frames to characterize tracking errors and improving temporal coverage for comparisons across controllers. For each motion and scoring frame, we average position errors over the selected body points and all frames preceding the first threshold violation, excluding the violating frame. The full motion is used if no violation occurs. These per-motion means are then averaged with equal weight across motions and reported in millimeters; motions without retained frames are excluded. Whole-body error averages all 14 tracked points, while wrist and ankle errors average the corresponding left and right points in the local scoring frame. Root error averages the Euclidean distance between the executed and reference pelvis positions in the horizontal plane, using the global scoring frame and its corresponding observation window. It uses the same equal-motion aggregation as the other position errors.

For the Reachability track, we measure Slip and Extra step over the task window defined in Sec. 4.3.3. Slip counts sustained sliding of a supporting foot, with at most one event per continuous contact phase. Extra step counts foot-lift events that begin within the task window while the reference requires continuous ground contact throughout the event. Both metrics sum events from the two feet and are averaged equally across the 3,210 references. Foot-event counts use the complete task window even when tracking fails. Reachability task SR requires global tracking success throughout the task window under Eq. (2), with no slips or extra steps.

## 4.5 Sparse human references and decoupled error feedback

## 4.5.1 Sparse motion references and offline distribution curation

Human and robot skeletons difer in body proportions and local coordinate conventions. We account for these diferences by calibrating human motion against the robot’s T-pose to construct a structure-preserving skeletal representation $H ^ { \prime }$ at robot scale. The calibration uses human and robot T-pose references to determine segment- and body-specific scale factors, rotational alignments, and positional ofsets, without solving for robot joint configurations. These parameters remain fixed for each calibrated skeleton and are applied frame by frame to obtain the positions and orientations of the corresponding links.

From $H ^ { \prime }$ , we extract the positions and orientations of the pelvis, both wrists, and both ankles to form the five-pose sparse motion references $H _ { 5 } ^ { \prime }$ . This interface is designed to focus control on end-efector precision and sustained pose tracking, which are central to many whole-body manipulation tasks, while retaining flexibility in whole-body coordination. Leaving the remaining joint configuration unspecified preserves kinematic redundancy, allowing the policy to adjust the trunk and limbs to meet the commanded poses. Execution therefore requires learned whole-body motion completion: the policy must infer coordinated behavior from the five-pose references. Because these references admit multiple realizations, the behavior distribution used for training is an essential part of this design. We strictly curate the paired $H ^ { \prime }$ and robot references � to favor stable, physically plausible motions, introducing a deliberate bias toward the whole-body behaviors preferred for whole-body manipulation. This data selection guides how the policy uses the freedom aforded by sparse commands.

For training, each $H ^ { \prime }$ sequence is paired with a temporally aligned robot reference � obtained through ofline retargeting of the same human motion [38]. The sparse references $H _ { 5 } ^ { \prime }$ provide the motion input, whereas � provides robot motion targets for training supervision and reward computation. We curate the paired sequences through automated quality checks and additional manual review of in-house data, as described in Sec. 4.2.2, to favor stable and coherent whole-body behavior. At deployment, the controller uses $H _ { 5 } ^ { \prime }$ directly without requiring online retargeting to �, avoiding exposure to the solver failures illustrated in Fig. 6. This also removes the computational overhead and latency associated with online retargeting.

## 4.5.2 Motion encoding, target prediction, and error feedback

Explicit tracking-error feedback helps the controller correct deviations during execution [2, 13, 23]. However, the same motion reference can produce diferent tracking errors depending on the robot’s current state. Encoding these errors into the motion token couples the representation of the command to its ongoing execution, potentially complicating token interpolation and prediction by higher-level policies. We therefore separate explicit tracking-error feedback from motion encoding within an encoder–quantizer–decoder architecture (Fig. 10B), building on the motion-token formulation of SONIC [12]. At control time �, we construct the encoder input $x _ { t }$ from the global references $H _ { 5 } ^ { \prime }$ at ofsets of 0, 20, 40, and 60 ms, expressing the target poses in the current robot-root frame. The motion encoder � compresses this window, and a finite scalar quantizer � produces the motion token $z _ { t } = Q ( E ( x _ { t } ) )$ ). Explicit wrist and ankle tracking errors are excluded from the encoder input and supplied separately to the control decoder alongside proprioception.

A trajectory head � decodes $z _ { t }$ into near-term pose reconstructions and longer-horizon motion predictions. The near-term output reconstructs the five target poses at the four input times, whereas the future output predicts changes relative to the first target pose at longer horizons. A target selector uses either the supplied near-term references or their reconstructions to form the control feedback. The future predictions serve only as an auxiliary training objective and are not passed to the control decoder.

We compute current wrist and ankle poses by forward kinematics from measured joint positions. For each near-term target, we compute the position error in the current robot-root frame and the relative orientation error between the target and measured poses to form the feedback $e _ { t }$ . The control decoder � combines this feedback with the token and proprioceptive observations $o _ { t }$ :

$$
a _ {t} = D (z _ {t}, o _ {t}, e _ {t}).\tag{3}
$$

Here $a _ { t }$ denotes the joint action and $o _ { t }$ includes robot state history. The token therefore reaches the decoder directly, while explicit execution-error correction enters through a separate path. When commands are supplied as motion tokens, reconstructed targets provide the feedback needed for closed-loop execution without an accompanying pose-reference sequence.

## 4.5.3 Policy training and auxiliary objectives

We train the controller in simulation using proximal policy optimization (PPO) [39] on the curated motion pairs. The actor is conditioned on $H _ { 5 } ^ { \prime } .$ , and reference-tracking rewards are evaluated against the paired robot motion �. To support feedback from both supplied references and token reconstructions, we train the shared control decoder with both sources of near-term targets. In the reconstructed-target branch, policy gradients propagate through the tracking-error computation to the reconstruction head, allowing its outputs to be optimized for control as well as pose reconstruction. Measured poses and supplied reference targets are treated as constants in this computation.

The motion representation is trained with near-term reconstruction, future prediction, and cycle-consistency objectives in addition to PPO:

$$
\mathcal {L} = \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{PPO}} + \lambda_ {\text {near}} \mathcal {L} _ {\text {near}} + \lambda_ {\text {future}} \mathcal {L} _ {\text {future}} + \lambda_ {\text {cycle}} \mathcal {L} _ {\text {cycle}}.\tag{4}
$$

Near-term and future supervision comes from the calibrated sparse human references $H _ { 5 } ^ { \prime } .$ , rather than the robot reference �. The near-term objective penalizes pose-reconstruction errors, and the future objective predicts positional and rotational changes beyond the input window. Following [12], we re-encode the reconstructed near-term sparse poses with explicit tracking-error channels masked and align their bounded continuous latent with a stop-gradient latent from the original reference window to enforce cycle consistency. These auxiliary objectives use a clean reference encoding with shared encoder, quantizer, and trajectory-head parameters.

## 4.5.4 Controller deployment

At deployment, Anchor 1 exposes two command interfaces that share the same trained control decoder (Fig. 10C). The sparse-reference interface accepts five target poses, which are encoded into motion tokens. For teleoperation, these poses are obtained from motion data from motion-capture systems or virtual reality (VR) tracking devices through skeletal calibration and five-pose extraction. Higher-level policies can use either interface, generating five-pose sparse motion references for the encoder or supplying motion tokens directly to bypass it. In the latter case, the trajectory head reconstructs the near-term targets needed to compute tracking errors. In both cases, the decoder combines motion tokens with proprioception and explicit tracking-error feedback to generate joint actions, without requiring the paired robot training reference �.

During training, the reference motion retains its global trajectory. At inference time, global mode preserves this trajectory after an initial position and heading alignment and requires robot-root localization. Alternatively, we apply re-locate, which treats the robot’s current root position as having reached the previous reference frame’s root position. We align these two positions by translating the entire reference window:

$$
\widetilde {\mathbf {p}} _ {i} (t + \tau) = \mathbf {p} _ {i} ^ {\mathrm{ref}} (t + \tau) + \left[ \mathbf {p} _ {\mathrm{root}} ^ {\mathrm{robot}} (t) - \mathbf {p} _ {\mathrm{root}} ^ {\mathrm{ref}} (t - \Delta t) \right].\tag{5}
$$

Here � indexes the five target bodies, � is the current reference time, and $\tau \in \{ 0 , \Delta t , 2 \Delta t , 3 \Delta t \}$ indexes the time ofsets in the reference window, with $\Delta t = 2 0 \mathrm { m s }$ . The root denotes the pelvis, and the first reference frame serves as the anchor at the start of a sequence. The same translation is applied to all five targets and all times in the window, preserving their relative geometry and the reference displacement from the previous frame to the current frame. Target orientations remain unchanged. This alignment is applied before motion encoding and is separate from the T-pose calibration of skeletal dimensions. Both inference modes use the same trained controller without additional optimization.

## S1 Benchmark coverage and composition

We define eligible In-house track tasks using upper-body and lower-body action descriptions to specify manipulation-related coordination demands. Sit–stand tasks are excluded because the benchmark lacks a unified quantitative evaluation protocol for seated configurations and transitions between support contacts. Their tags are retained in the reference set to make this scope limitation explicit in the coverage analysis.

For each eligible task, we select up to five motion-capture sequences that pass the same automated screening and human review procedures used for the in-house training data (Sec. 4.2.2). We retain all qualifying sequences when fewer than five are available and record tasks with none as unrepresented. We assess the resulting track using the released action tags and compatible pairs of first-level and second-level tags.

## S1.1 Tag and combination coverage

Tag coverage is the fraction of reference tags represented in an evaluation set. Pair coverage is the fraction of compatible pairs of first-level and second-level tags represented within the upper-body or lower-body description. Let T denote the reference tag set and $\mathcal { P } _ { b }$ the valid pair set for body description $b \in \{ U , L \}$ where � and � denote upper and lower body. For an evaluation set D, let $\mathcal { T } ( \mathcal { D } )$ and $\mathcal { P } _ { b } ( \mathcal { D } )$ denote the distinct tags and jointly represented pairs of first-level and second-level tags identified in its annotations. Coverage is defined as

$$
\begin{array}{l} C _ {\mathrm{tag}} (\mathcal {D}) = \frac {| \mathcal {T} (\mathcal {D}) \cap \mathcal {T} |}{| \mathcal {T} |}, \\ C _ {\mathrm{pair}} ^ {b} (\mathcal {D}) = \frac {| \mathcal {P} _ {b} (\mathcal {D}) \cap \mathcal {P} _ {b} |}{| \mathcal {P} _ {b} |}, \qquad b \in \{U, L \}, \\ C _ {\mathrm{pair}} (\mathcal {D}) = \frac {\sum_ {b \in \{U , L \}} | \mathcal {P} _ {b} (\mathcal {D}) \cap \mathcal {P} _ {b} |}{\sum_ {b \in \{U , L \}} | \mathcal {P} _ {b} |}. \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

Here, $| \mathcal { T } | = 4 3 , | \mathcal { P } _ { U } | = 3 3$ , and $| \mathcal { P } _ { L } | = 2 0$ . Overall pair coverage pools the covered counts across the 53 valid pairs, weighting each pair equally. Coverage values are reported as percentages. These measures characterize coverage relative to the predefined tag and pair sets.

The In-house track covers 39 of the 43 reference tags (90.70%; Table S1). The four uncovered tags concern torso coordination during sit–stand transitions (U1-8), sit–stand transitions (L1-6), floor sit–stand transitions (L2-18), and chair sit–stand transitions (L2-19). It also covers 30 of 33 upper-body pairs (90.91%) and 18 of 20 lower-body pairs (90.00%), yielding an overall pair coverage of 48 of 53 (90.57%).

## S1.3 Spatial coverage of benchmark tracks

Under the coordinate transformations specified below, the in-house references show broader wrist-position coverage, particularly in the stationary subset (Fig. 2C). The public references span a larger region of pelvis displacement after initial alignment. These observations describe empirical occupancy of reference motions.

Positions are computed by forward kinematics of the G1 robot model, with X forward, Y left, and Z upward. For upper-body workspace, we compute both wrist-yaw link origins with pelvis position and orientation fixed, retaining waist and arm motion. For lower-body workspace, we compute both ankle-roll link origins with pelvis position and orientation fixed. For stationary manipulation, we remove instantaneous pelvis translation and yaw while retaining roll and pitch before computing wrist-link origins. For locomotion coverage, we align pelvis trajectories for moving motions by their initial horizontal position and heading, preserving subsequent displacement and absolute height. The upper-body, lower-body, and stationary-manipulation views include a constant vertical ofset of 0.78 m for visualization only.

## S2 Reference fidelity evaluation

We compare wrist trajectories extracted from $H ^ { \prime }$ and the input representation used by SONIC’s SMPL encoder [12], using the same human motion as a reference before motion encoding (Fig. 5). For the SMPL input, we first obtain the corresponding joint rotations from the human motion and then apply forward kinematics on a standard SMPL body to reconstruct the whole-body kinematics supplied to the encoder. Inspired by the comparison of pairwise motion vectors for temporal consistency in STaR [41], we define unit-normalized pairwise displacement-vector discrepancy (unit-PDV) to quantify directional diferences between the human reference and each encoder input.

Let $\rho \in \{ H , H ^ { \prime } , S \}$ index the human reference $H , H ^ { \prime }$ , and the SMPL encoder input �, respectively, and let $T \in \{ H ^ { \prime } , S \}$ index the two representations being compared. We express wrist positions in common human chest axes and normalize by each representation’s own fixed arm length:

$$
\mathbf {q} ^ {\rho} (t) = \frac {\mathbf {C} _ {H} (t) ^ {\top} [ \mathbf {p} _ {\mathrm{wrist}} ^ {\rho} (t) - \mathbf {p} _ {\mathrm{shoulder}} ^ {\rho} (t) ]}{L _ {\rho}}.\tag{9}
$$

Here $\mathbf { C } _ { H }$ contains the human chest basis vectors, $\mathbf { p } _ { \mathrm { s h o u l d e r } } ^ { \rho }$ is the representation-specific shoulder origin, and $L _ { \rho }$ is the fixed arm length.

Using matched start and end times, we compute and normalize the displacement vectors as follows:

$$
\mathbf {d} _ {i j} ^ {\rho} = \mathbf {q} ^ {\rho} (t _ {j}) - \mathbf {q} ^ {\rho} (t _ {i}), \quad u (\mathbf {d}) = \frac {\mathbf {d}}{\max (\| \mathbf {d} \| , \epsilon)}.\tag{10}
$$

Here $\epsilon > 0$ stabilizes direction normalization.

The human displacement determines a weight shared by both encoder inputs, yielding the pairwise directional error $e _ { i j } ^ { T } .$ :

$$
w _ {i j} ^ {H} = \frac {\| \mathbf {d} _ {i j} ^ {H} \| ^ {2}}{\| \mathbf {d} _ {i j} ^ {H} \| ^ {2} + \tau^ {2}}, \qquad e _ {i j} ^ {T} = w _ {i j} ^ {H} \left\| u (\mathbf {d} _ {i j} ^ {T}) - u (\mathbf {d} _ {i j} ^ {H}) \right\| ^ {2}.\tag{11}
$$

Here $\tau > 0$ controls the downweighting of small human displacements.

Consider a window of � samples starting at frame � and a nonempty set of temporal lags $K \subseteq \left\{ 1 , \ldots , N { - } 1 \right\}$ For each lag $k \in K$ , the within-window ofset � identifies a displacement pair spanning frames $s + i$ and $s + i + k$ . We define the unit-PDV score $E _ { s } ^ { T }$ by averaging errors over all valid time-matched pairs within each lag and then averaging the lag-specific means equally:

$$
E _ {s} ^ {T} = \frac {1}{| K |} \sum_ {k \in K} \left[ \frac {1}{N - k} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {N - k - 1} e _ {s + i, s + i + k} ^ {T} \right].\tag{12}
$$

For visualization in Fig. 5A, each normalized trajectory is centered by subtracting its mean position over the plotted interval, and the front-view axes use equal scales. Panel B plots the unit-PDV scores $\overline { { E _ { s } ^ { H ^ { \prime } } } }$ and $E _ { s } ^ { S }$ at the window centers, with a dashed line indicating zero discrepancy. The mean scores for this motion are $1 . 4 0 3 7 7 3 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ and $7 . 1 0 3 1 1 2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ , respectively. Both overlays in panel C use the same human reference, sample times, and coordinate scales. The example pairs $t _ { 1 } \to t _ { n }$ and $t _ { 2 }  t _ { 3 }$ illustrate displacement vectors matched using identical start and end times.
