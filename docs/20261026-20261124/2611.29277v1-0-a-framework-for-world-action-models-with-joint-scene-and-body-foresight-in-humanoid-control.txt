## Abstract

World Action Models anticipate visual outcomes to guide robot actions, yet humanoid interaction also depends on how the robot’s body responds. Visualforesight alone can miss body motion, while latent-distance losses do not directly supervise action responses. We present ζ , aframework that turns vision-language-action policies into World Action Models byforeseeingfuture visualfeatures and measuredjoint trajectories in a shared latent representation. During training, a teacher conditioned on recorded futures provides a reference action response, and dual-response transfer trains the causal predictor to match it through the teacher and the executing policy. All components are trained jointly, and deployment uses only causally predicted futures. On seven HumanoidArena tasks, $\zeta _ { 0 }$ built on $\Psi _ { 0 }$ and GR00TN1.7 reaches 63.6% and 79.5% mean success, 18.8 and 9.0 points above these bases. On six real-world tasks, it reaches 78.3% and 85.0%, gains of 16.7 and 10.0 points. On GR00T, its 79.5% tops all compared seven-task joint policies. Project page: https://chenyizhi123.github.io/zeta0-project/.

## 1. Introduction

Humanoid interaction couples whole-body motion with changes in the surrounding scene. Pushing a box, for example, moves the object while the arms keep contact and the legs and torso support the motion. World Action Models couple action generation with anticipated future observations [28, 33, 46, 50, 51, 56]. For a humanoid, the consequences of an action also include its own body motion, which egocentric images may capture only partially and measured joint trajectories record directly. We therefore ask a policy to foresee scene and body changes jointly.

Demonstrations provide this foresight as supervision: each action is followed by recorded images and measured body states. At execution time, those futures have not yet occurred and must be predicted from causal inputs. A common approach fits future predictions to recorded observations or their latent representations [21, 23, 56]. Latent-distance supervision, however, does not directly reflect how a policy uses the prediction: nearby futures need not induce the same action, and equally sized errors can have different effects on behavior. We therefore also supervise prediction in action space, asking whether a predicted future induces the action that the recorded future would.

We introduce $\zeta _ { 0 } ,$ , a framework that turns a visionlanguage-action policy into a World Action Model with joint scene-and-body foresight for humanoid control, illustrated in Fig. 1. During training, a future encoder summarizes subsequent visual features and measured body trajectories into a shared temporal latent representation, and a causal predictor learns to estimate it from inputs available at execution time. The representation, predictor, and action models are trained jointly; at deployment, the policy acts on the predicted future and current observations.

To supervise prediction in action space, we introduce dual-response transfer. A teacher learns to generate actions conditioned on recorded futures, providing a reference action response, and we train the predictor so that predicted futures induce this response through two readers. The teacher term asks the prediction to carry the action-relevant information the teacher extracts from recorded futures; the executingpolicy term asks for the same response from the reader used at deployment.

We make three contributions:

• Joint scene-and-body foresight framework: $\zeta _ { 0 }$ turns vision-language-action policies into World Action Models.

• Dual-response transfer: a training objective that supervises causal prediction through the responses of a recordedfuture teacher and the executing policy.

• Consistent gains in simulation and the real world: $\zeta _ { 0 }$ built on $\Psi _ { 0 }$ and GR00T N1.7 raises mean success over each base by 18.8 and 9.0 points on seven HumanoidArena tasks and by 16.7 and 10.0 points on six real-world tasks.

## 2. Related Work

Whole-body humanoid learning. Whole-body humanoid learning studies coordinated locomotion, posture, and manipulation, with hierarchical control separating high-level decisions from low-level execution [9, 13]. Tracking controllers such as ExBody, ExBody2, HOVER, and SONIC learn to execute reference motions or end-effector targets across the body [14, 26, 30, 39], and ASAP aligns simulated and realworld physics to learn agile whole-body skills [25]. Teleoperation systems collect coordinated demonstrations [24, 53], while HumanPlus and 3D diffusion policies connect visual observations to autonomous humanoid skills [17, 54]. HumanoidBench evaluates locomotion and manipulation across simulated tasks [45], and HumanoidArena emphasizes egocentric hierarchical whole-body learning [47]. These directions provide motion-control interfaces and demonstrationbased learning settings for humanoid interaction; $\zeta _ { 0 }$ builds on such an interface and asks how a high-level policy can anticipate the consequences of its own commands.

Vision-language-action policies. Recent VLA work adapts pretrained vision-language representations to robot control using demonstration data [6, 11, 31]. Building on large-scale robot transformers [7], generalist policies such as Octo and the π series learn across tasks and embodiments [4, 42, 44]. Action generation has developed alongside representation learning, with action chunking [55] and diffusion- or flow-based generators [4, 15] providing interfaces for continuous robot commands. Receding-horizon execution refreshes the observation context between action chunks, combining multi-step generation with feedback during control [15]. For humanoids, GR00T N1, $\Psi _ { 0 } .$ , and HuGe combine pretrained vision-language backbones with action generators [38, 41, 49], and Kimodo-Policy learns kinematic action models for loco-manipulation [48]. These policies provide foundations for studying how additional predictive information can condition action generation.

World Action Models. World models learn how an environment responds to actions, either to learn behaviors by imagining latent trajectories [21–23] or as generative and predictive models trained on large video corpora [2, 8, 40]. Text-guided video generation can serve directly as a policy [16], and frozen self-supervised visual features can serve as world-model states for planning [57]. In feature-space planning, candidate action sequences are optimized so that predicted future features approach those of a goal observation [57]. Predictive policies couple action learning with future visual prediction [28, 46, 50] or jointly model future observations and actions [34, 58]. FLARE aligns policy features with future visual latents [56], DIAL separates latent intent prediction from execution [12], and ARPS learns predictive states from future features and their changes [20]. Being-M0.7 jointly models visual and motion futures, then trains an action expert on a frozen, robot-adapted prior [52]. Most of these methods represent the future by images or visual features and supervise prediction by reconstruction or latent distance. $\zeta _ { 0 }$ foresees the scene and measured body trajectories in one shared latent representation and supervises its causal prediction in action space, keeping the predictor coupled to action learning throughout training.

Privileged information and action supervision. Learning with privileged information transfers guidance from training-only observations to deployable policies [10, 32]. Knowledge distillation matches a student’s outputs to a teacher’s and thereby updates the student’s parameters [27]. Action-space constraints provide another form of supervision: cross-view action consistency matches flow velocities across action-equivalent observations [29]. Dual-response transfer in Sec. 3.5 also compares action responses, but it holds the readers’ parameters fixed and updates the predictor only through the readers’ input, the predicted future.

## 3. Method

$\zeta _ { 0 }$ is a framework that turns a base vision-language-action policy into a World Action Model by adding joint scene-andbody foresight, a causal prediction of how the scene and the robot’s body will change over a short future horizon. The base supplies the vision-language backbone, the state and action interfaces, and, where available, pretrained weights. It adds a shared latent representation of future scene and body changes, predicts it from causal inputs, and conditions action generation on the prediction. We instantiate $\zeta _ { 0 }$ on $\Psi _ { 0 }$ and GR00T N1.7, denoting the resulting models by $\zeta _ { 0 } ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ and $\zeta _ { 0 } ~ ( \mathrm { G R 0 0 T } )$ , as set up in Sec. 4.1. Sections $3 . 2 \mathrm { - } 3 . 6$ describe the representation, its causal and world prediction, action learning, dual-response transfer, and joint optimization and deployment; Fig. 2 separates training from deployment.

## 3.1. Problem formulation

At time t, let $\boldsymbol { x } _ { t } = \left( o _ { t } , s _ { t } , \ell _ { t } \right)$ contain the current image observation, measured state, and instruction. Let $p _ { t }$ be the prefix of an action chunk already committed by the controller [5]. A training demonstration additionally provides an action chunk $a _ { t }$ and a continuation $y _ { t }$ of future visual features and body measurements, with timestamps and validity masks. We divide this continuation into K temporal groups ending at increasing horizons. Each group contains a visual target and body measurements at timestamped query points; the body target is a trajectory rather than a single joint vector at the group endpoint.

The base policy’s vision-language backbone extracts current visual-language context and visual features for future supervision. Although both may use joint coordinates, the measured body target differs from the commanded action: the action is a target for the low-level controller, whereas the measured trajectory records how the body actually moves under contact, balance, and tracking dynamics, which is the consequence the policy must anticipate.

## 3.2. Learning scene-and-body futures

An online encoder $E _ { \phi }$ maps the current visual and body context $x _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { v b } }$ and the recorded continuation to $\boldsymbol { z } _ { t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { K \times N \times \breve { d } }$ with N tokens of width d per group:

$$
z _ {t} = E _ {\phi} (x _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{vb}}, y _ {t}), \qquad z _ {t} ^ {\star} = \mathrm{sg} \left(E _ {\bar {\phi}} (x _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{vb}}, y _ {t})\right).\tag{1}
$$

The target parameters $\bar { \phi }$ track ϕ by exponential moving average (EMA), as in self-supervised predictive learning [1, 19]. Neither encoder reads the instruction or demonstrated actions; both summarize only the recorded consequences. The teacher defined below therefore receives privileged information that is available only in training [10, 32]; its action loss updates the online encoder to make these consequences useful for action generation.

A readout D takes the latent and current visual features and reconstructs future scene features and measured body trajectories. Write $\ell _ { s } ( z ; D )$ and $\ell _ { b } ( z ; D )$ for their masked mean squared errors. The temporal term $\ell _ { \Delta } ( z ; D )$ sums scene and body errors on differences between adjacent groups, using only valid corresponding endpoints. We define

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{rec}} (z; D) = \ell_ {s} (z; D) + \ell_ {b} (z; D) + \lambda_ {\Delta} \ell_ {\Delta} (z; D),\tag{2}
$$

where $\lambda _ { \Delta }$ weights the temporal term. This supervision preserves both the state of the interaction and its temporal change.

## 3.3. Shared causal and world prediction

A conditioner H encodes the causal context as $h _ { t } = H ( x _ { t } )$ A shared deterministic predictor $F _ { \theta }$ predicts both modes:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \widehat {z} _ {t} ^ {\text {prior}} = F _ {\theta} (h _ {t}, p _ {t}; \text {prior}), \\ \widehat {z} _ {t} ^ {\text {world}} = F _ {\theta} (h _ {t}, a _ {t}; \text {world}). \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

The prior mode uses the committed prefix; the world mode additionally uses demonstrated future actions. Both modes share parameters and predict all temporal groups in parallel. The prior mode predicts what will happen from information available at decision time and is the mode used at deployment; the world mode predicts what happens under given future actions, an action-conditioned dynamics problem with less ambiguity. Sharing parameters lets the prior predictor reuse the dynamics learned in world mode, and Sec. 4.3 shows that removing the world latent MSE lowers success on both bases.

For mode $m ,$ , we minimize

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{pred}} ^ {m} = \lambda_ {m} \| \widehat {z} _ {t} ^ {m} - z _ {t} ^ {\star} \| _ {M _ {m}} ^ {2} + \lambda_ {D} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{rec}} (\widehat {z} _ {t} ^ {m}; D _ {\mathrm{fix}}),\tag{4}
$$

where $\lambda _ { m }$ and $\lambda _ { D }$ are loss weights and $\| \cdot \| _ { M _ { m } } ^ { 2 }$ is a masked mean squared error. The readout parameters are detached for this prediction loss, while derivatives with respect to its latent input remain active. Thus $D$ provides a common measurement of recorded and predicted futures. It is trained separately by Eq. (2) on online-encoder latents.

## 3.4. Action learning from recorded and predicted futures

We use distinct flow-matching [36, 37] action models: teacher $T _ { \psi }$ and executing policy $G _ { \omega }$ . For a normalized demonstration ${ \widetilde { a } } ,$ Gaussian noise $\epsilon ,$ and a sampled flow time $\tau \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ , the uncommitted action suffix is

$$
a _ {\tau} = (1 - \tau) \widetilde {a} + \tau \epsilon , \qquad v ^ {\star} = \epsilon - \widetilde {a}.\tag{5}
$$

Committed prefix entries remain clean, have flow time zero, and are excluded from the loss. Let $X = \left( a _ { \tau } , \tau , x _ { t } , p _ { t } \right)$ and let $M _ { a }$ select valid, uncommitted action entries. The flow-matching action objectives are

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\mathcal {L} _ {T} = \mathbb {E} \| T _ {\psi} (X, z _ {t}) - v ^ {\star} \| _ {M _ {a}} ^ {2},} \\ & {\mathcal {L} _ {G} = \mathbb {E} \| G _ {\omega} (X, \widehat {z} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{prior}}) - v ^ {\star} \| _ {M _ {a}} ^ {2}.} \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

## 3.5. Dual-response transfer

The teacher learns to generate actions from recorded futures. Dual-response transfer uses this learned action mapping to supervise causal prediction, asking predicted futures to induce the teacher’s recorded-future response. For one shared flow sample $X$ , the teacher conditioned on the EMA future defines

$$
v _ {\text { ref }} = \text { sg } \left(T _ {\psi} (X, z _ {t} ^ {\star})\right).\tag{7}
$$

Writing $\widehat { z } = \widehat { z } _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { p r i o r } }$ , we apply

$$
\begin{array}{r} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{resp}} = \frac {1}{2} \| T _ {\mathrm{fix}} (X, \widehat {z}) - v _ {\mathrm{ref}} \| _ {M _ {r}} ^ {2} \\ + \frac {1}{2} \| G _ {\mathrm{fix}} (X, \widehat {z}) - v _ {\mathrm{ref}} \| _ {M _ {r}} ^ {2}, \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

where $M _ { r }$ combines valid action-suffix entries with availability of future supervision. The subscript “fix” detaches reader parameters and buffers within this objective; it does not detach z. All other response inputs and the reference are detached, so this loss updates the prediction path through zb.

The teacher term asks the same reader to interpret the prediction as it interprets the recorded future. The executingpolicy term asks for that response in the reader used at deployment. These are different constraints: even if $\widehat { z } = z _ { t } ^ { \star }$ the second residual need not vanish because $G$ and $T$ are different models. The objective thus supervises prediction in action space, complementing the latent MSE of Eq. (4) rather than measuring latent prediction error alone. The flowmatching action objectives in Eq. (6) continue to update both readers; “fix” applies only within response transfer.

## 3.6. Joint optimization and deployment

During joint learning, forecasting alternates between prior and world updates. The executing policy always trains on a causal prior prediction, including on world-forecast updates. Each update uses

$$
\begin{array}{c} \mathcal {L} = \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{rec}} (z _ {t}; D) + \mathcal {L} _ {T} + \lambda_ {\mathrm{reg}} \mathcal {R} (z _ {t}) + \mathcal {L} _ {G} \\ + \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{pred}} ^ {m} + \mathbf {1} [ m = \mathrm{prior} ] \lambda_ {r} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{resp}}, \end{array}\tag{9}
$$

where $\lambda _ { r }$ and $\lambda _ { \mathrm { r e g } }$ are loss weights. The regularizer bounds latent scale and penalizes collapse of tokens within a group. The vision-language backbone is frozen. Appendix A specifies initialization, masking rules, and parameter updates.

At deployment, we compute $F ( H ( x _ { t } ) , p _ { t } ; \mathrm { p r i o r } )$ once per action chunk, then integrate G from noise at $\tau = 1$ to actions at $\tau = 0$ , keeping the committed prefix fixed. The predictor is deterministic; stochasticity enters action sampling. The future encoder, EMA encoder, readout, teacher, and world-mode action labels are absent from deployment.

## 4. Experiments

We evaluate $\zeta _ { 0 }$ built on $\Psi _ { 0 }$ [49] and on GR00T N1.7, a later release of GR00T N1 [41]. On seven HumanoidArena [47] tasks, we compare $\zeta _ { 0 }$ on each base with that base policy and include ACT [55] and $\pi _ { 0 . 5 }$ [44] as additional baselines, supplemented by published seven-task joint-policy results. Component ablations on both bases examine the shared future representation and its coupling to action generation. We then measure body forecasts with $\zeta _ { 0 } ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ and evaluate both $\zeta _ { 0 }$ models on six real-world tasks.

## 4.1. Experimental setup

Benchmark and data. We use the Unitree G1 setup in HumanoidArena and seven tasks: double desk, football, pick and place box, boxing, open door, sit sofa, and visual naviga tion. Each task contributes 100 demonstrations, giving 700 demonstrations in total. Each policy trained in our experiments is one shared model trained on all seven tasks with uniform task sampling and global batch size 512. In these runs, policies consume current egocentric visual context, a 64-dimensional state, an instruction, and the committed action prefix, and output 40-dimensional semantic actions. Future-body supervision in our models comprises 29 mea sured joints: 14 arm, 12 leg, and three waist coordinates.

Instantiations. $\zeta _ { 0 } ~ ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ uses the frozen Qwen3-VL-2B vision-language backbone [3] released with $\Psi _ { 0 }$ and randomly initializes the $\zeta _ { 0 }$ task modules, whereas the $\Psi _ { 0 }$ baseline starts from its full pretrained checkpoint. $\zeta _ { 0 }$ (GR00T) and the GR00T N1.7 baseline both start from the pretrained GR00T N1.7 checkpoint; in $\zeta _ { 0 }$ (GR00T), the teacher and executing policy are copies of its native diffusiontransformer [43] action head, and the predicted future enters its cross-attention as additional context tokens. All seventask policies we train, including ablations and baselines, use 40,000 updates and are evaluated at the final checkpoint; $\pi _ { 0 . 5 }$ and $\Psi _ { 0 }$ start from their pretrained checkpoints. In all experiments, execution uses a 30-step planning window at 50 Hz: the controller executes 24 steps and retains the last six as the committed prefix of the next chunk, corresponding to 0.6, 0.48, and 0.12 s. The forecast covers this window in $K = 3$ groups ending at 0.2, 0.4, and 0.6 s, with $z _ { t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 3 \times 8 \times 2 5 6 }$ . Loss weights are $\lambda _ { \Delta } = \lambda _ { D } = 0 . 2 5$ $\lambda _ { \mathrm { p r i o r } } = \lambda _ { \mathrm { w o r l d } } = \lambda _ { r } = 1$ , and $\lambda _ { \mathrm { r e g } } = 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ . Optimizer and latent settings are listed in Appendix A.

Closed-loop evaluation. We use scenario seeds 0, 1, and 2 with 20 episodes per seed, giving 60 episodes per task and 420 per setting. All policies in our runs share the benchmark environments, success predicates, episode limits, and SONIC low-level controller [39], and chunked execution with a committed action prefix, detailed in Appendix A.5. We report per-task success and the equal-weight mean. Scenario seeds vary evaluation conditions, and each of our settings uses one training seed.

## 4.2. Enhancing different base policies

Table 1 shows that $\zeta _ { 0 }$ improves both bases. $\zeta _ { 0 } ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ reaches 63.6% mean success versus 44.8% for $\Psi _ { 0 }$ , an 18.8-point gain; it improves six of seven tasks, most on sit sofa, open door, and pick and place box, by 33.3, 30.0, and 23.3 points. $\zeta _ { 0 }$ (GR00T) reaches 79.5% versus 70.5% for GR00T N1.7, a 9.0-point gain; it improves six of seven tasks, most on navigation, football, and boxing, by 20.0, 18.3, and 13.3 points, while open door stays near ceiling at 96.7%. This is the highest reported seven-task mean in Table 1. $\zeta _ { 0 }$ (GR00T) also has the highest success of any policy in Table 1 on pick and place box, boxing, and navigation, at 96.7, 88.3, and 78.3%. $\zeta _ { 0 } ~ ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ exceeds ACT and $\pi _ { 0 . 5 }$ by 11.4 and 12.6 points and outperforms both on six of seven tasks.

## 4.3. Component analysis

Table 2 removes one component at a time on both bases: future body measurements (BF), dual-response transfer (DR) and its executing-policy and teacher terms (ER, TR), the action gradient into prediction (AG), future conditioning of G (FC), and the world latent MSE (WL). Every ablation lowers the seven-task mean on both bases; per-task results are in Appendix C.

Acting on predicted futures. Zeroing G’s future condition in training and deployment lowers the mean by 16.4 points on $\Psi _ { 0 }$ and 6.2 on GR00T N1.7, confirming on both bases that the executing policy acts on its foresight. On $\Psi _ { 0 }$ it lowers success on six of seven tasks, most on pick and place box, by 23.3 points.

Body futures. Keeping only visual futures, without future body inputs or supervision, lowers the mean by 6.4 points on GR00T N1.7, the largest ablation drop on this base, and by 2.1 on $\Psi _ { 0 }$ . On GR00T N1.7, the drop concentrates on tasks with object contact and whole-body coordination: football, pick and place box, boxing, and double desk each lose 10.0– 13.3 points, whereas open door and sit sofa do not decrease. In these tasks the body’s response to contact can be difficult to read from egocentric images.

The effects of body supervision and future conditioning also differ by task. On GR00T N1.7, removing body futures lowers box success by 11.7 points, compared with 1.7 points when future conditioning is removed; on navigation, the corresponding drops are 5.0 and 20.0 points. Body supervision is more influential on box, while access to the predicted latent is more influential on navigation.

Dual-response transfer. Removing dual-response transfer lowers the mean by 5.5 points on $\Psi _ { 0 }$ and 4.5 on GR00T N1.7; keeping only one reader at the same total coefficient leaves the mean 1.9–4.3 points below the full model. Removing the teacher term lowers the mean by 2.6 and 4.3 points on Ψ<sub>0</sub> and GR00T N1.7, more than the 1.9 and 2.4 points for the executing-policy term, and removing both costs more than removing either, suggesting that the two readers supply partly complementary supervision.

The single-reader controls have different task profiles despite the same total response weight. For $\zeta _ { 0 } ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ , teacheronly supervision obtains 88.3% on box and 55.0% on boxing, compared with 71.7% and 58.3% for executing-policy-only supervision. On GR00T N1.7, the corresponding boxing rates are 80.0% and 85.0%. Teacher-only supervision has the higher mean on both bases, while executing-policy-only supervision performs better on boxing.

Other components. Blocking the executing-policy action gradient into prediction lowers the mean by 4.0 and 2.6 points on $\Psi _ { 0 }$ and GR00T N1.7, and removing the world latent MSE by 1.7 and 4.8 points.

The per-task results in Appendix C show where these losses differ. On $\zeta _ { 0 } ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ , blocking the action-loss gradient reduces box and boxing success by 21.7 and 16.7 points; removing the world latent MSE lowers sit-sofa success by 16.7 points. On GR00T N1.7, removing the world latent MSE reduces football, box, and navigation by 13.3, 10.0, and 11.7 points, whereas blocking the action-loss gradient mainly affects navigation, by 11.7 points, and leaves boxing, open door, and sit sofa unchanged.

## 4.5. Real-world experiments

Setup. We compare $\zeta _ { 0 } ~ ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ and $\zeta _ { 0 }$ (GR00T) with their respective bases and $\pi _ { 0 . 5 }$ on six Unitree G1 tasks: hair drying, pushing a chair, clearing a table, carrying tape around an obstacle, placing a doll, and tidying a scene, shown in Fig. 4. The tasks place different demands on the policy: hair drying requires sustained arm motion near a person; pushing a chair and placing a doll combine walking with grasping; carrying tape around an obstacle couples carrying with obstacle-aware locomotion; and table clearing and scene tidying chain several object interactions in one episode. All five policies share three RGB views, measured body-andhand state, the action interface, the SONIC controller, and the execution protocol, and each is trained separately on each task with the same demonstrations and 20,000 updates. Each policy is evaluated for 10 trials per task with randomized robot and object positions; failures and timeouts count as unsuccessful, and Appendix B lists demonstrations, success criteria, and per-task counts.

Results. $\zeta _ { 0 } ~ ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ reaches 78.3% mean success versus 61.7% for $\Psi _ { 0 }$ and 50.0% for $\pi _ { 0 . 5 } .$ , and $\zeta _ { 0 }$ (GR00T) reaches 85.0% versus 75.0% for GR00T N1.7, gains of 16.7 and 10.0 points over the bases, as Fig. 5 shows. The gains concentrate on table clearing, obstacle avoidance, and scene tidying, three long-horizon tasks in which several stages must succeed in sequence: $\zeta _ { 0 } ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ succeeds in 19 of their 30 trials versus 11 for $\Psi _ { 0 } ,$ and $\zeta _ { 0 }$ (GR00T) in 23 versus 19 for GR00T N1.7. For $\zeta _ { 0 } ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ , scene tidying rises from 20 to 60% and table clearing from 50 to 80%; for $\zeta _ { 0 } ~ ( \mathrm { G R 0 0 T } )$ table clearing rises from $^ { 7 0 }$ to 90%; on the other three tasks, every policy succeeds in 8 to 10 of 10 trials. We observed that small deviations in the relative placement of the body and objects tended to accumulate from stage to stage in baseline trials until a later stage failed, whereas $\zeta _ { 0 }$ executed more stably across stages. $\zeta _ { 0 }$ (GR00T) matches or exceeds the other four policies on every task; Fig. 1(a) shows one of its scene-tidying episodes.

Model size and latency. The deployed $\zeta _ { 0 } ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ has 2.59B parameters, of which 2.13B form the frozen backbone and 461M the task modules. On one RTX 4090, a policy call takes 212 ms on average and 221 ms at the 95th percentile, using 6.3 GiB, excluding sensing, networking, and control; measurement conditions are in Appendix A.5.

## 5. Conclusion

$\zeta _ { 0 }$ is a framework that turns vision-language-action policies into World Action Models by learning joint scene-and-body foresight together with them. A shared latent representation captures future visual features and measured body trajectories, and dual-response transfer supervises its causal prediction in action space through a recorded-future teacher and the executing policy. Built on $\Psi _ { 0 }$ and GR00T N1.7, $\zeta _ { 0 }$ raises mean success over both bases by 18.8 and 9.0 points on seven HumanoidArena tasks and by 16.7 and 10.0 points on six real-world tasks with 10 trials each. Removing future conditioning causes the largest drop on $\Psi _ { 0 } ,$ , whereas removing body futures causes the largest drop on GR00T N1.7; dual-response transfer improves both bases. Foreseeing the body as well as the scene, and judging that foresight by the actions it induces, lets a policy anticipate the consequences of its own whole-body commands.

Future work. Pretraining the shared scene-and-body representation on large, diverse humanoid and human-motion data offers a direct path to broaden foresight across tasks and embodiments; combining it with explicit planning or language-level reasoning over longer task structure is a natural next step. Dual-response transfer could also supervise other predictive signals, such as future touch or force.

## A. Implementation Details

This appendix gives the initialization, losses and parameter updates, architecture and compute, action execution, and simulation-baseline settings of $\zeta _ { 0 }$ on both bases, followed by real-robot details, per-task ablation results, and bodyforecast evaluation details.

## A.1. Initialization and vision-language backbone

In $\zeta _ { 0 } ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ , all task modules, in simulation and on the real robot, are randomly initialized. Its simulation and real-robot versions use the Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct vision-language model released with $\Psi _ { 0 } ,$ , frozen throughout. Each camera image keeps its aspect ratio, is resized with bilinear antialiasing to a short side of at most 240 pixels without upscaling, and is padded symmetrically with RGB value 127 to a multiple of 32; pixels are rescaled by $1 / 2 5 5$ and normalized with mean and standard deviation 0.5, and the backbone’s own resizing is disabled. Each image yields at most 160 merged visual tokens, which the backbone’s context sequence keeps unchanged. The current-visual branch and the future visual targets use post-merger features averaged over $\mathbf { a } \ 4 \times 4$ grid with area weights over the valid image region, giving 16 tokens of 2048 dimensions per image; pure padding receives zero weight (Fig. S1a).

In simulation, $\zeta _ { 0 }$ (GR00T) is built inside the native GR00T N1.7 architecture. It keeps the frozen Qwen-based vision-language backbone of the GR00T N1.7 checkpoint (16 text layers, 2048-dimensional context) and its flowmatching DiT action head (32 layers, preceded by four vision-language self-attention layers). The DiT, visionlanguage normalization and self-attention, and position embeddings load from the checkpoint; a new embodimentspecific state encoder, action encoder, and action decoder handle the 64-dimensional state and 40-dimensional semantic actions. The teacher $T$ is initialized from this head, and the executing policy $G$ starts as an independent copy with separate parameters; the future encoders, readout, conditioner, predictor, and future projections are newly initialized. The three latent groups of eight tokens are projected to the native context width and appended to the vision-language context of the DiT cross-attention, with one masked copy for the image-attention blocks and one for the text-attention blocks; the upstream attention code is unchanged. ## A.2. Losses, masks, and parameter updates

Reconstruction errors are averaged over valid elements per sample and combined with their loss weights; body errors are additionally weighted per joint. Temporal scene differences require valid visual targets in consecutive groups. Body differences additionally require valid corresponding query slots in the two groups. World prediction uses a cumulative action-coverage mask: a missing action segment invalidates the affected group and subsequent world groups. This eligibility rule applies to world losses; the example remains available for ordinary policy learning when its own targets are valid.

The latent regularizer is

$$
\mathcal {R} (z) = \frac {1}{| \mathcal {V} |} \sum_ {g \in \mathcal {V}} \left([ r _ {g} - 4 ] _ {+} ^ {2} + [ 0. 1 - d _ {g} ] _ {+} ^ {2}\right),\tag{S1}
$$

where V is the set of valid groups, $r _ { g }$ is the root mean square across that group’s latent entries, and $d _ { g }$ is the mean channelwise standard deviation across its eight tokens. This penalizes excessive scale and identical tokens.

Table S3 summarizes parameter updates during joint learning. The online encoder receives reconstruction and teacher-action gradients. EMA targets are stop-gradient labels. Forecast readout and response losses hold their readers fixed while retaining input derivatives. The executing-policy flow-matching loss updates both G and its causal prediction path.

## A.3. Joint training algorithm

Algorithm S1 summarizes joint training, with parameter gradients following Table S3. Prior and world forecasting alternate by optimizer update; the executing policy uses the causal prior prediction in both modes. The EMA encoder advances once at the start of each update, before target encoding, including when gradients are accumulated over multiple microbatches.

## A.5. Action sampling and execution

In the action-flow convention of Sec. 3.4, clean data is at time zero and Gaussian noise at time one, with velocity target equal to noise minus the normalized action. $\zeta _ { 0 } ~ ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ integrates backward from one to zero in ten steps; $\zeta _ { 0 }$ (GR00T) in simulation uses four Euler steps in its native time convention, as described above. A committed prefix remains clean throughout sampling and is excluded from the action loss. The predictor estimates the future latent once per chunk; it has no flow-time input (Fig. S1b).

In HumanoidArena, the policy plans 30 action steps, executes 24, and keeps the last six steps of the preceding chunk as the committed prefix.

The real-robot latency of $\zeta _ { 0 } ~ ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ in Sec. 4.5 was measured after the trials on one RTX 4090 with batch 1, three 640 × 480 views, a BF16 backbone, and ten flow steps. One call generates a 50-step chunk, from which execution takes a 30-step window; it takes 212 ms on average (p95 221 ms) with no prefix and 216 ms with a 15-step committed prefix (deployment uses six), using 6.3 GiB, which fits well within the 480 ms (24-step) execution window under receding-horizon execution.

ACT, $\pi _ { 0 . 5 } ,$ and $\Psi _ { 0 }$ are trained jointly on the same 700 demonstrations for 40,000 updates with global batch 512 and are evaluated at their final checkpoints under the common protocol. $\pi _ { 0 . 5 }$ and $\Psi _ { 0 }$ start from their pretrained checkpoints, and $\pi _ { 0 . 5 }$ is fine-tuned with all parameters. ## B. Real-Robot Details

All five policies use a Unitree G1 with two dexterous hands, current snapshots from three RGB cameras, and a 41-dimensional measured state containing 29 body joints and 12 hand joints. They output a 76-dimensional action of 64 whole-body motion commands for SONIC and 12 hand commands. Execution follows the simulation protocol: plan 30 steps, execute 24, and keep the last six as the committed prefix of the next chunk. Policies read only observations available at decision time; recorded committed actions serve only as training labels. Each policy is trained independently for each task on the same demonstrations, using a global batch of 512 and 20,000 updates, and is evaluated at its final checkpoint. Architecture and base-policy initialization follow the respective methods. Both $\zeta _ { 0 }$ models use the full dual-response objective (teacher and executingpolicy response weights 0.5 each), body auxiliary weight 1, and temporal weight 0.25; future-body supervision uses 29 measured body joints. Timing gaps in the recordings split episodes into continuous segments, and future supervision does not cross segment boundaries. In experiments involving people, informed consent was obtained for participation and for the use of their images in the paper. Identifiable faces in the experimental images are obscured. ## D. Body-Forecast Evaluation

The body-forecast statistics in Sec. 4.4 use one closed-loop rollout per task of the shared $\zeta _ { 0 } ( \Psi _ { 0 } )$ checkpoint, fixed before inference, with an anchor every 0.1 s over its first 8 ${ \bf S } ,$ giving 526 windows (the boxing rollout ends earlier). Each window forecasts 30 steps at 50 Hz (0.02–0.60 s, or 10 query steps per 0.2 s group) across the 29 measured body joints (14 arm, 12 leg, and 3 waist joints). The scene panels of Fig. 3 are separate windows selected post hoc for display and are not used in these statistics.

Figure S5 and Table S10 report the closed-loop joint trajectories and error breakdowns across all seven tasks and three limb groups. Along continuous rollouts, the causal forecasts anticipate gait peaks in navigation left-knee flexion (10.4<sup>◦</sup> RMSE versus 30.4<sup>◦</sup> for holding the anchor pose), hip swing during football kicking (5.1<sup>◦</sup> versus 17.8<sup>◦</sup>), and rightelbow extension in double-desk manipulation (7.2<sup>◦</sup> versus

12.3<sup>◦</sup>; Fig. S5a–c).

Although the lower limbs are frequently outside the head camera’s field of view or occluded by manipulated objects (Fig. S5d), the 12 leg joints incur the largest pose-persistence error across the 526 windows (10.34<sup>◦</sup> pooled and 12.54<sup>◦</sup> at +0.6 s) and gain the largest absolute error reduction from $\zeta _ { 0 }$ (4.35<sup>◦</sup> pooled and 5.23<sup>◦</sup> at +0.6 s; Fig. S5e). Across all seven tasks, $\zeta _ { 0 }$ lowers pooled RMSE and wins 83.46% (439/526) of +0.6 s windows (Fig. S5f, Table S10); pick and place box has the smallest drift (5.45<sup>◦</sup>, endpoint win 50.62%), yet still drops to 4.11<sup>◦</sup>.

Limitations. Each simulation setting uses one training seed (420 episodes), so 1–2 pp ablation gaps should be read alongside consistent cross-base trends. Real-world models train per task (10 trials each), body forecasts omit root motion, and the 212 ms latency excludes perception, networking, and SONIC.
