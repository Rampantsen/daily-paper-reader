Abstract— We study humanoid navigation across multiple
destinations under long-horizon language instructions. This
task is challenging because robots must maintain progress
across successive goals while continuously producing natural,
stable whole-body motion through confined spaces. However,
task decomposition in existing methods does not by itself
ensure progress throughout execution, while waypoint-based
navigation does not guarantee natural, route-faithful whole-
body motion. Our key idea is to organize navigation into
three complementary roles operating at different time scales:
a Brain for task reasoning, a Cerebellum for local motion
generation, and a Brainstem for whole-body execution. We
realize this idea in LHNAV, an asynchronous system that
uses progress and arrival feedback to coordinate navigation
goals, VLA motion-token prediction, and whole-body control.
To train the Cerebellum for natural, route-faithful motion,
we develop a physics-grounded data engine that improves
physical tracking of motion references along collision-aware
routes and pairs the resulting demonstrations with rendered
visual observations. Together, these designs allow task planning
to proceed alongside continuous movement and extend single-
goal navigation to successive destinations with natural, route-
faithful whole-body motion. In simulation, our navigation VLA
outperforms OmniNav and DualVLN in single-goal success
and motion smoothness, with fewer collisions and falls. Using
a VLA trained only on single-goal demonstrations, LHNAV

I. INTRODUCTION
Humanoid robots assisting people in everyday environments
need to follow long-horizon language instructions that involve
multiple destinations and task dependencies [1], [2]. For ex-
ample, preparing a meal may require visiting the refrigerator,
chopping board, stove, and dining table in an appropriate order.
In this work, we focus on the navigation required by such
instructions, where a humanoid must infer navigation goals
and maintain progress across destinations without step-by-step
guidance. This setting requires both task-level coordination
across successive goals and natural, stable whole-body motion
along long routes through confined spaces.
Long-horizon humanoid navigation is challenging because
task progress must remain consistent with continuous execu-
tion across multiple destinations. Existing methods couple
navigation decisions to reasoning [3]–[5], while asynchronous
and hierarchical approaches allow task planning and action
to proceed at different time scales [2], [6]–[8]. However,
separating these processes does not itself ensure coherent
task progression: goal transitions must reflect actual execution

progress while navigation continues. A second challenge is
reconciling route constraints with natural, stable whole-body
motion, particularly in confined spaces. Waypoints, trajecto-
ries, and velocity commands describe intended movement but
leave its whole-body realization to downstream controllers [2],
[7], [9], so a collision-free route does not directly guarantee
collision-free physical execution. Recent methods introduce
richer body-level actions and multi-stage behaviors [10]–[13],
yet maintaining natural motion while limiting position and
heading errors over long, confined routes remains challenging.
We present LHNAV, combining asynchronous navigation
with a physics-grounded data engine for long-horizon hu-
manoid navigation. Inspired by the human brain, it comprises
three layers: a Brain that maintains task context and dispatches
goals, a Cerebellum that predicts motion tokens with auxiliary
waypoint-based geometric supervision, and a Brainstem
that executes whole-body motion through a frozen SONIC
decoder [14]. Progress and arrival feedback coordinate succes-
sive goals, allowing a single-goal policy to execute navigation
while task planning proceeds asynchronously. Our data engine
uses ARDY [15] to generate natural motion references along
collision-aware routes and trains the SONIC encoder with
additional reference-root and velocity information for accurate
tracking through the frozen decoder. Rendering closed-
loop rollouts in reconfigurable Gaussian–mesh scenes [16]
pairs visual observations with synchronized motion-token
targets for VLA training. Together, asynchronous coordination
and physically executed supervision support natural, route-
faithful whole-body navigation across multiple goals. Joint
training on these simulated navigation demonstrations and
real teleoperated manipulation data further enables navigation
followed by tabletop pushing on Unitree G1.
Our contributions are threefold:
1) An asynchronous multirate system integrating task
reasoning, motion-token prediction, and whole-body
execution, using progress-aware transitions that extend a
single-goal policy to long-horizon humanoid navigation.
2) A scalable, physics-grounded data engine that im-
proves global motion-reference tracking through a
privileged encoder and frozen SONIC decoder, gen-
erating execution-aligned navigation demonstrations in
Gaussian–mesh scenes.
3) Experimental validation demonstrating higher naviga-
tion success in unseen simulation scenes, smoother
motion, fewer collisions, and faster long-horizon com-
pletion, alongside real-world single-goal navigation and
zero-shot long-horizon navigation.
II. RELATED WORK
From navigation commands to whole-body motion.
Navigation policies typically output motion primitives [17],
metric commands [18], [19], or local waypoints and trajecto-
ries [9], [20], [21]. While natural for wheeled robots, these
interfaces rely on downstream controllers for actuation and,
on humanoids, whole-body coordination. Richer interfaces
expose body targets: LEGS combines wrist poses with base
commands [13], FetchMan uses base height and upper-body

joint targets [12], and VLK predicts root-and-joint trajectories
with contacts [11]. Others encode motion as pose tokens [10],
learned latents [22], or FSQ tokens [14]. SONIC decodes such
tokens with proprioceptive feedback and supports direct VLA
prediction. We adopt this interface for navigation, motivating
supervision aligned with executed humanoid motion.
Physics-grounded navigation supervision. Navigation
data draw on instruction–route datasets [23], simulators [24],
Gaussian scene representations [16], [25], and geometric
experts that synthesize routes [21], [26]. These provide visual
context and geometric routes, but humanoid supervision must
also specify how the body executes them. VLK synthesizes
whole-body kinematics in reconstructed scenes [11], while
LEGS and FetchMan collect control commands from physical
rollouts [12], [13]. Tracking reveals failures and route
deviations missed by kinematic references; LEGS therefore
retains only successful episodes [13]. Our data engine targets
global route fidelity and pairs rendered executed states with
the post-FSQ tokens consumed during rollout, matching the
deployment action interface.
Reasoning and execution for long-horizon tasks. Long-
horizon navigation couples semantic planning with local
execution. Fig. 2 summarizes representative strategies. Fanta-
syVLN uses reasoning supervision only during training [5],
whereas NavGPT and CL-CoTNav reason online before
acting [3], [27]. OctoNav and AwareVLN reduce overhead by
adapting reasoning frequency or switching between reasoning
and action modes [4], [28]. OmniNav connects planning and
execution through subgoals [29], while DualVLN enables
asynchronous action prediction from cached goals and current
observations [6]; TIC-VLA further models latency and egomo-
tion [7]. Task coordination also requires execution feedback:
SAGE-Nav updates semantic waypoints upon completion or
deadlock [8], while Uni-LaViRA, Being-0, and HoloAgent-
0 organize multistage execution through subgoal or skill
interfaces [2], [30], [31]. We use asynchronous execution,
with short instructions and target images linking task planning
to single-goal navigation. Arrival verification and progress
feedback guide advancement and recovery; motion tokens
drive high-rate proprioceptive whole-body control.

III. METHOD
We consider a G1 humanoid robot operating in a scene with
a catalog of reference images depicting candidate destinations.
Given a long-horizon language instruction, LHNAV selects
the required destinations from this catalog and navigates
the robot to them in sequence. As shown in Fig. 3, the
Brain decomposes the instruction into navigation subtasks
(Section III-A), the Cerebellum predicts local motion tokens,
and the Brainstem decodes them into whole-body actions
(Section III-B). All three layers execute asynchronously.
A. Brain: Agent with Tools
The Brain coordinates long-horizon navigation through a
planning agent with task-management and arrival-verification
tools, tracking progress, revising the remaining plan, and
deciding when to advance.
The planning agent receives the full instruction and
target catalog, together with task-decomposition and tool-use
guidance and recent observations, decisions, and execution
feedback. It can be implemented with GPT-6 or Qwen3.8-
27B. The task management tool (Tool 1 in Fig. 3) maintains
an explicit plan of completed, active, and pending navigation
subtasks. The agent uses this tool to create the plan and revise
pending subtasks during execution using new observations,
task updates, and execution feedback. Revisions can remove
goals that are no longer needed, while preserving completed
visits and the active goal. The arrival verification tool provides
goal-completion feedback through an independent Qwen
VLM, which checks arrival at up to 1 Hz using the active
subtask, target reference image, and temporal observations.
Diagnostic analyses and subtask-specific arrival judgments are
written to a shared buffer for the planning agent to access. At a
navigation stop, the runtime harness blocks new VLA requests
and discards outstanding predictions. It records completion
after verified arrival, motion-plan exhaustion, and odometry-
confirmed root stability, then dispatches the next subtask from
the updated plan following the external stage-completion
event. At each stage, the Brain supplies the Cerebellum
VLA with a simple textual instruction specifying the active
navigation goal, such as “go to the table.”

Given a navigation subtask from the Brain, the Cerebellum
uses a humanoid VLA to predict whole-body motion tokens
from visual observations and proprioception. The Brainstem
decodes these tokens into joint targets for execution.

IV. HUMANOID NAVIGATION DATA ENGINE
We design a physics-grounded data engine for training
and evaluating humanoid navigation policies. As shown in
Fig. 4, it converts target-conditioned routes into physically
executed demonstrations that provide visual observations,
motion tokens, and waypoint labels.
A. Scene and Route Construction
Generating visual navigation demonstrations requires en-
vironments that support realistic rendering. For training, we
use 100 photorealistic scenes from InteriorGS [25] as the
scene backgrounds. To plan routes within these environments,
we manually annotate a background mesh (BgMesh) that
identifies where the robot can move. To vary target and
obstacle layouts, we augment the Gaussian backgrounds with
movable foreground meshes (FgMesh), forming GS+Mesh
scenes. Route planning then uses the traversable region deter-
mined by BgMesh and the current foreground configuration.
Target-conditioned navigation also requires specifying
where the robot should stop and how it should face the target.
We annotate target bounding boxes and facing directions,
then select an accessible approach pose outside each target’s
bounding box, prioritizing its front side and orienting the robot
toward it. To generate routes from different initial conditions,
we randomly sample a start position and heading in the same
connected traversable region and connect the start to the
approach pose using 2D A*. To maintain obstacle clearance
and avoid abrupt changes in direction, we refine each route
for clearance, smoothness, and length. This produces a target-
conditioned navigation path connecting the sampled start
position to the approach position, together with the initial
and terminal headings.
B. Route-Conditioned Motion Generation and Tracking
Given a route, we use the ARDY motion generator [15]
to synthesize a whole-body reference motion at 50 Hz.
Throughout generation, the prompt is “A person is walking.”
Soft path and heading constraints and target standing poses at
both endpoints guide route following and motion transitions.
Physical tracking errors can cause executed demonstrations
to deviate from their planned routes. We therefore improve
global reference tracking to obtain route-faithful demonstra-
tions for navigation-policy training. SONIC [14] encodes
future reference motion into quantized motion latents and
decodes them with robot proprioception into joint actions.

reduces the proportion of executed path length outside the
traversable region from 3.318% to 1.841% and joint-position
jerk by 44.7%, compared with guiding SONIC along the
same routes using velocity commands toward a look-ahead
point. This yields smoother navigation demonstrations with
fewer traversable-region violations.
C. Execution-Aligned Rendering and Supervision
We replay recorded robot states to render first-person
observations using the robot-mounted camera configuration.
For GS+Mesh scenes, the Gaussian background and FgMesh
are rendered from the same camera and composited using
depth. Each episode shares an instruction and target image
across its observation and proprioception sequences. At each
observation, supervision includes a temporal motion-token
chunk and waypoints sampled by path distance from the
executed trajectory, expressed in the current robot frame.

A. Experimental Setup
a) Datasets: We select 100 training scenes and 20 held-
out test scenes from InteriorGS [25]. Our data engine produces
185,401 single-goal training demonstrations, and we evaluate
all methods on 522 episodes with identical starts and goals
in the held-out scenes. For long-horizon experiments, we use
20 scenes from the training split, with 14,410 single-goal
demonstrations for policy training and 45 evaluation tasks
requiring at least three ordered goal visits.
b) Training Settings: For single-goal evaluation, we fine-
tune OmniNav [29] and DualVLN [6] from their released
checkpoints, and our Navigation Cerebellum from pretrained
GR00T N1.7 weights [32], on the same 100-scene dataset. For
long-horizon evaluation, we train a separate Cerebellum from
the same initialization using single-goal demonstrations from
the 20-scene subset. Zero-shot denotes execution of long-
horizon instructions unseen during navigation-policy training
on single-goal demonstrations. All navigation policies are
trained for 100K steps with a global batch size of 64 on eight
NVIDIA H20 GPUs.
c) Evaluation Settings: All methods use SONIC [14]
for whole-body control. Motion-token execution does not
require odometry-based trajectory tracking. Under the same
execution setting, waypoint-based policies issue velocity com-
mands along the initial direction of each predicted trajectory,
refreshed with every new prediction. The commanded speed
is 0.6 m/s, matching the walking speed used by ARDY during
data collection.
d) Hardware Setup: We deploy on a Unitree G1 with
a forward-facing Orbbec Gemini335L camera secured to the
head using a custom 3D-printed mount (Fig. 6). The camera
provides egocentric RGB observations for task reasoning,
arrival verification, and local navigation.

VI. CONCLUSION
We presented LHNAV, an asynchronous framework for
long-horizon humanoid navigation. Task-progress manage-
ment and arrival verification coordinate successive goals,
while a motion-token VLA and a frozen whole-body decoder
support whole-body navigation. A physics-grounded data
engine supplies route-conditioned demonstrations with aligned
visual observations and humanoid action supervision. Simu-
lation experiments show improved goal reaching, smoother
motion, and fewer collisions and falls, while asynchronous
coordination extends policies trained on single-goal demon-
strations to long-horizon tasks. Deployment on Unitree G1
further demonstrates household single-goal navigation, zero-
shot long-horizon navigation, and single-goal navigation under
object-layout changes.
