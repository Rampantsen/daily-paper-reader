Abstract— Humanoid motion interfaces should accommodate
application-specific movement constraints while retaining useful
reference behavior. Local content redaction requires a controller
to replace selected movements even when it continues to receive
the original reference. Removing the tracking incentive for those
movements leaves their replacement unspecified. We introduce
RedirectMimic, which separates the reference observed by the
actor from the target used to train its execution. A rule-
generated edited reference guides tracking rewards during
proximal policy optimization, while the deployed observation
and action interfaces remain unchanged. We evaluate pose-
and speed-defined arm editing in Unitree G1 simulation using
a tracker pretrained on 7,509 motion clips. On the overhead-
arm task, RedirectMimic reduces core-window content occur-
rence to 2.7%, compared with 58.9% for reward zeroing and
32.8% for anti-tracking. It achieves 94.2% core-window joint
success, combining content suppression, fall-free execution,
and local retention. On the speed-defined task, fast outward
arm movements occurred at similarly low rates under all
three local editing methods. Across both tasks, RedirectMimic
achieved the lowest replacement error among these methods
and tracked unedited motions with accuracy comparable to
that of the pretrained controller. Project page: https://
xiaopeng-whu.github.io/RedirectMimic/.
I. INTRODUCTION
Motion tracking provides a practical interface between hu-
man demonstrations and humanoid control, including whole-
body teleoperation and data collection [1], [2]. Physics-based
imitation methods learn diverse motor skills from reference
trajectories [3], [4], [5], and large motion collections broaden
the behaviors available to a controller [6]. Faithful reproduc-
tion, however, is not always the desired deployment behavior.
A reference may contain an arm movement that a particular
application excludes, embedded within otherwise acceptable
locomotion or gestures. This calls for selective editing that
replaces the specified movement while retaining the rest of
the motion.
Figure 1 illustrates this local objective. The controller must
execute a suitable replacement while maintaining balance
and the surrounding movement. Arm motion and balance are
dynamically coupled: a local joint edit can affect torso and
leg execution even when their reference coordinates remain
unchanged. Useful redaction therefore combines content sup-
pression, replacement fidelity, and physical retention.
Recent work studies selective behavior modification in
humanoid policies [7] and motion generators [8], [9], as well
as learned policy redirection [10]. Local humanoid editing
couples this objective to the preservation of surrounding
physical behavior. The resulting control question is how
a tracker can learn a chosen replacement while its input
continues to request the original movement.
We address this question with RedirectMimic. An offline
rule constructs a paired reference (m, em), preserving the

The central distinction is between suppressing a movement
and specifying its replacement. Reward zeroing removes the
incentive to track the selected arm, whereas anti-tracking
rewards deviation from it. Neither objective specifies where
the arm should go. In G1 simulations of pose- and speed-
defined edits, the arm followed the prescribed replacement
more closely when that trajectory guided training.
Our contributions are threefold:
• Local redaction under original-reference inputs. We
formulate selective motion replacement as policy edit-
ing, requiring the controller to retain surrounding mo-
tion while its input continues to specify the original
behavior.
• Paired-reference policy fine-tuning. We introduce
RedirectMimic, combining rule-generated tracking tar-
gets and edit-window sampling to train local replace-

II. RELATED WORK
A. Humanoid Tracking and Physical Feasibility
DeepMimic establishes reinforcement-learning imitation
of reference skills [3], while AMP learns motion-style
rewards from demonstrations [12]. Perpetual Humanoid
Control extends imitation and recovery across large mo-
tion libraries [13]. Recent robot systems pursue expres-
sive upper-body imitation with locomotion in ExBody [14],
and more general whole-body tracking in ExBody2 [15].
GMT combines adaptive sampling with motion-specialized
experts [16]. SONIC studies scaling motion data, model
capacity, and compute [17]. HOVER distills multiple con-
trol modes into one policy [18], while TWIST combines
reinforcement learning and behavior cloning for coordinated
imitation [19]. A recent empirical study examines design
choices for general motion tracking [20]. BeyondMimic
provides the tracking formulation underlying our mjlab im-
plementation [4], [21]. Our study modifies selected local
responses of an existing tracker.
Reference fidelity can conflict with physical feasibility.
ASAP adapts policies to simulation–reality discrepancies
using a learned residual action model [22]. AMO com-
bines trajectory optimization and reinforcement learning for
adaptive control [23], while OmniTrack generates physically
consistent references before training a general tracker [24].
PGMT relaxes tracking to accommodate terrain [25]. These
approaches motivate validating a replacement dynamically.
Here the reason to deviate is a fixed content rule, and the
actor must continue observing the unedited reference.
B. Motion Completion and Local Editing
MoMask supports text-conditioned temporal inpainting
through masked motion modeling [26]. CondMDI supports
sparse and partial keyframe constraints [27], while Dart-
Control generates motion autoregressively with text and
spatial control [28]. MaskedMimic formulates physically
simulated character control as motion inpainting from partial
descriptions [29]. CLoSD closes the loop between a diffusion
planner and a tracking controller [30]. Our input–target
split brings local replacement into policy fine-tuning: the
actor learns to execute the edited behavior from the original
reference.
C. Behavior Suppression and Policy Redirection
ForgetMimic suppresses designated humanoid motions
while retaining other motions [7]. Local redaction addi-
tionally requires preservation within the targeted motion.
Behavior Uncloning encodes redirection in policy weights
without inference-time steering [10]. For local humanoid
editing, the desired response must specify both the affected
body parts and their replacement trajectory.
Human Motion Unlearning suppresses unwanted gener-
ated motions through latent code replacement, including edits
within composite behaviors [8]. SafeMo combines language-
guided data refinement with continuous-space motion un-
learning [9]. Our paired references specify a local replace-
ment for closed-loop humanoid execution, where balance and
surrounding motion must be preserved throughout the edit.

B. Constructing Consistent Local Targets
The editor uses a content rule to locate a movement in the
original clip and a replacement target to specify what the
selected joints should do there. The application determines
which joints and motion features enter the rule. For each
detected event, the editor defines a core interval C for the
replacement and an extended interval I that includes the
transitions into and out of the surrounding motion.
The motion feature follows from the constraint. A position
threshold can identify entry into a restricted region. When
the same path is acceptable at low speed, the rule can instead
use velocity along the relevant direction to identify a rapid
movement. Once the rule selects an interval, the application
supplies the desired replacement for the affected joints.

For a selected joint, the weight rises smoothly to one, stays
at one throughout C, and returns to zero by the end of I. It is
zero for all other joints and outside I. This preserves the root
trajectory and every unedited joint coordinate while giving
the tracker a continuous reference at the edit boundaries.
Changing a joint angle also moves its descendant bodies.
We therefore recompute body poses by forward kinematics
and derive joint and body velocities from the edited motion.
The edit support B includes the transitions and neighboring
frames used to compute velocities. We check the resulting
reference against the content rule and verify that the edit
stays within its support. The resulting position, orientation,
and velocity targets describe the same movement.

The edited reference supplies one full-body tracking target.
During the edit, the selected bodies follow the replacement
and the unaffected bodies follow their original targets. Out-
side the edit support and throughout unedited clips, the
reward trains the policy to track the original motion.
The policy optimizes the full-body action jointly. A local
change in the reference can require coordinated adjustments
elsewhere in the body to maintain balance. Reference locality
specifies the desired motion, and closed-loop optimization
learns how to execute it with physical regularization. PPO
updates both actor and critic under this objective.
Both references advance on a shared timeline and use the
same anchor alignment. The reward therefore evaluates the
replacement at the phase represented by the actor’s original
input. Episodes initialize from m, so training includes reach-
ing the replacement from the original motion state.
D. Sampling Sparse Edit Intervals
Adaptive motion sampling is established in general track-
ing [16]. Here, failure-driven sampling need not emphasize
intervals where following m remains physically feasible but
produces the wrong content. We therefore mix the base start
distribution Qbase with uniform sampling over an edit-start
pool P. The pool contains frames where the selected joints
differ between the two references and a preceding lead-in
within the same clip. If its fraction of the motion timeline is
ppool, the start distribution is
Q = (1−ρ)Qbase +ρU (P),
ρ = min(ρmax,κ ppool).
(4)
The factor κ increases exposure to sparse replacements,
while the cap ρmax reserves training coverage for the rest

of the motion library. The lead-in exposes the policy to
the transition from the original movement into the edited
interval. With an empty edit pool, sampling reduces to Qbase.
The base sampler combines failure-rate weighting with
uniform coverage. Its feedback uses base-sampled episodes
exclusively, keeping its estimate of tracking difficulty in-
dependent of the extra edit-window starts. This separation
controls exposure to the editing objective while preserving
adaptation to difficult motions across the library.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
In the experimental section, we aim to answer the follow-
ing key research questions.
Q1 (Editing effectiveness). Can the controller suppress a
specified movement while staying upright and preserving the
surrounding motion?
Q2 (Motion retention). How closely does the fine-tuned
controller track motions that require no edit?
Q3 (Replacement fidelity). How closely does the edited
limb follow the prescribed trajectory when different con-
trollers suppress the same movement?
Q4 (Training choices). How do edit-window sampling and
initialization from a pretrained tracker affect learning the
replacement?
A. Experiment Setup
We consider two arm-editing scenarios for the 29-joint
Unitree G1 in mjlab. S1 replaces rapid outward extensions
when wrist speed along the shoulder-to-wrist axis exceeds
1.5m/s. S2 replaces overhead reaches when the wrist rises
more than 0.2m above the shoulder. In both scenarios, the
edited reference brings the selected arm to a neutral pose
with the upper arm near the torso and the elbow bent. Both
training sets draw from the same 7,509 clips in Retargeted
AMASS for Robotics [31], [6]. S1 uses the full pool. S2
retains 6,650 clips after excluding borderline wrist heights
and edited references that still contain an overhead reach.
We evaluate all 173 eligible S1 windows and 149 S2
windows sampled across motion sources and screened for
reference feasibility. Each window includes 16 randomized
rollouts per controller with matched starting states before the
edit.

C. Local Editing Performance
To answer Q1, we compare content occurrence and joint
success across controllers. As shown in Table II, Redirect-
Mimic achieved the lowest overhead-content occurrence and
the highest joint success among the matched local methods.
ZeroTrack removes the selected arm’s tracking objective,
leaving other reward terms to favor motion close to the
original reference. AntiTrack rewards departure from that
reference. Tracking em additionally directs the arm toward
the chosen replacement.
The stretching example in Fig. 3(a) makes this distinction
visible. The local reward controls retain upward or lateral
arm responses, while RedirectMimic lowers the edited arm
toward the replacement during the core. In the cup-drinking
example in Fig. 3(b), ZeroTrack satisfies the content thresh-
old with the hand near its original pose. RedirectMimic
brings the hand closer to the lowered target. The entry and
post-edit frames show the surrounding transitions.
The lower aggregate replacement error confirms closer
agreement with the intended alternative. The S2 joint-success
advantage persists despite a higher fall rate than AntiTrack
and remains under unfiltered evaluation.

F. Ablation Studies
To answer Q4, we first isolate the effect of edit-window
sampling on one LAFAN1 arm-mirroring motion. Without
targeted sampling, both scratch training and fine-tuning re-
tained the original content. Adding edit-pool starts enabled
both to learn the replacement. Repeated exposure to the
edited interval was therefore necessary in this single-pair
experiment.
The S1 initialization comparison tests reuse of an existing
tracker. Both scratch training and fine-tuning learned local
replacements, with fine-tuning yielding closer target agree-
ment and similar joint success relative to clip-level uncer-
tainty. This supports fine-tuning as a route for modifying
an available repertoire. The comparison treats the pretrained
tracker as an existing resource, so total training costs differ.
V. DISCUSSION
An explicit replacement target guides the arm even
when several trajectories satisfy the same content rule.
On the speed-defined task, slowing the wrist along its original
path can satisfy the rule. The local methods achieved similar
7


suppression rates, but RedirectMimic tracked the prescribed
trajectory more closely. On the overhead-arm task, this gain
in accuracy coincided with higher joint success.
Local editing also requires the robot to maintain bal-
ance and preserve the surrounding movement. Under the
shared training protocol, the adapted ForgetMimic objective
reduced content occurrence on edited clips, yet the robot
often fell before the edit began. In RedirectMimic, the paired
references preserve tracking targets for unaffected body parts.
On unedited clips, tracking accuracy remained close to that
of the pretrained controller.
The original reference interface allows the policy to
execute a learned replacement, but its timing matters
for streaming control. The offline editor uses the full
clip to place transitions, while the actor must initiate them
from current-frame inputs. The results on known clips in
simulation motivate measuring transition timing over a full
streaming rollout, including whether the selected movement
occurs before or after the intended edit interval.
VI. CONCLUSION
We present RedirectMimic, an approach to local humanoid
motion redaction that combines paired-reference fine-tuning
with edit-window sampling. Training against an edited refer-
ence while observing the original motion lets the policy learn
selected replacements through its existing control interface.
G1 simulations demonstrate pose- and speed-defined arm
editing. On unedited clips, tracking errors and fall rates
remained close to those of the pretrained tracker. Among
the matched local editing methods, RedirectMimic achieved
the highest joint success on the overhead-arm task and the
lowest replacement error on both tasks. Trajectory analy-
sis distinguishes suppression of the specified content from
faithful execution of the replacement, supporting the use
of explicit targets. Future work will extend evaluation to
streaming references and physical humanoids, with emphasis
on transition timing and whole-body stability.
