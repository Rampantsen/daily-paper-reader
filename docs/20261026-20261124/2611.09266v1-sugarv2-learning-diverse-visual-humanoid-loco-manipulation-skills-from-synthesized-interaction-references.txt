## Abstract

Scaling up visual humanoid loco-manipulation skill acquisition requires reducing the human effort needed as tasks and spatial configurations expand, particularly in demonstration collection and task-specific training design. We present SUGARv2, a unified framework for learning diverse visual humanoid loco-manipulation skills through interaction reference synthesis and a shared training pipeline. To obtain diverse references at low cost, we adapt reusable task templates to different spatial configurations, synthesizing coordinated robot and object motions. To reduce task-specific reward design and training effort, we use reference-based imitation as a common learning objective across tasks. We first train privileged teachers to execute these references, then train visual policies through teacher distillation and reinforcement learning with the same referencebased mimic rewards, using diverse object instances and visual domain randomization to support visual generalization and sim-to-real transfer. Across five humanoid loco-manipulation tasks, SUGARv2 achieves a mean success rate of 91.0% in simulation. Deployment on a real humanoid robot further demonstrates successful sim-toreal transfer for whole-body manipulation. These results demonstrate the framework’s potential for learning diverse visual humanoid loco-manipulation skills. Project Page: tianshuwu.github.io/sugarv2-humanoid/.

## 1. Introduction

Scaling up visual humanoid loco-manipulation skill acquisition requires reducing the human effort needed for each new task and spatial configuration. Existing approaches rely on manually collected interaction data or task-specific train ing designs, making it costly to extend skill learning across tasks and spatial configurations. HumanoidMimicGen [23] expands teleoperated demonstrations through whole-body planning, but depends on initial demonstrations and uses decoupled upper- and lower-body control. GRAIL [44] avoids teleoperation and supports whole-body control, but requires video generation and motion reconstruction for every spatial configuration, incurring high costs and potentially producing physically implausible interactions. Task-specific policy learning methods [9, 33, 50] acquire visual locomanipulation skills through tailored training designs, requiring further engineering for new tasks. These dependencies make scaling skill acquisition costly in data preparation and training design (Table 1). Motivated by this, we present SUGARv2, a unified framework for learning diverse visual humanoid loco-manipulation skills through interaction reference synthesis and a shared training pipeline.

For low-cost reference synthesis, we use GPT-6 Astra [29] to generate reusable task-level interaction templates. These templates specify ordered sequences of key whole-body poses, robot–object and hand–object spatial relations, and intended contact labels. We adapt them to different spatial configurations of the robot, object, and goal to synthesize robot and object trajectories for policy learning.

To reduce task-specific reward design and training effort, we use reference-based imitation as a common learning objective across tasks. We first train privileged teachers to execute these references under physical dynamics, then train visual policies through teacher distillation and reinforcement learning with the same reference-based mimic rewards. Teacher supervision is retained during reinforcement learning to guide policy optimization at states visited by the visual policy. Diverse object instances and visual domain randomization further support visual generalization and sim-to-real transfer.

Across five humanoid loco-manipulation tasks, SUG-ARv2 achieves a mean success rate of 91.0%, outperforming the strongest baseline by 51.4 percentage points. Deployed on a real humanoid robot, the visual policies successfully perform whole-body manipulation tasks, demonstrating the framework’s potential for learning diverse realworld manipulation skills. Our main contributions are:

• We present an interaction reference synthesis pipeline for humanoid loco-manipulation. By adapting reusable task templates to different spatial configurations, the pipeline synthesizes diverse interaction references across tasks, reducing the cost of collecting demonstrations.

## 2. Related Work

## 2.1. Synthetic Demonstrations for Policy Learning

Demonstration-based methods synthesize interaction references by adapting existing motions [4, 6, 15, 51]. MimicGen [27] adapts object-centric demonstration segments to new configurations. HumanoidMimicGen [23] extends demonstration adaptation to humanoids using explicitly annotated skills and separate planning for locomotion and stationary manipulation, which constrains whole-body coordination. Generative approaches instead synthesize interaction motions from learned priors [3, 13, 16, 19, 20, 41, 47]. GRAIL [44] generates interaction videos conditioned on 3D assets, reconstructs human and object motion, and retargets the reconstructed motions to humanoid robots.

Beyond generating diverse motions, interaction reference synthesis must account for task requirements and scene geometry. Prior work uses contact relations and scene-adapted keyframes to specify interaction constraints [1, 21, 54]. Conditional motion models generate continuous motion from trajectories, joint constraints, or sparse keyframes [2, 17, 31, 34, 45]. Building on conditional motion generation, SUGARv2 adapts reusable tasklevel interaction templates to different spatial configurations, uses the adapted key poses to guide whole-body motion generation, and constructs coordinated object trajectories to form interaction references for policy learning.

## 2.2. Motion Tracking and Interaction Imitation

Motion tracking connects kinematic references to physical execution [7, 25, 30]. Physics-based character controllers can infer motion from sparse conditions [37], while humanoid tracking controllers track motion references and enable whole-body teleoperation [8, 10, 22, 55]. Scaling motion data and controller pretraining further broadens the range of motions that can be executed [26, 56].

Object interaction additionally requires coordinating body motion with object dynamics [24]. The SkillMimic series [38, 53] learns diverse interaction skills through a unified imitation formulation and extends skill coverage from sparse and noisy demonstrations. InterMimic [48] uses physics-based teachers to refine imperfect interaction demonstrations and distills their behavior into a general policy. Subsequent work extends this direction through generative control and the augmentation of robot interaction motions [49, 57]. Reference-based interaction learning also enables humanoid robots to acquire manipulation skills by imitating coordinated human and object motions [39, 42, 43]. These works show how reference-based learning can support interaction skills without a separately designed task reward for each behavior. Building on this, SUGARv2 uses synthesized interaction references to define a common learning objective for visual loco-manipulation across tasks.

## 2.3. Visual Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

Imitation learning trains visual humanoid manipulation policies from demonstrations collected through whole-body teleoperation [5, 8]. Humanoid vision-language-action models draw on teleoperated demonstrations, human data, and heterogeneous robot data to learn diverse skills [12, 14, 28, 46]. Synthetic demonstrations also support visual policy learning by expanding training data without collecting additional robot demonstrations [4, 18, 40].

Reinforcement learning provides another route to learning visual loco-manipulation skills through interaction with the environment. Some approaches train privileged policies with task-specific rewards and then transfer their behavior to visual policies [52]. VIRAL [9] trains visual policies to imitate privileged teachers through online DAgger and behavior cloning. Reinforcement learning can further refine visuomotor behavior [11, 33, 50]. Extending these approaches to new tasks requires additional reward design and tuning. SUGARv2 uses synthesized interaction references to define a common learning objective across tasks. A shared reference-based mimic reward guides both privileged teacher training and visual policy optimization, reducing task-specific reward design effort.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Problem definition and framework overview

We aim to learn visual humanoid loco-manipulation policies that coordinate whole-body motion and object manipulation. Using egocentric images and proprioception, a policy must complete the task across different spatial configurations. We learn a separate policy for each task.

As shown in Figure 2, SUGARv2 shares a two-stage pipeline across tasks: interaction reference synthesis and policy learning. The first stage synthesizes robot motion, object trajectories, and intended contact labels for each spatial configuration (Section 3.2). In the second stage, we train a privileged teacher to execute the synthesized references, then distill its behavior into a visual policy and refine the policy through reinforcement learning with the same reference-based mimic rewards (Section 3.3).

## 3.2. Interaction reference synthesis

Each task has its own interaction template T. Given a sampled spatial configuration c specifying the robot’s initial state, the object’s initial pose, and the goal position, a shared synthesis pipeline G produces an interaction reference $\tau _ { c } = G ( \tau , c )$ . Each reference is a sequence:

$$
\tau_ {c} = \left\{\left(q _ {t} ^ {\text { robot }}, q _ {t} ^ {\text { obj }}, l _ {t}\right) \right\} _ {t = 1} ^ {T}.\tag{1}
$$

At each time step t, the reference contains a whole-body robot pose $q _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { r o b o t } }$ <sup>t</sup>, an object pose $q _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { o b j } }$ , and intended contact labels $l _ { t }$ for each hand. T denotes the reference length. These kinematic references are not tied to specific object instances and can be reused with same-category objects during training. Figure 2 illustrates how we construct task templates and instantiate them across spatial configurations, and Figure 3 shows the resulting references.

## 3.2.1. Task template construction

For a given task, motions across spatial configurations typically share the same interaction sequence and contact requirements. For example, carrying an object requires establishing a grasp, transporting the object, and reaching the goal, while the robot’s stance, turns, and path depend on the object and goal locations. A task template captures these shared interaction stages, allowing the same task design to be reused across configurations.

For each task, we use GPT-6 Astra [29] to generate an interaction template T consisting of an ordered sequence of template keyframes. Each template keyframe specifies a whole-body pose, robot–object and hand–object spatial relations, and intended contact labels. The poses in the template provide starting points for adaptation, while the spatial relations guide robot positioning and hand placement in each configuration. The contact labels specify which hands interact with the object at each template keyframe.

## 3.2.2. Task template instantiation

We adapt the template keyframes to each sampled spatial configuration to obtain instantiated keyframes and their timing. Starting from the object’s initial pose and the goal position specified by the spatial configuration, we use the template’s hand–object relations and the object’s symmetries to generate candidate hand targets. We then adapt the robot’s position, heading, and whole-body pose to these targets under reachability and scene constraints. Among feasible candidates, we select the keyframe requiring the least movement and pose adjustment. Once the keyframe poses are instantiated, we determine their timing from the movement demands between consecutive poses.

Given the instantiated keyframes and their timing, we use an off-the-shelf motion generation model [34], additionally conditioned on task text, to generate continuous wholebody motion. For each configuration, we generate multiple candidate trajectories, filter out those that violate kinematic or scene constraints, and rank the remaining candidates to favor smooth motion with less unnecessary movement.

After generating robot motion, we construct the object trajectory from the motion of the interacting hands and the template’s hand–object relations. We correct object positions so that the trajectory passes through the positions specified at the keyframes. Using the template’s contact labels and keyframe times, we assign contact intervals to the interacting hands. Together, the robot motion, object trajectory, and contact labels form a complete interaction reference for policy learning in the sampled spatial configuration.

## 3.3. Policy learning

Building on the synthesized interaction references, our policy learning pipeline uses reference-based imitation as a common learning objective across tasks, reducing taskspecific reward design effort. We first train privileged teachers to execute these references, then train visual policies through teacher distillation and reinforcement learning with the same reference-based mimic reward.

## 3.3.1. Privileged teacher training

We train a privileged teacher to execute the interaction references through a frozen pretrained whole-body controller [26]. The teacher policy $\pi _ { T } ( a _ { t } \mid o _ { t } ^ { T } )$ receives privileged observations $o _ { t } ^ { T }$ comprising privileged states and reference trajectories, and outputs a latent command $z _ { t }$ for the whole-body controller and hand commands $h _ { t }$ specifying whether each hand is open or closed. The whole-body controller executes body motion, while hand commands regulate grasping and release based on reference contact labels.

![](images/d7ca9a5e238a77da5677b532cf80df052842c40c4ff0517d02f634f3c8f45292.jpg)  
Figure 4. Visual diversity across (a) Floor-to-Table and (b) Basket Transport. Each paired example shows the same robot state and scene from a third-person view in the top row and an egocentric RGB view in the bottom row.

We train the teacher with a mimic reward comprising tracking, interaction, and regularization terms:

$$
r _ {t} = r _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{track}} + r _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{int}} + r _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{reg}}.\tag{2}
$$

The tracking reward encourages the robot and object to follow their reference motions. The interaction reward encourages their relative poses and hand–object contact states to match the references. The regularization reward penalizes abrupt control changes and undesirable physical behavior. To support visual policy learning, it also encourages the teacher to keep task-relevant objects and goals within the camera’s field of view. Together, these terms guide the teacher to reproduce the reference interaction under physical dynamics. This reference-based reward formulation supports a common training procedure across tasks.

Training episodes start from reference states before the first intended contact, with random perturbations applied to the robot and object states. We apply early termination when the robot or object deviates substantially from the reference or enters an invalid physical state. Reward definitions and training details are provided in the appendix.

## 3.3.2. Visual policy learning

We train visual policies to perform manipulation from egocentric images, using privileged teachers for action supervision. The policy $\pi _ { S } ( a _ { t } \ | \ o _ { t } ^ { S } )$ receives egocentric RGB images and proprioceptive inputs $o _ { t } ^ { S }$ . A visual encoder [32] projects image features and concatenates them with proprioception, and an MLP predicts the commands $\left( \boldsymbol { z } _ { t } , \boldsymbol { h } _ { t } \right)$ .

Learning directly from images requires jointly learning visual representations and control. We first train the visual policy through teacher distillation, querying teacher actions at states visited by the policy [35]. Matching individual teacher actions does not directly optimize the full interaction, so we continue with the reference-based mimic reward while retaining teacher distillation, optimizing robot and object motion throughout the interaction.

The frozen teacher provides the complete body-control latent and hand commands as action supervision at studentvisited states. We minimize the mean squared error between the student’s predicted action and the teacher target:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{distill}} = \mathrm{MSE} \big (a _ {t} ^ {S}, a _ {t} ^ {T} \big),\tag{3}
$$

where $a _ { t } ^ { S }$ is the student’s predicted action, $a _ { t } ^ { T }$ is the teacher target at the visited state. We jointly optimize the visual policy with PPO [36] and teacher distillation:

$$
\mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{policy}} = \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{PPO}} ^ {\mathrm{actor}} + \lambda_ {\mathrm{distill}} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{distill}},\tag{4}
$$

where $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { P P O } } ^ { \mathrm { a c t o r } }$ is the PPO policy loss and $\lambda _ { \mathrm { d i s t i l l } }$ weights action distillation during policy optimization.

The entire visual policy is trained end-to-end throughout training. A privileged critic is warmed up during distillation and updated alongside the policy during joint optimization.

We render observations online with diverse object instances, backgrounds, materials, and lighting conditions. Each interaction reference can provide training data under varied visual appearances without regenerating the underlying motion. Figure 4 illustrates this visual diversity.

During visual training, we randomize camera poses, augment rendered images, and add noise to images and proprioceptive observations. We also use dynamics randomization and external disturbances in both training stages to improve robustness to physical variations.

## 4. Experiments

Through our experiments, we aim to answer the following questions: (1) How well does SUGARv2 learn visual manipulation skills under varied spatial configurations and unseen backgrounds? (2) How efficiently can we synthesize interaction references, and how well do they support visual policy learning? (3) How does the number of synthesized references affect visual policy performance? (4) How do key design choices in our learning framework affect policy performance? (5) Can the learned visual policies transfer to a real humanoid robot?

## 4.1. Experimental Setup

Tasks. We evaluate five humanoid loco-manipulation tasks covering pickup, pick-and-place, and object transport. Tabletop Pickup requires grasping an object from a tabletop and holding it. Floor-to-Table and Same-Table involve picking up an object from the floor or tabletop, respectively, and placing it into a target basket on a table. Box Carrying and Basket Transport require carrying the corresponding object while walking to a designated target region. These tasks demand tightly coordinated whole-body control, requiring continuous coordination between the upper and lower body throughout object interaction.

Training and evaluation. We train visual policies across diverse spatial configurations of robot, object, and goal within prescribed ranges. We use 100 synthesized references per task and train separate privileged teachers and visual policies with the same pipeline. In simulation, we report full-task success as mean ± standard deviation over three seeds, using task-specific criteria.

Baselines. For visual policy learning, we compare with VI-RAL [9] and GR00T N1.7 [28]. VIRAL combines DAgger with behavior cloning. GR00T is fine-tuned on image–action pairs from teacher rollouts. For interaction reference synthesis, we compare with GRAIL [44], which reconstructs motion from generated videos.

GRAIL can introduce physically implausible object motion during video generation and depth or pose errors during reconstruction. ## 4.4. Influence of Reference Data Size

We examine how reference data scale affects visual policy learning. We use 20, 50, 100, 200, and 500 references per task while keeping the training pipeline and budget fixed. Data scale counts kinematic robot–object trajectories with contact labels across spatial configurations. During training, we vary object instances within each category and render visual observations from the simulation, using the same visual randomization settings across experiments.

Figure 5 shows that increasing the reference set from 20 to 100 trajectories per task generally improves policy success rates, while further increases offer limited gains and sometimes reduce performance. We therefore use 100 references per task in the main evaluation, balancing policy performance with the cost of reference synthesis.

## 4.5. Ablation study

We evaluate the key design choices in our visual policy learning pipeline through ablations under the same training budget (Table 4). Without teacher distillation, training the policy from scratch using reference-based reinforcement learning yields near-zero success on all five tasks under this training budget. Without reference-based reinforcement learning, training through teacher distillation alone results in lower success than the full framework on every task. Finally, after the same initialization through teacher distillation, continuing with reference-based reinforcement learning alone reduces success across all tasks compared with retaining teacher distillation.

These results support the design of our visual policy learning pipeline: teacher distillation provides an effective initialization, while reference-based reinforcement learning refines task performance with continued teacher supervision. Together, these components enable effective learning of visual loco-manipulation skills.

## 4.6. Real-world evaluation

Real-world transfer. We directly deploy the simulationtrained visual policies on a Unitree G1 without fine-tuning on any real-world data. Across trials, we vary the initial positions. Table 6 reports successful trials out of total attempts for each task, and Figure 6 presents representative executions. The policies successfully perform all five tasks on the real robot, demonstrating sim-to-real transfer for coordinated whole-body motion and object manipulation.

Failure analysis. Execution errors can cause missed grasps, after which the policy may continue subsequent motions without securing the object. Recovery from these failures remains limited, as the policy does not consistently retry the grasp before proceeding.

## 5. Conclusion

In this work, we presented SUGARv2, a unified framework for interaction reference synthesis and visual policy learning across tasks. We adapt reusable task templates to different spatial configurations to obtain diverse interaction references at low cost. These references support a unified training pipeline combining teacher distillation and reinforcement learning with shared reference-based mimic rewards, reducing task-specific reward design effort. Experiments across five tasks and real-humanoid deployment demonstrate the framework’s effectiveness, highlighting its potential for learning diverse real-world manipulation skills.

Limitations and Future Works. Our reference synthesis currently focuses on short-horizon interactions in simplified scenes. Extending it to long-horizon tasks and cluttered environments will require more scalable and automated interaction reference synthesis. Our visual policies are taskspecific; we aim to use synthesized references to support multitask vision-language-action policies that follow language instructions across diverse interaction tasks.
