SONIC-K1: Porting a Universal Humanoid Motion Tracker
to a Small Humanoid on a Single GPU
Kyrylo Kolesnichenko1,2, Irvin Steve Cardenas2, and Jong-Hoon Kim2
Fig. 1.
A human reference motion (left) and SONIC-K1 tracking it on the
Booster K1 in simulation (right).
Abstract— We present SONIC-K1, to our knowledge the first
open-weights whole-body motion tracker for the Booster K1
humanoid, obtained by adapting the SONIC framework to this
smaller robot. The policy is trained from scratch on a single
shared GPU with a small fraction of the compute reported for
SONIC. In MuJoCo simulation, it tracks nearly all held-out
motion clips and performs well on external benchmarks under
SONIC’s success criterion. The first release ran on the real K1
but shook its legs on steps and turns, and simulation showed
a sensitivity to actuation delay. We therefore post-trained it
with the robot’s deployment torque limits, randomized 0–
40 ms actuation delay and gain randomization. The post-trained
v1.0 tracks 96.6% of held-out clips under real-robot torque
limits and 40 ms of delay, compared with 76.7% before, and
in our runs on the real K1 it walks and turns without the
earlier leg shaking. These results show broad motion-tracking
capability on a modest training budget. Code, policy weights
and evaluation tools are publicly available.
I. INTRODUCTION
A general motion tracker is a single policy that takes
a reference trajectory and outputs joint commands. Once
a robot has one, motion capture, pose estimation from
video and teleoperation can all drive the same controller.
SONIC [1] pushed this approach to 700 h of motion data and
128 GPUs. The code is open, but it is built for the Unitree
G1, and most labs cannot afford to train it at that scale.

Our lab works with the Booster K1 [2], a 22-DoF hu-
manoid that is noticeably smaller than the G1, and we wanted
a SONIC-style tracker for it on the hardware we have.
We kept SONIC’s architecture, PPO settings and rewards,
retargeted 9,991 Bones-SEED clips [3] to the K1 with
UMR [4], and trained from scratch for 50,000 iterations on
a single H100 that was shared with other users, then post-
trained it for 4,000 more iterations on one RTX PRO 6000.
The resulting model, SONIC-K1, is to our knowledge the
first general motion tracker for the K1 with public weights.
It tracks 97.4% of 1,985 held-out clips in MuJoCo [5] under
SONIC’s success rule. On the real robot this first release
(v0.1) shook its legs on steps and turns. A short post-training
with the robot’s torque limits and actuation delay (v1.0, 4,000
more iterations) fixed most of this: in simulation v1.0 tracks
96.6% of held-out clips under real-robot limits and 40 ms of
delay (v0.1: 76.7%), and in our robot runs it walks and turns
without the earlier leg shaking.
This report makes three contributions:
1) SONIC-K1, the first open-weights general motion
tracker for the Booster K1. It is trained with roughly
1–1.5% of SONIC’s compute, and we list every change
we made to the original recipe.
2) An evaluation in two simulators on a 1,985-clip hold-
out and on PHUMA, LAFAN1 and 100STYLE, plus
a robustness sweep.
3) A real-robot post-training recipe that makes the policy
robust to the K1’s torque limits and actuation delay,
tested on hardware, plus the released code (Apache-
2.0), weights (CC BY-NC 4.0), evaluation tools and
robot application.
II. RELATED WORK
SONIC [1] trains a single tracker for the Unitree G1
on 700 h of motion data. It uses per-modality encoders, a
quantized motion token and a shared decoder. We start from
its public code and keep its architecture and training recipe.
To give a sense of scale for our results, we also list numbers
that the SONIC paper reports for BeyondMimic [6] and
Any2Track [7], two other trackers for the G1.
Our training data is the Bones-SEED motion-capture
dataset [3], which we map to the K1 with UMR [4], a
learned retargeting method for humanoid robots. Besides our
own held-out Bones-SEED clips, we test on PHUMA [8],
the external benchmark used in the SONIC paper, and
on two standard human-motion datasets, LAFAN1 [9] and
100STYLE [10].

III. METHOD: PORTING SONIC TO THE K1
A. Robot and data
The K1 has 22 actuated joints: 2 in the head, 4 in each
arm and 6 in each leg. It has no waist and no wrists. We use
Booster’s official K1 22dof model for both retargeting and
training. Bones-SEED contains 142,220 clips, all of which
we retargeted to the K1 with UMR. An automatic check then
removes clips with joint-limit violations, feet going through
the floor, velocity spikes or NaNs, and tags each clip as
feasible or not for the K1 (for example, motions that need a
waist are not). We keep only clips that pass the check and are
tagged feasible, which leaves 62,427. We trained on 9,991
of them and kept 1,985 aside as a test set, chosen before
training started and with no overlap (Fig. 2).
B. Policy and training
The architecture is unchanged from SONIC. Encoders turn
a reference motion into a 64-D quantized (FSQ) token, and
an MLP decoder combines the token with proprioception to
produce joint targets. A PD controller tracks those targets;
the policy runs at 50 Hz and physics at 200 Hz. The actor
has 25.1M parameters. We trained the robot-motion and
teleoperation encoders. The SMPL encoder is in the network
but never saw any data.
PPO hyper-parameters, adaptive motion sampling and re-
wards are the same as in SONIC, and Table I lists what
we changed. The biggest difference is scale. We ran 16,384
environments for 50,000 iterations on one H100 NVL, which
took 135.4 h of wall-clock time because other jobs were
sharing the GPU (about 103 h if it had been ours alone). That
is roughly 1–1.5% of SONIC’s 128 GPUs × ∼3 days. The
real-robot post-training (v1.0, Sec. V) added 4,000 iterations,
about 9.2 h on one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000. We also raised
the initial action standard deviation from 0.05 to 0.5, because
with 0.05 the KL-adaptive scheme throttled the learning rate
on the K1. Finally, we added a reward for tracking the head
joints, since the K1 head is actuated, and scaled pushes and
center-of-mass randomization by 0.7 for the smaller body.
v0.1 did not model actuation delay.
C. Evaluation protocol
We use SONIC’s success rule: a rollout fails if the root or
any end-effector ends up more than 0.25 m away in height
from the reference. We also report two other rules. No-
fall only checks that the robot stays up (root height within
0.25 m, orientation within 1 rad). Official, the stricter rule in
the SONIC repository, also limits world-frame foot position
to 0.2 m. Tracking error is MPJPE-L over 12 K1 links, in
mm, averaged over successful clips. For the MuJoCo runs
we use the robot-motion encoder and deterministic actions.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
All results in this section are for the first release (v0.1,
50,000 iterations), use 10 s windows, and apply SONIC’s
0.25 m success rule. Section V covers the real-robot post-
training (v1.0).
A. Benchmarks
Table II shows the MuJoCo results. On the 1,985 held-
out Bones-SEED clips the policy succeeds 97.4% of the
time, with an MPJPE-L of 15.4 mm. PHUMA provides G1
trajectories, so we sampled 2,000 motions across its 15
sources and mapped them from the G1 to the K1 with our
own inverse kinematics. Of these, 1,758 passed our checks,
and the policy succeeds on 93.6%. On 100STYLE we used
the forward walk and run of all 100 styles, and the policy
succeeds on 99.0%. LAFAN1 was the hardest set, at 70.6%
of 10 s windows; we come back to it in the limitations.
The right side of Table II lists G1 numbers for SONIC,
BeyondMimic and Any2Track, copied from the SONIC
paper. We did not rerun these methods. The robot, data,
retargeting and test clips are all different, so these columns
are only there to give a sense of scale and should not be read
as a head-to-head comparison.
We also checked the same hold-out in Isaac Lab, the
simulator we trained in. No-fall success is 98.7% there and
98.4% in MuJoCo, and official-rule success in Isaac Lab is
93.1%, so moving between simulators costs very little. The
amount of training data makes a large difference. An earlier
run trained on only 10 clips reached 63.6% official success
on the same hold-out (33.5 mm MPJPE-L). The released
9,991-clip model reaches 93.1% (14.7 mm).
B. Robustness
Fig. 3 shows a perturbation sweep in MuJoCo with 200
held-out clips per setting, reported under the no-fall and
official rules. No-fall success stays at 98.0% or higher with
PD gains scaled anywhere from 0.6 to 1.4×, with 70% motor
strength, with a 2 kg payload, with 3× observation noise
and with Booster’s torque caps. Dense pushes up to 3× the
training range still give 97.0%, and very low friction (0.2)
costs about 5 points. Latency is the exception. Success is
98.5% at 20 ms of delay but falls to 56.0% at 40 ms.

C. First test on the real K1
We exported the policy to ONNX and ran it at 50 Hz on
the robot’s onboard computer, with the robot on a safety belt,
using the sample clips from the release kit. Upper-body clips,
calm motions and slower whole-body motions such as squats
ran smoothly. Clips with steps and turns were less reliable,
with visible jitter in the legs. We do not have motion capture
for the robot, so we cannot report tracking error on hardware.
Section V addresses the leg problem.
V. POST-TRAINING FOR THE REAL K1 (V1.0)
Problem. On the real robot the first release (v0.1) shook
its legs on steps and turns (Sec. IV-C). Two facts point
to the training plant. First, training allowed leg torques of
68/76/38/112/38 Nm for hip pitch/roll/yaw, knee and ankle,
while Booster’s deployment limits are 30/35/20/40/20 Nm.
Second, training had no actuation delay, and in simulation
v0.1 drops from 98.5% to 56.0% success when the delay
grows from 20 to 40 ms (Sec. IV-B). Raising kd helped in
simulation but made the robot worse, and fine-tuning on a
single failing clip did not help on hardware either.
Method. We resume v0.1 with four changes and keep
observations, rewards, PD gains, action scales and data
unchanged, so the checkpoint stays valid:

VI. WEAKNESSES AND LIMITATIONS
Post-training largely removed the leg shaking we observed
on the real robot; on some motions the hands sometimes
touch the body. Motions that need floor contact or props,
such as falling and getting up in LAFAN1, are outside our
training data, and very long clips build up global drift. Most
of our evaluation is in simulation, with one training seed, and
we did not ablate the four post-training changes. The SMPL
and video encoders are not trained yet, so video input is
retargeted to the K1 first.
VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
SONIC-K1 is the first open-weights general motion tracker
for the Booster K1. SONIC was designed for 128 GPUs, but
our port, trained on one GPU, still reaches 97.4% success
on held-out motion under SONIC’s own rule. A short post-
training with the robot’s torque limits, actuation delay and
gain randomization made it work on the real K1: v1.0 tracks
96.6% of held-out clips under real-robot limits and 40 ms of
delay, and in our runs it walks and turns without the earlier
leg shaking on hardware.
Our next step is a proper evaluation on the robot with
tracking metrics. Further plans are a larger model trained
on the full 62K-clip pool, whole-body teleoperation through
the teleoperation encoder we already trained, and driving the
robot from video. For video we currently estimate human
pose and retarget it, and a trained SMPL encoder would
remove the retargeting step.
