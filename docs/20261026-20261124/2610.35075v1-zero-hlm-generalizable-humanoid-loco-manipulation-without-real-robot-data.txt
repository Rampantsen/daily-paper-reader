Learning generalizable humanoid loco-manipulation remains bottlenecked by costly real-world demonstrations. Simulation ofers a scalable alternative, but extending it to humanoids requires solving two coupled challenges: constructing scene-scale environments that are both visually faithful and physics-ready, and generating successful demonstrations under unified whole-body control. We present Zero-HLM, a real-to-sim-to-real framework that transforms smartphone captures and simulated source demonstrations into scalable training data for humanoid loco-manipulation. We couple Gaussian visual reconstruction with agentic physical scene construction to build interaction-ready digital twins with aligned visual and physical geometry. Within the environment, our motion– environment co-adaptation strategy synthesizes unified whole-body references and uses rollout feedback to restore interaction geometry when tracking deviations disrupt execution. From 45 source demonstrations teleoperated in simulation, Zero-HLM generates 4,800 physically validated demonstrations, averaging a 1.8× speedup over real-world data collection. Policies trained solely on these data transfer zero-shot to a Unitree G1 across three long-horizon tasks. With GR00T N1.7, simulation-only training achieves 66.7% mean full-task success with a 20-percentage-point gain over real-data training under matched configurations. Across five generalization settings, pooled real-world success increases from 23% to 69% without additional real demonstrations. To support systematic evaluation, we establish a benchmark with nine skill stages and shared protocols for comparing models and studying data scaling.

## 1 Introduction

Humanoid robots combine mobility and dexterity with a morphology naturally suited to human environments. Recent advances in whole-body control and visuomotor policies have enabled diverse loco-manipulation behaviors [1–8]. However, learning generalizable behaviors requires diverse interaction data that remain costly and dificult to collect on a physical humanoid [9–12].

Simulation-based data generation ofers a scalable alternative to costly physical collection, but its practical value depends on constructing simulation environments that support reliable sim-to-real transfer [13]. For humanoids, locomotion expands the workspace beyond a local manipulation area, while a moving egocentric camera observes the environment from changing positions and heights. This calls for scene-level reconstruction that preserves both visual fidelity and the physical geometry and dynamics required for interaction. Existing Gaussian-based real-to-sim-to-real methods have shown promising transfer in tabletop settings [14–20]. However, extending this paradigm to scene-scale humanoid interaction is considerably more demanding, as meshes extracted from Gaussian reconstructions can contain contact artifacts, and articulated mechanisms require additional modeling efort [15, 21].

Beyond reconstructing such environments, generating humanoid interaction data at scale remains challenging. Recent methods [10, 22, 23] simplify data generation by specifying locomotion and upper-body commands separately. This separation can limit motion naturalness and support for leg-critical interactions, motivating unified whole-body reference motion tracking [1, 2]. However, scaling data generation under unified tracking presents two challenges: (i) Whole-body reference motion synthesis. Adapting source demonstrations to varied spatial configurations requires coordinated changes throughout the body while preserving task-relative interaction [24]. (ii) Execution mismatch. Whole-body controllers must reconcile reference tracking with balance and contact constraints, so precise state-by-state replay cannot be assumed [25]. Even kinematically valid reference motions may produce tracking deviations in the robot base and hands, disrupting intended object interactions.

To address these challenges, we propose Zero-HLM, a real-to-sim-to-real framework that combines visually faithful, physics-ready real-to-sim reconstruction with scalable demonstration generation under unified whole-body control. First, inspired by recent MLLM-based agentic frameworks that produce faithful digital cousin reconstructions [26, 27], we propose Gaussian-Guided Agentic Real-to-Sim, which builds a scene-level digital twin by coupling a high-fidelity Gaussian reconstruction with an agentic pipeline that iteratively aligns the digital cousins to the real scene, using the Gaussian reconstruction as the alignment reference. We further composite the Gaussian rendering with the simulator’s rendering of the aligned scene, yielding a hybrid rendering that narrows the visual sim-to-real gap. To tackle the mismatch between synthesized reference motions and their tracked execution, we introduce a motion–environment co-adaptation strategy for scalable data generation under unified whole-body control. Demonstration-Anchored Whole-Body Motion Synthesis combines locomotion planning with object-relative adaptation of source demonstrations, producing continuous whole-body reference motions for varied spatial configurations. Execution-Conditioned Correction then uses rollout feedback to adjust scene configurations and reference motions, restoring interactions disrupted by tracking deviations. We pair the resulting physically validated trajectories with photorealistic egocentric observations for policy training. Policies trained solely on our simulated data transfer directly to the real world and generalize across diverse scene configurations. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

• We propose Zero-HLM, a real-to-sim-to-real framework for unified humanoid loco-manipulation. It combines Gaussian-guided agentic reconstruction with whole-body data generation in visually faithful, physics-ready scenes.

• We introduce motion–environment co-ada<sub>p</sub>tation, which couples demonstration-anchored motion synthesis with execution-conditioned correction to compensate for tracking deviations, yielding 4,800 successful trajectories across three tasks at a 1.8× speedup over real-world collection.

• We demonstrate direct sim-to-real transfer and real-world <sub>g</sub>eneralization through simulation-only training. With GR00T N1.7, simulation-only training yields a 20-percentage-point gain over real-data training under matched configurations. Across five extended generalization settings, pooled real-world success increases from 23% to 69% without additional real demonstrations.

• We establish a humanoid loco-mani<sub>p</sub>ulation benchmark comprising three long-horizon tasks and nine skill stages, enabling systematic comparisons across diverse policy families under unified training and execution protocols.

## 2 Related Work

Humanoid Loco-Manipulation. Humanoid loco-manipulation depends on controllers that coordinate mobility with contact-rich interaction. Decoupled systems such as HOMIE [31] combine lower-body control with separately commanded arms and hands. Unified whole-body trackers, including SONIC [1] and OmniClone [32], instead track coordinated reference motions through a shared control interface, enabling human-motion-driven teleoperation and demonstration collection. Although teleoperation provides embodiment-aligned interaction data, expanding its task and scene coverage requires additional operator time and robot rollouts.

Robot-free interfaces such as HuMI [33] and egocentric approaches such as EgoHumanoid [7] reduce dependence on a physical robot during demonstration collection, but still rely on human-recorded interactions to cover new settings. In parallel, vision-language-action and world-action models have advanced generalizable robot learning [3, 34–36], but still depend on diverse interaction data. In this work, we address this data bottleneck through scalable whole-body data generation in simulation, enabling zero-shot real-world deployment with generalization to unseen scene configurations.

Real-to-Sim-to-Real for Robot Learning. Real-to-sim-to-real learning leverages reconstructed environments to generate simulation data for real-world policy transfer. Gaussian-based methods preserve captured appearance to reduce the visual sim-to-real gap [14, 15, 17], while recent agentic approaches facilitate physical scene construction through geometry modeling, physical parameter inference, and simulator configuration [37, 38]. Our approach brings these capabilities together by grounding agentic scene construction in Gaussian geometry and matched-view renderings, aligning physical interaction geometry with the reconstructed visual scene.

For demonstration generation, MimicGen adapts source demonstrations to new object configurations through simulation rollouts [39], whereas DemoGen performs demonstration and point-cloud transformations without simulation [40]. Extending data generation to humanoids, VIRAL and FetchMan rely on task-specific rewards [28, 29], while other approaches decouple locomotion from upper-body control [10, 22, 23]. VLK adopts unified whole-body control but focuses primarily on single-object transport [30]. In contrast, we adapt source interaction skills without specialized motion generators and correct tracking deviations through motion–environment co-adaptation, validating the resulting motions under the deployment controller. Tab. 1 compares these approaches.

## 3.1 Gaussian-Guided Agentic Real-to-Sim Reconstruction

To narrow the sim-to-real gap, we aim to construct a visually faithful, physics-ready simulation environment with aligned visual and physical representations. We build this environment E from hybrid representations: a Gaussian reconstruction $\mathcal { G }$ for appearance and spatial reference, and an editable mesh-based physical scene for collision and articulation. We reconstruct G from smartphone images using MAtCha [41], with checkerboards establishing metric scale and ground alignment. A multimodal agent then uses G and a mesh extracted from it to construct and configure the physical scene using Blender MCP and IsaacLab. The extracted mesh guides the initial layout and geometry, while G provides an active-view rendering interface for local refinement. The agent therefore selects inspection viewpoints and compares Gaussian and mesh renderings at matched camera poses to iteratively refine local geometry and placement The resulting hybrid scene E integrates a mesh-based physics backend with a Gaussian visual frontend in a shared metric frame, aligning physical interactions with visual observations. More details appear in Appendix A.1.

Interaction-critical assets are reconstructed separately from dedicated video captures to preserve the local geometry required for grasping, placement, and physical contact. Camera poses are estimated using COLMAP, while checkerboard calibration establishes metric scale for asset reconstruction. Each asset is then aligned and placed within the scene by the agent according to the task configuration. In IsaacLab, the agent further configures collision geometry, articulated joints, and physical parameters including mass, friction, and center of mass, then refines them through interaction checks. For example, cabinet opening tests articulated motion, while object release tests gravity-driven motion and collision.

## 3.2 Motion–Environment Co-Adaptation for Scalable Data Generation

In this section, we aim to generate physically executable whole-body demonstrations across diverse spatial configurations from a small set of source demonstrations. We first collect 15 successful simulation teleoperation demonstrations per task as the source pool D<sub>0</sub>. Each demonstration is segmented into locomotion, manipulation, and leg-critical interaction using event annotations. For each sampled configuration, we construct a whole-body reference motion $\mathcal { R } = ( r _ { 1 } , \ldots \ldots , r _ { T } )$ from D<sub>0</sub>, where each pose $r _ { t } = ( p _ { t } , R _ { t } , q _ { t } )$ specifies the root position, orientation, and body joints. A simulated rollout executes this motion through SONIC [1], with separate hand commands. Successful rollouts are retained in the dataset D. Here, stage denotes a generation step and segment a temporal portion of the motion.

Source Annotation and Selection. We identify interaction segments using task-specific event predicates based on hand closure, object proximity, displacement, and task success conditions. Only demonstrations with valid interaction events and successful physical replay are retained. For each sampled target pose $T _ { o } = ( p _ { o } , R _ { o } )$ , we rank source demonstrations by spatial proximity:

$$
d _ {i} = w _ {p} \| p _ {o} - p _ {o} ^ {\mathrm{src}, i} \| _ {2} + w _ {R} d _ {R} (R _ {o}, R _ {o} ^ {\mathrm{src}, i}),\tag{1}
$$

where $d _ { R }$ measures the relative rotation angle, and $w _ { p } , w _ { R }$ weight positional and rotational diferences. During data generation, we randomly select one of the $K _ { \mathrm { s r c } }$ nearest valid demonstrations and adapt its interaction segments to the sampled target.

Stage 1: Demonstration-Anchored Whole-Body Motion Synthesis. For each generation attempt, we sample a task configuration around the source setup, varying the robot interaction pose and relevant object or placement-target poses. The initial walking position is sampled from navigable space. We then synthesize a continuous whole-body reference by replanning locomotion and adapting source interaction motions to the sampled configuration.

For locomotion segments, $\mathbf { A } ^ { * }$ plans collision-free paths between the required robot poses. The SONIC motion planner converts these paths into whole-body reference motions using a walking speed sampled once per trajectory from [0.5, 0.7] m/s and a four-frame motion context. For vertical adjustments, such as squatting, we use the planner to generate root-height transitions. When a sustained upper-body posture is required, such as carrying an object, we preserve the corresponding joint angles in both the planning context and generated reference. The resulting reference updates the motion context for subsequent planning. For leg-critical interactions, we retain the demonstrated lower-body motion and combine it with the preceding segment’s terminal upper-body posture.

For manipulation segments, we adapt source hand trajectories to the sampled interaction targets while keeping the reference root pose fixed. Let $T _ { o } ^ { \mathrm { s r c } } , T _ { o } \in S E ( 3 )$ denote the source and sampled poses of an interaction object or placement target, and $T _ { e , t } ^ { \mathrm { s r c } } \in S E ( 3 )$ the source hand pose, all expressed in the world frame. The adapted hand target is

$$
\widetilde {T} _ {e, t} = T _ {o} (T _ {o} ^ {\mathrm{src}}) ^ {- 1} T _ {e, t} ^ {\mathrm{src}}.\tag{2}
$$

This transformation preserves the demonstrated hand–target relation under the new configuration. For placement, $T _ { o }$ represents the receptacle target; when its orientation is unchanged, adaptation reduces to a positional shift while retaining the source hand orientation. Given a baseline reference pose $r _ { t } ^ { \mathrm { b a s e } }$ from the selected source or preceding segment, arm inverse kinematics (IK) updates the active arm joints:

$$
\widetilde {q} _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{arm}} = \mathrm{IK} _ {\mathrm{arm}} (\widetilde {T} _ {e, t}; r _ {t} ^ {\mathrm{base}}).\tag{3}
$$

The root pose and inactive joints remain unchanged during reference construction, while their executed states are determined by the subsequent whole-body rollout.

To ensure continuity between segments, we align their boundary poses and adjust transition timing. Each segment inherits the final reference pose of its predecessor. We connect this inherited pose to the retargeted manipulation trajectory using a gradually decaying hand-position ofset:

$$
p _ {e, t} ^ {\mathrm{blend}} = \widetilde {p} _ {e, t} + \lambda_ {t} \big (p _ {e} ^ {\mathrm{in}} - \widetilde {p} _ {e, t _ {0}} \big).\tag{4}
$$

Here, $\widetilde { p } _ { e , t }$ is the position component of $\widetilde { T } _ { e , t } , p _ { e } ^ { \mathrm { i n } }$ is the inherited hand position, and $t _ { 0 }$ marks the transition start. $\operatorname { A s } \lambda _ { t }$ decreases smoothly from one to zero, the hand moves from its inherited position onto the adapted trajectory. Since retargeting may increase the displacement between segments, we adjust transition durations according to the source hand’s linear and angular speeds, keeping grasp and release commands synchronized with the retimed motion. This timing adjustment is independent of the sampled walking speed. Initial hand perturbations are defined in the robot-local frame and incorporated through arm IK. After grasping, connecting motions lift the object before moving toward the next target to reduce contact with the supporting surface.

Stage 2: Physics-Grounded Rollout Validation. We execute each synthesized reference motion in simulation to evaluate its feasibility under the deployment controller. At each step, the SONIC encoder maps a reference-motion window $\mathcal { R } _ { t }$ to a 64-dimensional latent command $z _ { t } = E ( \mathcal { R } _ { t } )$ . The decoder combines this command with proprioceptive history $h _ { t }$ to produce joint targets $u _ { t } = D ( z _ { t } , h _ { t } )$ for low-level PD control. Two hand commands $\bar { g _ { t } } \in \bar { [ 0 , 1 ] ^ { \bar { 2 } } }$ are executed separately. We record the resulting actions $a _ { t } = ( z _ { t } , g _ { t } ) \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 6 6 }$ together with the executed robot and object states. Each rollout is evaluated against task success, stability, and collision criteria. Successful executions are added to ${ \mathcal { D } } ,$ while failed rollouts are examined for recoverable tracking errors. Deviations in the realized root, hand, or object poses, such as locomotion-endpoint or grasp-height errors, provide feedback for Stage 3. Falls, invalid collisions, and failures beyond the correction scope instead trigger resampling in Stage 1.

Stage 3: Execution-Conditioned Correction. Even when reference motions are kinematically valid, tracking deviations may alter the robot–object relations required for successful interaction. We therefore use rollout feedback to jointly correct the environment configuration and reference motion. Specifically, we compensate for planar tracking errors by adjusting relevant scene assets or interaction targets, while vertical grasp errors are corrected through reference-motion adaptation.

Robot-Pose-Guided ��(2) Scene Correction. Locomotion tracking errors can shift the executed robot pose away from its intended interaction configuration. We apply an $S E ( 2 )$ transformation to relevant scene assets to restore their intended spatial relations with the executed robot. Let $( x , \psi )$ and $( x ^ { \prime } , \psi ^ { \prime } )$ denote the intended and executed planar root poses, where $\boldsymbol { x } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ and $\psi$ denotes heading. For a scene asset at $( x _ { j } , \psi _ { j } )$ in the same world frame, we apply

$$
x _ {j} ^ {+} = x ^ {\prime} + R _ {2} (\psi^ {\prime} - \psi) (x _ {j} - x), \qquad \psi_ {j} ^ {+} = \psi_ {j} + \psi^ {\prime} - \psi .\tag{5}
$$

Here, $R _ { 2 } ( \cdot )$ denotes planar rotation, and the superscript + indicates the corrected asset pose. This transformation preserves the intended robot–asset relationship relative to the executed root pose.

Object �� (2) Correction. Scene-level alignment cannot fully correct local tracking errors during grasping or placement. We therefore adjust the interaction object or placement target using residual error relative to a successful source rollout:

$$
\Delta e _ {x y} = e _ {x y} ^ {\mathrm{cand}} - e _ {x y} ^ {\mathrm{src}}.\tag{6}
$$

Here, $e _ { x y } ^ { \mathrm { c a n d } }$ and $e _ { x y } ^ { \mathrm { s r c } }$ are the planar tracking errors of the candidate and successful source rollouts, measured at the corresponding grasp or release event. Each error is defined as the executed-minus-reference position of the interacting hand or carried-object anchor in a common planar frame. Their diference isolates the additional tracking deviation introduced in the candidate execution. We compensate by translating the interaction object or placement target by $\Delta e _ { x y }$ and adjusting its yaw according to task-specific rules. Robot-relative targets retain their source yaw ofset from the executed robot heading, while fixed-orientation targets preserve their configured yaw.

Z-axis Compensation. Planar adjustments cannot resolve vertical wrist–object misalignment during grasping. We compare the relative wrist heights at grasp closure in the candidate and successful source rollouts. The resulting discrepancy adjusts the reference wrist height to restore the source-relative grasp geometry. This correction is applied smoothly around grasp closure and converted to arm joint targets through IK, while keeping the object height fixed.

After correction, the updated environment and reference motion are re-executed in Stage 2. Only rollouts satisfying the same task success, stability, and collision criteria are accepted. These validated executions provide synchronized robot and object states, together with action labels, for subsequent rendering and policy learning.

## 3.3 Visual Observations and Real-World Deployment

We generate visual observations from physically validated executions rather than planned reference motions. By replaying the recorded robot and object states, we obtain synchronized egocentric views. We then combine Gaussian background renderings with simulator-rendered robots and objects through mask-guided z-bufer compositing to produce the final egocentric images.

Each rendered image $I _ { t }$ and the corresponding proprioceptive state $\rho _ { t }$ form an observation $o _ { t } = \left( I _ { t } , \rho _ { t } \right)$ , paired with the task instruction ℓ and recorded action $\boldsymbol { a } _ { t } = \left( z _ { t } , g _ { t } \right)$ . We train a visuomotor policy to predict �-step action chunks, $\left( { \hat { z } } _ { t : t + H - 1 } , { \hat { g } } _ { t : t + H - 1 } \right) = \pi _ { \theta } ( o _ { t } , \ell )$ . At deployment, predicted latents drive the SONIC decoder, while hand commands are executed separately through the same interface used during data generation. See Section 4.1 for deployment details.

## 4.1 Experimental Setup

System Setup. We use a Unitree G1 with 29 body degrees of freedom and two Inspire Robots RH56E2 hands, each controlled through a single scalar opening command. A head-mounted RealSense D455 provides egocentric RGB observations with automatic exposure disabled to reduce visual variation. The visuomotor policy takes RGB observations and a 34-dimensional robot state consisting of 29 body joint positions, three projected-gravity components, and two hand-opening values. It predicts 66-dimensional actions comprising a 64-dimensional SONIC motion latent and two hand-opening commands. We use real-time chunking (RTC) [42] for asynchronous inference and execution at 50 Hz. The frozen SONIC decoder converts motion latents into body joint targets, while hand commands are executed through a separate interface. The policy and SONIC run on an RTX 4090; simulation uses the same robot and hand configuration.

Real-world demonstrations are collected through PICO whole-body teleoperation with the SONIC controller [1]. Simulation data generation runs on eight RTX 4090 GPUs, and all policies are trained on eight A100 GPUs. Fig. 3 summarizes the deployment pipeline.

Figure 3. System Setup and Deployment. The policy maps egocentric RGB observations and robot state to SONIC motion latents and hand-opening commands. The frozen SONIC decoder produces body joint targets, while the two hands are controlled through a separate interface.

Tasks. We evaluate three long-horizon loco-manipulation tasks covering diverse whole-body skills:

• Task A: Mobile Pick-and-Place. The robot walks to the table, picks up a banana, and places it in a basket. The reported stages are Walk, Pick, and Place, where Place reports full-task success

• Task B: Low-Reach Hat Placement. The robot squats to pick up a hat, stands, carries it to a cabinet, and places it on the cabinet. The reported stages are Squat-Pick, Carry, and Place, where Place reports full-task success.

• Task C: Le<sub>g</sub>-Assisted Basket Trans<sub>p</sub>ort. The robot carries a fruit basket bimanually, closes an obstructing cabinet door with its foot, and places the basket on a stool. The reported stages are Bi-Pick, Kick, and Bi-Place, where Bi-Place reports full-task success.

Task sequences are illustrated in Fig. 9 (a).

Camera Calibration. We first define an upper-body motion trajectory that maintains visibility of the robot’s arms and hands. The physical robot executes this trajectory while recording egocentric RGB video and joint configurations. From selected video frames, we annotate 2D arm and hand landmarks and associate them with corresponding link-local 3D points from the robot URDF. Forward kinematics transforms these points into the camera mounting-link frame using the recorded joint configurations $q _ { t }$ . Given fixed camera intrinsics $K ,$ , we obtain per-frame extrinsic estimates through PnP and discard frames with large reprojection errors. We then jointly optimize a fixed transform � from the mounting-link frame to the camera optical frame by minimizing the robust reprojection error:

$$
T ^ {\star} = \underset {T \in \mathrm{SE} (3)} {\arg \min} \sum_ {(t, i) \in \mathcal {V}} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {2} \rho \left(\left[ \pi_ {K} \left(T \mathbf {x} _ {i} \left(q _ {t}\right)\right) - \mathbf {u} _ {t i} \right] _ {k} ^ {2}\right),\tag{7}
$$

where $\mathbf { u } _ { t i }$ denotes the annotated image location, $\pi _ { K }$ is the camera projection function, and $\rho$ is a soft- $\cdot \ell _ { 1 }$ loss.

Evaluation Configurations and Simulation Sampling. To systematically evaluate spatial generalization, we define two types of task configurations: Base denotes the reference configuration used for real-world demonstration collection, while Expanded introduces shifted robot starts and interaction locations for Tasks A and B; Task A additionally includes an alternative object–goal pair, whereas Task C uses only Base. As illustrated in Fig. 4, each Base or indexed Expanded location [�, �] defines the center of a local sampling region. Robot starts are sampled within a 30 × 30 cm region around each center, while picking and placement locations use a 5 × 5 cm region. For simulation data generation, Tab. 2 summarizes the task-specific perturbations around these configurations. Notably, in Task B, the hat and cabinet are additionally perturbed by ±5 cm and ±40 cm per axis, respectively, with hat yaw varied by ±20<sup>◦</sup>; the reference root height is lowered by 5–10 cm relative to the source pose. ## 4.4 Understanding Sim-to-Real Transfer

Can Simulation Reflect Real-World Performance? We examine sim–real consistency using Zero-HLM-Bench (Section 4.5). Specifically, we evaluate Task A policies trained on Real-Only(100) and Sim-Ours(100) in both simulation and the real world under matched Base configurations. As shown in Tab. 10, Real-Only(100) achieves 40% real-world success versus 31% in simulation, while Sim-Ours(100) achieves 35% and 39%, respectively. The corresponding sim–real gaps are 9 and 4 percentage points.

These results suggest that the benchmark captures a comparable performance range across both training sources without consistently overestimating real-world success. However, the reversed policy ranking indicates that agreement in absolute success rates does not guarantee consistent relative performance. Thus, Zero-HLM-Bench provides an approximate measure of task dificulty under matched configurations, rather than an exact predictor of policy rankings. Further benchmark evaluations, including model comparisons and data scaling, are presented in Section 4.5.

What Gaps Remain in Actions and Observations? To examine the transferability of simulated actions and visual observations, we evaluate the banana pick-and-place subtask in Task A, varying its picking location to construct four training sets (Fig. 10). R-25 contains 25 real demonstrations with the banana placed in the lower region of the table, while S-25 contains 25 simulation demonstrations with the banana in the upper region. Mix-50 combines both datasets to cover both picking regions. For Mix-50\*, we successfully replay the S-25 trajectories on the physical robot by manually adjusting object placements to match the simulated interactions. We then replace the simulated observations in S-25 with recorded real images while retaining the original simulation action labels. Combining these trajectories with R-25 yields Mix-50\*, which preserves the same action supervision as Mix-50.

As shown in Tab. 11, R-25 and S-25 fail to generalize to their respective unseen picking regions, achieving 0/5 success. Although Mix-50 covers both regions, it achieves only 2/5 and 4/5 success in the upper and lower regions, respectively. Notably, upper-region performance drops from 4/5 for S-25 to 2/5 after mixing, suggesting that broader spatial coverage alone does not ensure consistent improvements. In contrast, Mix-50\* achieves 5/5 success in both regions without changing the demonstration count or action supervision. These results support the transferability of simulation-generated action labels while identifying visual mismatch as an important limitation in sim–real co-training.

## 4.5 Zero-HLM-Bench: Humanoid Loco-Manipulation Evaluation

We introduce Zero-HLM-Bench, a humanoid loco-manipulation benchmark comprising the three task environments and nine skill stages under shared execution and evaluation protocols. We compare task-specific imitation learning, pretrained vision–language–action policies, and video–action modeling using the same generated training data.

## 4.5.1 Simulation Setup and Shared Interface

The benchmark instantiates the three task environments in IsaacLab with a simulated Unitree G1. Each policy receives egocentric RGB observations at 640 × 480 resolution and a 34-dimensional robot state consisting of 29 body joint positions, three projected-gravity components, and two hand states. Language-conditioned models additionally receive a fixed task instruction. Privileged object poses and contact states are used only for evaluation, never as policy inputs.

All models predict the same 66-dimensional action representation, consisting of a 64-dimensional SONIC motion latent and two hand commands. A frozen SONIC decoder converts the motion latent into body joint targets. Closed-loop rollouts run at 50 Hz for at most 2,000 control steps (40 s), with early termination upon task success or robot fall.

All models are evaluated using the same task-specific initialization distributions. Fig. 12 visualizes the task layouts and sampled configurations used for benchmark evaluation.

## 4.5.2 Model Training and Execution

All model comparisons use Sim-Ours(800)<sup>∗</sup> for Tasks A and B, and Sim-Ours(800) for Task C, with 800 generated trajectories per task. We retain each model’s native training recipe while adapting its observation and action interfaces to the shared benchmark format. Image preprocessing, normalization, and padding follow the corresponding pretrained checkpoint.

Diffusion Policy. We adopt the LeRobot implementation of Difusion Policy [43] for task-specific imitation learning using RGB observations, robot states, and action sequences. Images are resized to 224 × 224, with robot states and actions normalized during training. The policy executes 16 actions per update.

## 4.5.3 Evaluation Protocol and Metrics

Each model–task pair is evaluated over 100 simulation trials. We report full-task success rates alongside independently evaluated stage-wise performance to assess individual skill capabilities. Unlike full-task success, stage-wise evaluations measure each skill independently rather than requiring successful completion of all preceding stages.

Task A. Walk requires the robot to reach the designated target region without a fall or execution error. Pick requires lifting the object by at least 3 cm. Full-task success additionally requires the object to be placed inside the basket target region while satisfying the configured height and final-speed conditions.

Task B. Squat-Pick requires lifting the hat by at least 10 cm. Carry requires the hat to enter the target region above the configured minimum height. Full-task success requires the hat to remain within the cabinet-local placement region with suficiently low linear and angular velocity over the stability window.

Task C. Bi-Pick requires lifting the basket by at least 10 cm. Kick records successful activation of the cabinet-door condition. Bi-Place reports full-task success and requires the door condition to hold while the basket is supported and settled within the stool-seat region over the stability window.

The reported Avg. is computed as the arithmetic mean of full-task success rates across Tasks A–C, excluding intermediate-stage evaluations.

## 5 Conclusions

We presented Zero-HLM, a simulation-based data-generation framework that provides a practical path toward generalizable real-world humanoid loco-manipulation. Across three long-horizon tasks, policies trained solely on our simulation data transfer zero-shot to the real world and achieve higher mean full-task success than their real-data-trained counterparts. Expanding simulation coverage further improves transfer across spatial configurations without additional real demonstrations, showing that scalable synthetic coverage can translate into broader real-world policy generalization. Our transfer analysis supports the efectiveness of generated action labels while revealing a remaining visual-domain gap. The co-training results show that mixing real and simulation data does not necessarily outperform simulation-only training, underscoring the importance of visual alignment for efective co-training. Future work will extend the framework to additional unified whole-body controllers and examine how tracking accuracy and motion capability afect demonstration quality. Another important direction is to extend the benchmark to richer interaction skills and multiple reconstructed scenes, enabling a more systematic study of cross-scene policy generalization.

## B.2 GPT-6 Astra Baseline

This section details the implementation of the Sim-Astra baseline introduced in Sec. 4.1. GPT-6 Astra receives the same reconstructed scenes, robot and object assets, task configurations, and source demonstrations as our method, with additional access to the full simulator state. The agent autonomously designs its data-generation pipeline, either by synthesizing complete reference motions or issuing incremental closed-loop commands. It can use the SONIC planner, inverse kinematics, and whole-body control interfaces. Fig. 16 presents the complete Task B prompt, specifying the available inputs, execution constraints, success criteria, and recording requirements.

We report results from the stabilized pipeline following human-assisted debugging and task alignment. Sim-Astra generates 100 successful trajectories per task for Tasks A and B, but none for Task C. Simulator-side planning and most agent reasoning latency are excluded from the recorded demonstrations, which contain only the executed robot trajectories used for policy training.

The generated trajectories frequently exhibit pauses and repeated attempts, often exceeding one minute compared with approximately 30 seconds for real-world demonstrations. Policies trained on these data show similar hesitation during real-world deployment, suggesting that such temporal irregularities may carry over into learned behavior.
