Abstract— Humanoid whole-body control requires a robot
to track diverse reference motions while maintaining balance
under gravity, torque limits, contact switching, and external
disturbances. Existing reinforcement-learning-based trackers
typically learn control outputs that jointly address motion
tracking and balance. However, similar reference poses can
correspond to substantially different dynamic demands, re-
quiring a unified policy to learn motion-dependent control
requirements alongside balance corrections. This can complicate
learning and lead to high-frequency fluctuations in control
outputs. We propose DKWBC, a dynamics-informed whole-
body control framework that combines explicit model-based
tracking with a learned balancing residual. Inspired by the
model-based and environmental-wrench contributions to joint
torques in floating-base dynamics, DKWBC uses inverse-
dynamics feedforward and joint PD feedback to supply tracking
torques, while a residual policy learns additional joint-torque
corrections for balanced execution. By explicitly accounting
for motion-dependent torque demands, this structure simplifies
the policy’s learning task. Experiments in simulation and on
real hardware demonstrate that a single learned balancing
policy generalizes across motion datasets while maintaining
accurate motion tracking and balance across ground support,
attached payloads, and physical interaction. Project page:
https://robbyant-research.github.io/DKWBC/.
I. INTRODUCTION
Humanoid robots offer a promising platform for general-
purpose embodied intelligence, with the potential to move,
manipulate, and interact flexibly with the physical world [1].
Realizing this potential requires a single whole-body control
policy that enables accurate and stable execution of diverse
motions. Recent studies have advanced humanoid whole-body
control by scaling human motion datasets [2], developing
more expressive policy architectures [3], [4], and learning
richer representations of motion and dynamics [5]–[7].
Despite these recent advances, many approaches rely on
a policy to predict joint position targets that a proportional-
derivative (PD) controller converts into motor torques, jointly
addressing motion tracking and balance [2], [8]. Diverse
motions, however, impose different dynamic demands: even
similar reference poses can be associated with substantially
different velocities, accelerations, and required torques. A
unified policy must learn these motion-dependent control
requirements while maintaining balance under changing

We propose DKWBC, a whole-body control framework
that combines model-based motion tracking with learned
residual control. Its core idea is inspired by the floating-base
equations of motion [9], [10], where joint torques can be
expressed in terms of a model-based contribution determined
by the robot state and acceleration and a contribution from
environmental wrenches. As shown in Fig. 1, DKWBC con-
sists of a model-based tracking module that supplies nominal
tracking torques and a residual policy that provides additional
torque corrections for balanced execution. Specifically, the
tracking module computes inverse-dynamics feedforward
torques from the current state and reference acceleration,
supplemented by joint PD feedback, while the residual policy
learns additional joint torques through interaction with the
environment. By explicitly modeling motion-dependent torque
demands, this separation simplifies the policy’s learning task:

This shift in the learning task enables DKWBC to
outperform SONIC [2], a strong whole-body motion tracker,
in scaling experiments under comparable training settings.
On both BONES-SEED test sets, DKWBC achieves higher
tracking accuracy and success rates than retrained SONIC
across all evaluated training-data scales. Furthermore, by
focusing learning on balanced execution, DKWBC maintains
accurate motion tracking and balance under attached payloads
and external disturbances, as demonstrated in simulation and
real-robot tests. Our contributions are threefold:
1) We propose DKWBC, a whole-body control framework
that decouples motion tracking from balancing control
by combining model-based nominal tracking torques
with a learned balancing residual to maintain balance
while accurately tracking reference motions.
2) We design the two modules around their distinct
objectives: inverse dynamics and joint feedback provide
accurate motion tracking, while feedforward-torque ob-
servations and direct residual torque actions encourage
the policy to learn balancing capabilities shared across
motions.
3) We demonstrate that a single balancing policy general-
izes across motion datasets while maintaining accurate
motion tracking and balance across ground support,
attached payloads, and external interaction, with vali-
dation in simulation and on real hardware.
II. RELATED WORK
Humanoid whole-body motion tracking. Given a ref-
erence robot motion, a motion-tracking policy computes
actuator commands to reproduce the reference motion.
DeepMimic [11] established motion imitation for learning
simulated skills, while BeyondMimic [8] combines motion
tracking on real humanoids with guided diffusion for skill
composition. SONIC [2] scales motion data and policy
capacity, while GMT [3] and Humanoid-GPT [4] use mixture-
of-experts and causal Transformer policies, respectively.
Representation learning further supports general control:
UniTracker [5] encodes motion with a conditional variational
autoencoder, BFM-Zero [6] represents motions, goals, and
rewards in a shared latent space, and Any2Track [7] learns
dynamics embeddings through world-model prediction for
online adaptation. Our work complements these advances by
computing motion-tracking torques with inverse dynamics
and learning a support residual, changing the physical role
of the policy output.
Model-based and hybrid whole-body control. Classical
whole-body controllers use robot dynamics to compute joint
torques that track desired motions while maintaining balance
and satisfying contact constraints, such as keeping supporting
feet from slipping [1], [9]. Learning has long been used
to improve these controllers: Gams et al. learn corrections
to the desired center-of-mass acceleration within inverse-
dynamics control [10], SEEC combines model-based end-
effector tracking with model-guided residual learning for
arm stabilization, including transfer to unseen locomotion
controllers [12]. DKWBC instead decomposes the torques
at all actuated joints: inverse dynamics computes the motion-
tracking torque at execution time, while a learned support
residual handles support and external loading without an
online contact-force optimization. The residual can also
compensate for model errors; the decomposition alone does
not identify physical contact forces.
Residual tracking and policy adaptation. Residual
methods differ in both their baseline and the quantity being
corrected. HiLo [13] and RobotDancing [14] learn joint-
target corrections around reference motions. ASAP learns
a delta-action model from real rollouts and inserts it into
the simulator for policy fine-tuning [15]. OmniXtreme uses
residual post-training with actuator-aware modeling for highly
dynamic skills [16]. FADA separates a learned planner from
a neural inverse-dynamics module and adapts the latter with
target-domain rollouts [17]. We train the residual alongside
an analytic tracking component that computes the motion-
tracking torque by inverse dynamics at execution time. The
distinction is therefore the torque-level allocation of motion
tracking and support control, rather than the use of residual
learning alone.
Force adaptation and physical interaction. FALCON [18]
and HAFO [19] coordinate upper- and lower-body policies
for force-adaptive loco-manipulation; FALCON uses inverse
dynamics to construct its torque-aware training curriculum.
ResMimic refines a frozen general motion tracker with
task-specific residuals for object interaction [20], whereas
SceneBot conditions a general tracker on per-link contact
labels to support terrain and object interactions [21]. CHIP
enables controllable end-effector compliance through hind-
sight perturbation [22], while ADAPT supplies a locomotion
policy with analytical disturbance-observer estimates [23].
The YAHMP study further examines how hand-force ran-
domization and action parameterization affect tracking and
physical interaction [24]. We evaluate full-body motion
tracking and balance across ground support, attached payloads,
and interaction forces using one support residual and run-
time inverse-dynamics feedforward. This evaluation does
not establish explicit force regulation or manipulation-task
success.
III. METHOD
Our goal is to accurately track diverse reference motions
while maintaining balance. To this end, we present DKWBC,
which combines model-based tracking with learned residual
control. As shown in Fig. 2, DKWBC consists of an inverse-
dynamic based tracking module and a balancing policy.
Section III-A explains the dynamics underlying our control
decomposition, Section III-B describes the tracking module,
and Section III-C defines the balancing policy and its learning
objective.

Equation (2) reveals two contributions to the joint torque:
a model-based term that can be computed given the robot
state and acceleration, and an environmental wrench term
involving contact and external forces that are difficult to
observe directly. This motivates a control structure with

computes nominal tracking torques, and a residual policy that
learns additional torque corrections for balanced execution.
The two modules have distinct objectives and challenges.
The tracking module aims to follow the reference motion
as accurately as possible. A key challenge is that reference
motions retargeted from humans are kinematically feasible
but not necessarily dynamically feasible for the robot. The
balancing module uses reinforcement learning to maintain
balance while the robot executes the motion driven by the
supplied tracking torques. Within the motion-imitation RL
framework, its design should discourage the policy from
relying on motion-specific tracking compensation, which can
limit generalization across motions. Sections III-B and III-
C describe how we design the two modules around these
objectives and address their respective challenges.

C. Learning Residual Corrections for Balanced Execution
Reinforcement learning for motion imitation optimizes a
control policy through closed-loop interaction between the
robot and a simulated environment, using rewards based on
reference-motion tracking errors and measures of stability to
enable accurate and stable motion execution [8]. We retain
this framework for residual policy learning. To encourage
the policy to learn a generalizable ability to maintain
balance under the supplied tracking torques and discourage
tracking compensation, we introduce two modifications to its
observations and actions.
Tracking-torque observations. We explicitly expose the
nominal tracking demand by adding the three n-dimensional
feedforward components τ acc, τ cor, and τ grav from Eq. (4) to
the observation. This lets the policy condition its corrections
on the torque already supplied by the tracking module. The
actor otherwise follows BeyondMimic, using a single obser-
vation frame that contains the reference-motion command and
proprioceptive information, excluding quantities unavailable
at deployment. The last-action feature records the previous
combined policy and feedforward torque, τ bal,t−1 + τ id,t−1.
Direct torque actions. A common design in RL-based
motion tracking is to predict joint-position targets at a lower
frequency (e.g., 50 Hz) and convert them into torques through
a higher-frequency PD controller (e.g., 200 Hz). The policy
output thus passes through a position servo: even when the
output is held fixed, the torque changes at each PD update
with the current joint position and velocity. DKWBC instead
predicts residual joint torques directly at 50 Hz and holds
them constant between policy updates, while the tracking
module provides joint feedback around the reference motion.
This design is motivated by two considerations. First, the
policy supplies additional torque directly, rather than indirectly
generating torque by adjusting joint-position targets through
a position servo. This removes the pathway through which
learned position targets participate in fast joint feedback,
making it harder for the policy to use that pathway for motion-
specific tracking compensation. Second, the tracking module
already provides nominal tracking torques and joint-error
feedback. We therefore aim for the balancing policy to supply
the additional torques required for balanced execution and
to learn balancing capabilities shared across motions, rather
than duplicating the joint-error corrections already provided
by the tracking module.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
Our evaluation focuses on three aspects:
1) Whether the DKWBC policy learns more transferable
balancing capabilities as the amount of training motion
data increases.
2) Whether DKWBC can adapt to different external forces
while maintaining tracking and balance.
3) Ablations of DKWBC to examine explicit decoupling
and the effectiveness of its Tracking Module and
Balancing Execution.
A. Training-Data Scaling
a) Baseline: We compare DKWBC with SONIC [2], a
whole-body controller trained on large motion datasets. We
adapt its official training pipeline for G1 motion tracking,
removing VR inputs and SMPL parameters from motion-
encoder training.
b) Training Data: We use the BONES-SEED dataset [2]
for training. We manually remove motions that are infeasible
in our setting, such as leaning on external objects or
penetrating the ground, and discard mirrored motions. The
resulting dataset contains 128.53 hours of motion. To study
the effect of training-data scale, we uniformly sample subsets
of 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128 hours from this dataset for
training.
c) Evaluation Protocol: We evaluate success rate and
MPJPE on BONES-SEED test-content and test-repetition [2].
Following the thresholds in the official SONIC repository, a
motion is considered successful only if every frame satisfies
all three conditions: pelvis height error does not exceed 0.25
m, pelvis orientation error does not exceed 1 rad, and the
height error of each wrist and ankle does not exceed 0.25
m. For each checkpoint, success rate is the proportion of
successfully tracked motions in the full test set, and MPJPE
is averaged over those successful motions with equal weight
per motion. We report the mean success rate and MPJPE
across four checkpoints from the same training run for
each configuration, with shaded bands indicating ±1 sample
standard deviation of the success rate. Test-content contains
motions from categories unseen during training, while test-
repetition contains unseen motions from categories seen
during training. We manually exclude infeasible motions, such

B. Tracking under External Force
a) Baseline: We compare DKWBC with FALCON [18]
and ResMimic [20] to examine tracking during external-force
interaction. FALCON adapts standing and locomotion to
external forces, while ResMimic learns a residual around
a general motion tracker. We port the training code for
FALCON and ResMimic from their official repositories and
train and evaluate both methods on the same training set.
ResMimic trains a residual policy on top of a GMT policy.
We evaluate two variants, using either the officially released
GMT checkpoint or a GMT policy trained from scratch on
this experiment’s training set.
b) Dataset: To accommodate FALCON’s control inter-
face, we select standing and locomotion sequences from
G1-retargeted LAFAN and AMASS Test. The resulting
set contains 217 motions: 209 locomotion and 8 stance
sequences.
c) Evaluation Protocol: We apply time-varying three-
dimensional forces to both hands. During standing, the
forces perturb the robot horizontally and vertically; during
walking, the horizontal components oppose the commanded
motion. Force amplitudes are determined from the current

scaling the generated forces to nominal zero, half, and full
strength. Small random perturbations remain at αg = 0. We
report upper-body MPJPE, upper-body MPJVE, root-velocity
error, and success rate at each force level separately. As
shown in Table 1, DKWBC achieves the lowest errors on all
three tracking metrics at every force level, with comparable
success rates.

C. Ablation Studies
We conduct three groups of ablations to examine the
contribution of DKWBC’s explicit decomposition and the
effectiveness of its two components:
1) Explicit decoupling. Compare explicitly decoupled and
coupled formulations to assess the benefit of separating
tracking from balancing.
2) Tracking Module. Evaluate the tracking module with-
out the learned residual to assess its ability to reproduce
reference motions.
3) Balancing Execution. Examine the effectiveness of
learned balancing execution through its dependence on
the tracking components and dynamics information.
LAFAN contains a small number of long motion sequences.
To prevent a localized tracking failure from causing an entire
long sequence to be counted as unsuccessful, we divide these
sequences into clips of up to 10 seconds for evaluation.
1) Decoupled versus Coupled Control:
a) Baseline: We use BeyondMimic [8] as the coupled-
control baseline and DKWBC as the decoupled formulation,
comparing position-target tracking with explicit analytic
tracking plus a learned balancing residual. To accommodate
the larger training set, both methods use the same adaptive
motion sampling strategy and an enlarged policy network.
Apart from the policy prediction targets, we keep their training
settings identical, including rewards, domain randomization,
network architecture, training budget, and maximum episode
length, to ensure a fair comparison. Both methods are trained
for 10,000 updates on LAFAN. During training, we introduce
both attached payloads and environmental disturbances so
that the controllers learn to track motions under external
perturbations.
b) Evaluation Protocol: We evaluate on LAFAN and
assess transfer to BONES-SEED test-content and test-
repetition [2] using the same checkpoints without fine-tuning.
At test time, payloads and environmental disturbances are
evaluated separately: G denotes ground tracking without
additional loads, P covers a 5 kg backpack and 2 kg in each
hand, and I applies horizontal root-velocity perturbations.
Table 2 reports success rate together with MPJPE and
MPJVE. Both errors are computed on the exact set of
motions successfully completed by both methods within each
condition, with equal weight per motion. For P, we first
compute each payload condition separately and then average
their results with equal weight.
DKWBC achieves higher success and lower position and
velocity errors on LAFAN and both BONES-SEED test splits
under all G/P/I conditions. Although the two methods achieve
similar success rates, coupled control incurs substantially
larger tracking errors. One possible explanation is that the
coupled policy favors more conservative control outputs,
maintaining stability at the expense of tracking accuracy.
These results show that DKWBC’s gains in tracking accuracy
and motion completion extend to unseen motion datasets
under payloads and external disturbances.

c) Action design: Full DKWBC achieves higher success
and lower MPJPE and MPJVE than the action variants on both
datasets. Removing both ID feedforward and actor ID observa-
tions (wo ID (FF + obs)) further reduces success and increases
tracking errors compared with removing ID feedforward alone
(wo ID), indicating that dynamics observations remain useful
when only reference-motion PD feedback is retained. With
the full Tracking Module, torque prediction improves tracking
accuracy over PD-target prediction on BONES-SEED test-
repetition, although the gain is smaller than the gain over the
torque-only policy. Without the Tracking Module, however,
Policy only (PD target) achieves higher success than Policy
only (torque) on both datasets, while its tracking errors
are higher on BONES-SEED test-repetition. These results
support pairing torque prediction with analytic tracking to
separate motion following from balanced execution; they do
not establish a general advantage of torque actions without a
tracker. The comparisons with matched training budgets also
support retaining PD feedback.
d) Observation design: Removing all or any one of the
actor ID components improves success and lowers MPJPE
on LAFAN, which shares the training motion set. MPJVE
does not improve consistently: only removing the Coriolis
component lowers it. This is because removing these obser-
vations makes the policy more prone to overfitting. On the
held-out BONES-SEED test-repetition set, every observation
ablation reduces success and increases both MPJPE and
MPJVE. Thus, improved fitting on LAFAN does not translate
into better transfer. This pattern supports our interpretation
that explicit tracking components provide useful context for
learning transferable balancing execution, encouraging the
policy to account for the analytic tracker’s contribution rather
than relearn motion-specific tracking compensation.
V. CONCLUSION
We presented DKWBC, a humanoid whole-body control
framework that separates model-based motion tracking from
learned support control. By explicitly supplying nominal
tracking torques through floating-base inverse dynamics
and joint feedback, DKWBC reframes policy learning as
achieving balanced execution across diverse reference motions.
Across the tested training-data scales, DKWBC achieves
higher success rates and lower tracking errors than the adapted
SONIC baseline on both BONES-SEED test sets. Further
experiments demonstrate improved cross-dataset transfer and
tracking under payloads and external disturbances, while
ablations highlight the contribution of inverse-dynamics
observations to generalization beyond the training motions.
Tests on the Unitree G1 demonstrate diverse motion execution
and tracking under attached payloads. Together, these results
support explicitly modeling nominal motion dynamics as
a foundation for learning transferable support capabilities
while preserving tracking accuracy. Future work will explore
extending this decomposition to a broader range of whole-
body physical interaction tasks.
