## Abstract

Deployment objectives often specify desired outcomes without providing revised trajectories, while parts ofthe original reference must still befollowed. We present OneSteer, a onestep diffusion whole-body controller that combines general motion tracking with selective test-time steering. To accommodate new objectives, differentiable task costs guide optimization of the generator’s input noise with frozen weights, while body-group reference masks relax selected tracking requirements. Predicted state–action plans provide a shared interfacefor task optimization andfeedback-conditioned execution. For nominal tracking, the model trainedfrom scratch achieves the lowest local tracking error among the evalu ated baselines on successful held-out AMASS and BONES clips. For steering, one frozen distilled controller adapts across diverse deployment objectives in simulation, raising the clean passage rate under a 1.25 m barfrom 2% to 90%. On a physical Unitree G1, OneSteer completes all 27 nominal tracking trials across nine diverse whole-body motion sequences without falling. With a steering cost derived from a signed distance field built using only onboard

LiDAR, it also passes under a 1.1 m low bar infour offive attempts and steps over a 12 cm hurdle without falling in all three attempts. Beyond control, prediction variation in the model trained from scratch ranks likely tracking failures without physics rollouts. This scoring method is more than 10×faster than simulation-based scoring withfive rollouts per clip and guides targeted fine-tuning. Project page: https://onesteer.github.io.

## 1. Introduction

General motion tracking enables a single humanoid policy to follow diverse reference motions [8, 15, 16, 35, 48]. References encode detailed timing and whole-body coordination, but deployment often imposes requirements beyond faithful reproduction. A robot may need to clear obstacles, stabilize a tray, or operate under reduced torque limits. These requirements specify desired outcomes without supplying revised whole-body trajectories. Meeting them calls for adapting the motion while reusing the coordination learned through general tracking.

Editing the reference with an upstream planner offers one route to steering [18, 54, 64], but the resulting trajectory must still be executed by a separate tracker, which can introduce a gap between planned and executed motion [65]. Guided generative controllers offer another route by steering learned behavior priors with task costs [20, 30, 62, 65]. However, these controllers are conditioned on goals, keyframes, text, or a trajectory rather than on a full-body reference window. For general tracking, the controller must adapt to the new objective while continuing to follow the reference components that have not been relaxed. Since both the prescribed motion and the deployment objective can vary, this raises a central question: Can a single controller reuse its tracking competence across new motion–objective combinations?

This calls for a reference-conditioned controller that supports efficient test-time steering. We introduce OneSteer (Fig. 1), a whole-body controller built around a one-step diffusion generator. Conditioned on proprioception and a future reference window, the generator maps input noise directly to a short state–action plan in one forward pass. This supports low-latency nominal tracking and allows each task-guidance update to differentiate through a single generator evaluation, without unrolling an iterative denoising chain.

For new objectives, we optimize the generator’s input noise using differentiable task costs while keeping network weights fixed. Body-group reference masks relax selected tracking requirements, while unmasked components remain tracking targets. An inverse-dynamics head combines the optimized near-term states with current robot feedback to produce commands, followed by replanning at the next control step. The state plan thus serves as a shared interface for task optimization and feedback-conditioned execution, allowing new objectives to reuse the same tracking controller.

The predictive representation also provides an empirical signal of tracking difficulty. With proprioception and the reference held fixed, we measure variation across plans generated from different noise inputs and use it to rank likely tracking failures without physics rollouts for scoring. The resulting ranking guides targeted fine-tuning.

We train the controller either by distillation from an RL teacher or from scratch using model-based RL, and evaluate tracking on held-out AMASS and BONES motions. On successful clips, the model trained from scratch achieves the lowest local tracking error among the evaluated baselines on both benchmarks: 26.5 mm on AMASS and 22.6 mm on BONES. Nominal inference for the distilled controller takes 3.8 ms per generator call on an RTX 4090 GPU. On a physical Unitree G1, OneSteer completes all 27 nominal tracking trials across nine diverse whole-body motion sequences without falling. With a steering cost derived from a signed distance field built using only onboard LiDAR, it also passes under a 1.1 m low bar in four of five attempts and steps over a 12 cm hurdle without falling in all three attempts. In simulation, one frozen distilled controller adapts across obstacle clearance, reaching, reduced torque limits, tray stabilization, and velocity commands. Across 79 walking motions from unseen actors, guidance increases the clean passage rate under a 1.25 m bar from 2% to 90%. For failure assessment, prediction variation on a model trained from scratch attains 90% precision among the highest-ranked 2% of 7,884 reference clips. Scoring requires no physics rollouts and is more than 10× faster than simulation-based scoring with five rollouts per clip.

Our contributions are:

• One-step predictive general tracking. We develop a reference-conditioned whole-body controller that generates state–action plans in one forward pass, enabling lowlatency tracking and differentiable test-time refinement. The distilled controller achieves 3.8 ms nominal inference on an RTX 4090 GPU.

• Selective steering with frozen weights. We combine cost-guided input optimization, body-group reference relaxation, and feedback-conditioned execution to adapt the same tracking controller to new objectives while retaining unmasked tracking targets. In simulation, one frozen distilled controller raises the clean passage rate under a 1.25 m bar from 2% to 90% across 79 walking motions from unseen actors.

• Failure discovery from prediction variation. We introduce an empirical score based on variation across generated plans to rank likely tracking failures and guide targeted fine-tuning. On the model trained from scratch, it attains 90% precision among the highest-ranked 2% of 7,884 reference clips, with scoring requiring no physics rollouts and running more than 10× faster than simulationbased scoring with five rollouts per clip.

## 2. Related Work

Humanoid motion tracking. Physics-based imitation [43] has scaled to general humanoid trackers that follow arbitrary reference motions. SONIC [35] trains on hundreds of hours of motion capture with unified tokens, BFM-Scale [68] treats tracking as pre-training for a behavior foundation model, MimicLite [48] provides an efficient training and deployment recipe, and GMT [8], HOVER [16], OmniH2O [15], UniTracker [67], RGMT [37], OmniTrack [28] and MO-SAIC [51] improve robustness and coverage. These controllers are deterministic policies whose behavior is fixed by the reference. Several works add flexibility on top of tracking. Latent skill models such as ASE [44], CALM [52] and PULSE [34] reuse a learned latent space but train a new high-level policy per task. BFM-Zero [27] embeds motions, goals and rewards in a shared latent space with forward-backward representations, which makes the policy promptable but requires careful RL training. Perceptive BFM [61] and ViBe [24] add vision or RL adaptation, at the cost of terrain-specific data or retraining when the task changes, and ConstrainedMimic [41] wraps an RL tracker with operational-space barrier functions. In our work, the tracker itself is generative, so any differentiable cost can be applied at test time without retraining.

Generative whole-body control. Generative models [1, 21, 56] enter whole-body control in two ways. Hierarchical pipelines such as CLoSD [54], TextOp [64], UniAct [22], OMG [18], TEXEDO [5] and Trace and Pace [47] use a generative planner to produce or edit kinematic references [69] for a separate low-level tracker. Even with closed-loop replanning, the planner does not know what the tracker can execute, and the resulting planner–controller mismatch [65] leads to infeasible motions and collisions. Integrated controllers such as BeyondMimic [30], Diffuse-CLoC [20], PredActor [65], UniPhys [62], SCDP [6] and ADAPT [63] instead model states and actions jointly (Tab. 1), but they are conditioned on sparse goals, keyframes or text rather than full-body reference horizons, and therefore cover the tasks seen in training rather than general tracking. Concurrently, OmniXtreme [59] tracks highly dynamic skills with a multi-step flow policy but does not support test-time steering. OneSteer is an integrated controller conditioned on a sliding full-body reference window: it tracks general motions in one step, and its plans can be steered by costs in the closed loop.

One-step generation and training-free guidance. The latency of iterative diffusion [9] can be removed by distillation [26, 33, 45, 60], by flow-based objectives that admit onestep sampling [13, 32], or by training one-step generators directly, as in drifting models [11] and priming models [42], which first train the generator on a denoising reconstruction objective. In legged locomotion, RFPO [19] distills an RL policy into a one-step rectified-flow policy, without reference tracking or steering. To our knowledge, OneSteer is the first to bring one-step generation to whole-body control for general humanoid motion tracking. Training-free guidance optimizes differentiable costs through the generator at inference time [2, 10, 17]. Optimizing the input noise of a frozen generator has been used for motion editing (DNO [23]) and image generation [3, 12, 58]; these methods optimize each sample offline, through a multi-step sampler or, in ReNO, a one-step generator, whereas OneSteer optimizes the input of a one-step generator inside a 50 Hz control loop. In robotics, SWAP [66] samples plans above a tracker, and DSRL [57] steers a diffusion policy by RL in its noise space. Our steering backpropagates analytic cost gradients through the onestep generator onto its input noise, restricted to selected body-group channels, and requires neither demonstrations nor a learned critic.

Partial references and failure discovery. Masked tracking frameworks such as MaskedMimic [53] and AnyBody [25] follow partial pose targets. In OneSteer, masking additionally hands the masked body groups over to a cost while the rest of the body keeps tracking. To find the motions a tracker fails on, prior work runs the policy in simulation; Extreme-RGMT [36], for example, uses five rollouts per clip over large motion libraries. We instead use the spread of plans across input noise as a simulation-free estimate of tracking difficulty.

## 3. Method

OneSteer uses a predictive state–action plan as a shared interface for general tracking, test-time steering, and feedbackconditioned execution (Fig. 2). Nominal tracking needs one generator and one head pass; a new objective steers the generator input by gradient descent on a cost, with the network weights fixed. The same predictions also support failure assessment. Dimensions, frames, constants, cost definitions, the analysis of the spread, the fine-tuning protocol, and the s.d. and run counts of all results are given in the appendix.

## 3.1. Predictive General Tracking

Reference conditioning. At control step k, the controller receives proprioception $o _ { k }$ and a future reference window $r _ { k }$ of root poses and joint angles. We split each frame into root, legs, and upper-body groups. A visibility mask $m _ { \mathrm { r e f } } \in$ $\{ 0 , 1 \} ^ { 3 }$ keeps visible groups (value 1) as tracking targets and replaces hidden groups with learned [MASK] embeddings. Group MLPs and FSQ [40] produce reference tokens, which join the proprioceptive token to form the condition $c _ { k } =$ $\mathrm { E n c } _ { \theta } \big ( o _ { k } , r _ { k } ; m _ { \mathrm { r e f } } \big )$ , where Enc is the encoder and θ also includes the generator’s parameters below. Random masking during training teaches completion under partial references.

One-step plan generation. A plan $\boldsymbol { X } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { H \times d }$ has H tokens that sample the near future densely and later times sparsely. Each token has d entries: a post-action state (root pose and velocity, joint positions and velocities, relative to the current robot pose) and the joint-target action that leads to it. Given the condition $c _ { k }$ above and an input $z _ { k } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { H \times d }$ with independent standard Gaussian entries, the transformer generator predicts

$$
\hat {X} _ {k} = f _ {\theta} (z _ {k}, c _ {k}),\tag{1}
$$

where θ collects the parameters of the generator and the reference encoder. One forward pass gives the whole plan, which allows frequent replanning and a direct differentiable map from generator input to plan.

Feedback execution. The robot does not execute the planned actions. An inverse-dynamics head with parameters ϕ computes the command as

$$
a _ {k} = g _ {\phi} \big (o _ {k}, \hat {s} _ {1: 4}, r _ {k}; m _ {\mathrm{act}} \big),\tag{2}
$$

where $\hat { s } _ { 1 : 4 }$ are the first four planned states of $\hat { X } _ { k }$ and $r _ { k }$ is the reference window with the groups hidden by a second mask $m _ { \mathrm { a c t } } \in \{ 0 , 1 \} ^ { 3 }$ zeroed. The head recomputes the command from the planned states, current feedback, and the visible reference, so a change to the planned states changes what the robot does; since it also sees the reference, it is a plan-conditioned tracker rather than a pure inverse-dynamics model, and $m _ { \mathrm { a c t } }$ hides the groups it should not follow. Only $a _ { k }$ is applied before replanning from updated feedback and a shifted reference window. In nominal tracking all groups are visible to both the generator and the head; the masks are set separately otherwise (Sec. 3.2).

## 3.2. Test-Time Steering

Cost-guided input refinement. A differentiable task cost C(X) scores a plan, for example by how deeply it penetrates an obstacle or how far a hand is from a target. Since $\hat { X } =$ $f _ { \theta } ( z , c _ { k } )$ , the cost can be lowered by changing the generator input z with the weights fixed (Fig. 3). One update moves z by a step of length α against the cost gradient:

![](images/a6eaae0fe9899b126a231bf9adb9efaa68366fed47465b38fdd55157a5853c05.jpg)  
Figure 3. Steering moves the plan. Four time steps (left to right) of one rollout under a 1.25 m bar. Top: unsteered; grey ghosts show the 0.96 s plan, red marks planned poses that hit the bar, and gold is the reference. Bottom: the same reference with test-time steering; the plan (blue) ducks under the bar and the robot executes it. Weights, reference and executor are unchanged.

$$
z \leftarrow z - \alpha \frac {G}{\| G \|}, \quad G = M _ {\text { guide }} \odot \nabla_ {z} C \big (f _ {\theta} (z, c _ {k}) \big).\tag{3}
$$

Here ⊙ is the elementwise product, ∥ · ∥ the Frobenius norm over the whole tensor, and $M _ { \mathrm { g u i d e } } \stackrel { \cdot \cdot } { \in } \{ 0 , 1 \} ^ { H \times d }$ selects the input entries the update may change, for example those of the legs; since the transformer mixes entries, this restricts the update, not the plan. The step length is $\alpha = s \cdot t \cdot \gamma \cdot { \sqrt { H d } } .$ with guidance strength s, noise level t of the input $\scriptstyle ( t = 1$ for pure noise, $t { = } t _ { 2 }$ in the second call below), and a factor $\gamma = \operatorname* { m i n } ( 1 , w / \rho )$ that shortens the step as the plan nears satisfying the task. Here w is the largest violation of the task constraint in the current plan (for example, the deepest penetration) and $\rho$ a small tolerance, so the push fades once it is met.

Two generator calls. Nominal tracking needs one generator call; the controller deployed on the robot keeps the two calls used for steering. When steering, we call it twice. The first call turns pure noise into a draft plan $\hat { X } ^ { ( 1 ) }$ . The second call starts from this draft, mixed with fresh noise at a level $t _ { 2 }$ that the generator is trained to repair [42], as in SDEdit [39], and returns the final plan $\hat { X } ^ { ( 2 ) }$ . Each call computes $\hat { X } ^ { ( n ) } = f _ { \theta } ( z ^ { ( n ) } , c _ { k } )$ from the input

$$
z ^ {(1)} = \epsilon \sim \mathcal {N} (0, I),\tag{4}
$$

$$
z ^ {(2)} = (1 - t _ {2}) \hat {X} ^ {(1)} + t _ {2} \epsilon^ {\prime},\tag{5}
$$

where $\epsilon ^ { \prime }$ is a fresh noise draw. Before the second call, J updates (3) are applied to its input $z ^ { ( 2 ) }$ (steering the first call as well is a variant), and $\hat { X } _ { k } \stackrel { \bf = } { = } \hat { X } ^ { ( 2 ) }$ goes to the inversedynamics head. Steering acts mainly through the second call: the loss-optimal generator maps every pure-noise input to nearly the same plan (Sec. 3.4), so its output responds to the input mostly when the input carries a draft (1.25 m bar: 0.02 clean if only the first call is steered, 0.90 if only the second).

Costs. A cost only needs to be differentiable with respect to the plan. Torque costs use the torques implied by the plan’s predicted actions, not the command recomputed by the head. Costs are given at test time and never seen in training.

Selective reference relaxation. When an objective conflicts with a reference group, hiding that group removes its tracking target while the others stay as conditions. To hand a group to the cost, it must be hidden from the head $( m _ { \mathrm { a c t } } ) ;$ : a head that still sees the reference follows it and works against the cost. Hiding it from the generator too $( m _ { \mathrm { r e f } } )$ is optional. The guidance mask $M _ { \mathrm { g u i d e } }$ is a different object: it selects the input entries the update may change. For example, hiding the upper-body reference lets a reaching cost steer that group while root and legs keep tracking. Visible groups remain tracking targets, not guarantees: the steered motion can deviate from them.

## 3.3. Training

Both training routes use BONES-SEED [4], AMASS [38], and LAFAN [14], without deployment-task demonstrations. For a clean plan $X ^ { 0 }$ , the generator is trained with

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {L} _ {\mathrm{gen}} = \mathbb {E} \Big [ \| f _ {\theta} (X ^ {(t)}, c) - X ^ {0} \| _ {F} ^ {2} \Big ], \\ X ^ {(t)} = (1 - t) X ^ {0} + t \epsilon , \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

where c is the condition $c _ { k }$ of (1), ϵ has independent standard Gaussian entries, and t = sigmoid(u) with $u \sim \mathcal { N } ( \mu _ { u } , \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } )$ As in the one-step recipe of [42], t is not a network input; in high dimension the noise level is evident from the input [50].

RL-teacher distillation. DAgger [49] gradually transfers control from a frozen MimicLite teacher [48] to the student, and the teacher labels every visited state. The plan targets $X ^ { 0 }$ are the future states the robot actually visited with the teacher’s actions. The head is trained separately by regression onto the teacher’s action, from a perturbed one-step generator sample under random reference masks; its loss does not update the generator.

Model-based RL from scratch. A second route learns a world model from simulated transitions and optimizes tracking and survival objectives through imagined rollouts with a terminal value critic [29], for a separate instance of the same architecture. The RL gradient reaches the head but stops at the planned states. These stay meaningful because the generator is also trained with $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { g e n } }$ on plans actually realized in the replay buffer (hindsight), and the head with an inverse-dynamics loss on the true next states.

## 3.4. Failure Assessment from Prediction Variation

The input noise carries no information about the future and, in high dimension, is recognized as pure noise, so the lossoptimal generator maps every pure-noise input to nearly the same plan. Variation across plans from different noise inputs therefore measures how far the trained generator is from that optimum on the motion (Fig. 4). For each clip, we replay the reference as the robot’s state, with no policy rollout. At each evaluated step $k ,$ we draw K independent noise inputs $\epsilon ^ { 1 } , \dots , \epsilon ^ { K }$ with proprioception and reference fixed, and generate $\hat { X } _ { k } ^ { ( n ) } = f _ { \theta } ( \epsilon ^ { n } , c _ { k } )$ . Let $\hat { q } _ { k , h , j } ^ { ( n ) }$ be the planned position of joint $j \in \mathcal I$ in token $h \in \mathcal H$ of plan $n _ { \mathrm { { ; } } }$ , where H excludes the first few tokens. The spread at step k and the clip score are

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sigma_ {k} = \frac {1}{| \mathcal {H} | | \mathcal {J} |} \sum_ {h \in \mathcal {H}} \sum_ {j \in \mathcal {J}} \mathrm{std} _ {n} \big (\hat {q} _ {k, h, j} ^ {(n)} \big), \\ S = \mathrm{Quantile} _ {0. 9 5} \big (\{\sigma_ {k} \} _ {k} \big), \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

where $\operatorname { s t d } _ { n }$ is the standard deviation across the K plans. High-scoring clips guide targeted fine-tuning with library replay and a consolidation loss. The score is an empirical proxy for tracking difficulty, not a calibrated failure probability; a low score can coexist with a biased plan.

Q3 Can the identical frozen controller adapt without training to diverse task objectives and body-group reference masks (Sec. 4.3)? Q4 Can the spread of plans diagnose tracking failures without simulation, and does fine-tuning on flagged motions repair them (Sec. 4.4)?

## 4.1. Q1: General Tracking Fidelity, Dual Training, and Physical Hardware Deployment

Protocol. All trackers run in the same MuJoCo [55] sim-tosim harness with their official policies at 50 Hz, on 150 heldout windows of AMASS [38] subjects and all 512 held-out windows of BONES-SEED actors. Following SONIC [35], a clip fails if the pelvis height deviates from the reference by more than 0.25 m; on successful clips we report the rootrelative error $E _ { l }$ and the median global error $E _ { g }$

Results. Tab. 2 benchmarks OneSteer against five competitive general-purpose humanoid trackers (six checkpoints). Distilled OneSteer performs on par with its RL teacher (local error 31.4 vs. 29.9 mm on AMASS, 27.3 vs. 27.0 mm on BONES); its larger global error than SONIC on AMASS comes from drift on near-static poses, as for the teacher. Trained from scratch (Sec. 3.3), OneSteer has the lowest local tracking error of all six baselines on both benchmarks (26.5 mm on AMASS, 22.6 mm on BONES) and the highest success and lowest median global drift on BONES, so the generative formulation costs no local tracking accuracy. One generator call tracks as well as two (in the harness of Tab. 2, 96.0% / 94.9% success with either, E 31.9 vs. 32.0 / 27.2 vs. 27.3 mm), so the second call is free to serve steering (Sec. 3.2). Steering (Q2, Q3) uses the distilled model, so that it is compared with editing the teacher’s reference in the same setting.

Latency and real-robot deployment. On an RTX 4090 GPU, a nominal tracking step takes 3.8 ms per generator call (5.3 ms for the robot’s two calls). On the robot’s Jetson Orin NX, timed offline under the same measurement, tracking with two calls takes 4.2 ms and steering the second call or both calls 14.4 or 18.2 ms (Tab. S8 in the appendix), within the 20 ms period of the 50 Hz loop. On a physical Unitree G1, the distilled OneSteer, exported to ONNX, completed all 27 trials (nine whole-body motions, three each) without a fall. With an SDF cost from a fixed LiDAR map built before each run, it passed under a 1.1 m bar in four of five attempts and stepped over a 12 cm hurdle without falling in all three (once without touching it), where the unsteered controller failed every attempt (Fig. 5); in these trials the steered policy ran on a tethered RTX 4090 computer.

## 4.2. Q2: Generative In-Controller Steering vs. Kinematic Reference Editing

Tasks and baselines. We steer 79 walking motions of unseen actors past 10 cm hurdles (feasible pass), under a low bar at 1.30, 1.25 or 1.20 m (clean pass) and a wrist target (hit), three runs each. A deterministic tracker can instead edit its reference under the same cost; the teacher tracks a reference edited offline or re-optimized in closed loop.

Results. On obstacles, steering inside the generator beats every reference editor in Tab. 3, including editors re-optimized from the robot’s state in closed loop and editors free to move the root: by a wide margin under the low bar, and by a smaller one on hurdles. A planner followed by a separate tracker also falls short: the teacher tracking our own steered plan passes the 1.25 m bar in 0.74 of the trials instead of 0.90, so the gain also relies on the head trained with the generator. In the real-time setting used on the robot (second call guided), OneSteer passes 0.68 of the hurdles against at most 0.58 for editing, and under the 1.25 m bar 0.90 against at most 0.11. The cause is execution, not planning: the edited references clear the bar, but where the edit lowers the pelvis by 6.2 cm the teacher realizes 1.0 cm, as a tracker trained on natural motions does not follow such edits; steered OneSteer lowers its pelvis by 5.9–6.6 cm, since its executor is trained to follow its own plan (Fig. 3). In reaching, real-time steering (0.57 hits) far exceeds every real-time editor (at most 0.38) and nearly matches closed-loop editing (0.60) at under a twentieth of its computation; 12 smaller steps reach 0.75. Controls attribute the gain to the cost gradient on the generator input (Tab. S21 in the appendix): a random update of equal norm and best-of-eight sampling yield little improvement; supplying OneSteer with the edited reference yields gains comparable to those of the teacher; and optimizing the generated plan directly improves reaching but degrades obstacle passage and loses the tray’s ball in nearly every window. Our steering covers generators from one step to many: a multi-step flow version of our model is steered the same way with comparable task results, while the onestep generator makes nominal tracking cheaper and steering smoother (Tab. S22 in the appendix). The settings, chosen on these windows, carry over unchanged to 79 held-out walking windows and to held-out dance.

## 4.3. Q3: Training-Free Multi-Objective Adaptation and Body-Group Handover

Multi-objective training-free steering. Without finetuning or demonstrations, the same frozen OneSteer adapts to new objectives through a cost (Fig. 6). Actuator faults: with a knee or hip motor limited to 15% of its torque and only the cost aware of the defect, penalizing torques above the degraded limit raises survival from 0.71±0.01 to 0.79±0.01 (knee) and from 0.79±0.02 to 0.84±0.01 (hip). Payload: holding a tray with a free rolling ball, a cost on the hand’s tilt halves ball loss (0.16 to 0.08). Velocity commands: with the leg and upper-body references masked, a velocity cost turns OneSteer into an omnidirectional velocity policy (error 0.159 to 0.112 m/s).

Body-group handover and sparse tracking. Masking a reference group hands its degrees of freedom to the cost, which matters when the reference opposes the cost (Tab. S14 in the appendix). For a velocity command the reference is a standing pose, so a tracker that still sees it shuffles: hiding the legs and upper body from the generator alone barely changes the error, whereas hiding them from the executor as well lowers it from 0.218 to 0.159 m/s. Where the reference does not oppose the cost, masking is harmless: with legs masked, OneSteer clears hurdles and low bars at the same rates as full tracking, nominal survival is unchanged without costs, and the visible groups keep following the reference.

## 4.4. Q4: Simulation-Free Failure Discovery and Targeted Fine-Tuning

Across the 7,884 training-library sequences, the nominal tracker fails on 537 clips (6.8%); held-out skill families test generalization below. Among the top 2% of clips ranked by the spread of plans across noise draws, 91% are real failures (90% when whole clips are scored), near the 93% of imagined world-model rollouts, with no model beyond the generator (Fig. 7a); on the distilled model, which has no world model, it reaches 86% (base rate 8.4%). Over the whole ranking the spread trails an RL critic and imagined rollouts (AUC 0.83 against 0.86 and 0.91), which need a learned value function or world model; at the top of the ranking, which is what selection uses, it comes close to imagined rollouts. Finetuning on the 329 clips it selects (Fig. 7b; three training seeds) fixes 48±4% of the base model’s 501 failures on a rerun of the library (7,788 clips) (rerunning alone: 12±5%; outside its own 329 clips: 44±6%), above the 35±6% of 329 random clips from the same candidate pool (27±2% from the whole library) and the 41±2% of clips ranked by an RL critic under the same recipe, and on par with 329 failures found by one simulated rollout per clip (51±4%), while regressions stay at 1.8±0.2% and the local error on 40 hard clips the base model tracks rises only slightly (35.3 vs. 33.6 mm). The repair generalizes beyond the selected clips: success on 110 held-out failures from unseen skill families rises to 48±2% (random: 35±4%; critic: 38±3%), above clips selected by imagined rollouts (43±1%), which need policy rollouts and a world model. Scoring needs no simulator and, on whole clips, is 11–28× cheaper than the five simulated rollouts per clip that Extreme-RGMT [36] uses for a reliable label, and 2.4× cheaper than one rollout per clip (Sec. S5 in the appendix).

## 5. Conclusion and Future Work

We presented OneSteer, to our knowledge the first one-step diffusion whole-body controller for general humanoid tracking. Trained from scratch, it has the lowest local tracking error among the evaluated general trackers, and its distilled version runs on a Unitree G1; with frozen weights, costs on its plan and body-group masks adapt it to new objectives while the unmasked reference is still followed; and the spread of its plans finds tracking failures without simulation to guide fine-tuning.

Future work. Vision: depth images or egocentric views can join the condition as tokens next to the reference, as the height scan does in our terrain variant, so that the plan itself anticipates obstacles and uneven ground; a cost could then also be read from the camera, e.g. a distance field predicted from images instead of a pre-built map. Force control: pushing, carrying or opening doors require sustained contact forces, which a kinematic plan does not represent; adding wrench or tactile tokens to the condition and contact forces to the plan would let a cost prescribe a force instead of a pose. In both cases the generator, steering and failure assessment carry over unchanged.

## S1. Implementation details

Tab. S1 lists the architecture, training, steering and evaluation settings that the main text refers to.

Steering costs. The costs used in the experiments are: (i) obstacle avoidance, with collision capsules on the G1 evaluated by differentiable forward kinematics against the signeddistance field (SDF) of oriented boxes; (ii) reaching, a hinge loss on the distance between an end effector and a target; (iii) pelvis-relative drawing, the distance of a hand to prescribed 3D curves in the pelvis frame; (iv) weak motors, a soft penalty on joint torques $k _ { p } ( q _ { \mathrm { d e f } } + \kappa a - q ) - k _ { d } \dot { q }$ above the degraded motor limits, with action scale κ; (v) tray balancing, a penalty on the tilt of a hand-held tray from vertical; (vi) velocity commands, a planar root-velocity error with foot-slip regularization. The commands are 64 synthetic clips of 10 s: 1 s of standing, then low-pass-filtered segments of 1.5–3 s (forward at 0.3–1.2 m/s, backward, sideways at 0.2–0.4 m/s, turning at 0.5–1.0 rad/s, walking turns and stops). The reference root is integrated from the command and its joints are a standing pose without a gait. The reported error is the planar velocity error in the body frame from 0.2 s finite differences, skipping 0.5 s after each change of command.

Method details. Constants. H = 24 plan tokens covering 0.96 s; d = 102 entries per token (73 state, 29 action); $J = 2$ updates per call; second-pass noise level $t _ { 2 } = 0 . 8 ;$ tolerance $\rho = 0 . 0 5$ (in the unit of w); default strength s per task (Tab. S1); training noise level $t = { \mathrm { s i g m o i d } } ( u )$ with $u \sim \mathcal { N } ( \mu _ { u } , \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } ) , \mu _ { u } = \sigma _ { u } = 0 . 8 ; K = 8$ noise samples and a 95th-percentile score; the reference window has 10 frames, 0.1 s apart. Reference window. The window has 10 frames at offsets of $0 , 5 , \ldots , 4 5$ control ticks (0.1 s apart). Each frame has 38 features: root 9 (position in the yaw frame of the current reference frame, and a 6-number orientation relative to the robot), legs 12 and upper body 17 (joint positions). Plan. The 24 tokens sit at tick offsets 0–15 and then every fourth tick up to 47 (about 0.96 s). A token has $d = 1 0 2$ entries: a state of 73 (root position 3, orientation 6, linear velocity 3, angular velocity 3, joint positions 29, joint velocities 29) and an action of 29. States are relative to the current robot pose: root xy rotated into the robot’s yaw frame, root height absolute, orientation relative to the current root, linear velocity in the yaw frame, angular velocity in the body frame. Plan and reference features are normalized with their dataset means and standard deviations; positions are in metres, angles in radians, and velocities in m/s and rad/s. Costs that act on world-frame quantities, such as obstacle distances, transform the plan back with the current pose. Head. $g _ { \phi }$ is an MLP (hidden widths 1024, 1024, 512, Mish) whose input is proprioception, the first four planned states, the reference window and the three group flags; a hidden group’s reference features are set to zero. It is trained with group masks drawn per group with probability 0.5 and for all groups with probability 0.2, whereas the generator’s reference masks use 0.3 and 0.1.

Steering update. The gradient is divided by max $\cdot ( \lVert G \rVert _ { F } , 1 0 ^ { - 6 } )$ , with the norm taken over the whole $H \times d$ tensor after masking, so with a channel mask the active channels share the full step; a zero gradient gives a zero update. The violation w that sets $\gamma = \operatorname* { m i n } ( 1 , w / \rho )$ is, per cost, the largest value over the plan of: the clearance violation of the whole-body capsules against the obstacle field (obstacle avoidance, m); the distance of the end effector to the target, beyond a 5 cm dead zone for reaching and without it for pelvis-relative drawing (m); and the tilt angle of the tray (rad, first 16 plan ticks). For velocity commands, w is the root-mean-square planned velocity error (m/s). Weak-motor and other resource costs (energy, quiet landing, support) have no constraint to satisfy and always take the full step $( \gamma = 1 )$ . The same $\rho = 0 . 0 5$ is used in the unit of w. The step length is set by the violation only; regularizing terms in C change the direction but not the length. The second-pass input is $0 . 2 \hat { X } ^ { ( 1 ) } + 0 . 8 \epsilon ^ { \prime }$ , the same corruption as in training, $X ^ { ( t ) } = ( 1 - t ) X ^ { 0 } + t \epsilon { \mathrm { a t } } t = 0 . 8$ , with fresh noise $\epsilon ^ { \prime }$

Steering cost. With both calls steered by two steps each, one control step evaluates the generator six times, four of them with a backward pass (four and two with the second call only), and the cost is not re-evaluated after the last step. The line-search variants (Sec. S4) halve a step up to n times and add one evaluation per halving. The settings with a larger optimization budget run 12 steps, or 6 steps with a line search, in each call (Sec. S4). Torque costs penalize the torque of the weak joints implied by the planned actions of the first 16 ticks (hinge squared above the reduced limit); a variant evaluates the action that the head would execute now, and is used in one setting only. Only the actions of the first 8 ticks are supervised in training (Sec. S1); restricting the cost to them gives the same result (left knee at 15%, in a sep arate batch: success 0.79±0.01 with 8 ticks and 0.78±0.01 with 16, against 0.79±0.01 with 16 ticks in Tab. S5; 0.71 unsteered; three runs).

Consistency refinement. The world model is an MLP (three hidden layers of 2048), trained once and offline on closed-loop rollouts of the distilled controller over 6,000 training clips (action noise 0, 0.1 and 0.3) without task data; it is separate from the world model that the scratch-RL route fits online. Starting from the measured state, we optimize offsets to the planned actions of the first K ticks $( K = 8$ or 12) with Adam (10 steps, learning rate 0.1) to match the steered joint positions, the root height and the root tilt of the plan. Errors are scaled by 0.05 rad, 0.02 m and 0.03, and the offsets carry an $\ell _ { 2 }$ penalty of 0.01. The rollout replaces the first K planned states before the head.

Training. In distillation, the executed action comes from the teacher with probability $\beta ,$ annealed from 1 to 0, and otherwise from the student; half of the environments add action noise of 0.1 (DART). Besides $\mathcal { L } _ { \mathrm { g e n } }$ , the generator is trained with a small reference reconstruction loss (straight-through gradients through the quantizer) and an action-difference smoothness term (weight 0.1, first eight plan ticks). The head’s input states are a one-step generator sample plus Gaussian noise of 0.02 (normalized units). In scratch RL, the head’s inverse-dynamics loss uses the true next states with noise 0.1 (normalized units) and a weight of 1; the generator’s hindsight loss has weight 1, and a small reference reconstruction loss (weight 0.01) is added. The generator’s action channels are supervised only on the first eight plan ticks, since only the head produces the executed action.

## S2. Evaluation protocol

Tracking protocol. We evaluate all trackers within a unified simulation benchmark: MuJoCo [55] sim-to-sim derived from the MimicLite deployment harness, executing each method’s official ONNX policy and control configuration at 50 Hz. Following SONIC [35], an episode is classified as failed if the vertical pelvis position deviates from the reference by more than 0.25 m at any frame. Tracking metrics are aggregated over successful clips: root-relative body joint position error $E _ { l }$ (mm) and global root-translated body position error $E _ { g }$ (mm). Owing to the heavy-tailed distribution of global drift, we report the median of $E _ { g } .$ . Evaluation spans two test sets: 150 windows of held-out subjects from AMASS [38] and all 512 windows of unseen actors from BONES-SEED (Tab. S1). On BONES, 18 windows (50 cm boxes, ladders, stairs, a 1 m jump) require scene geometry absent from the flat-ground harness and fail for every tracker; the RL-from-scratch OneSteer fails only these.

Steering protocol. Steering capabilities are evaluated in the training simulator (mjlab, on MuJoCo Warp), not in the MuJoCo harness used for Tab. 2, on 79 walking trajectories from unseen actors across three random trials per condition: (i) Hurdles: three 10 cm crossbars placed across the locomotion path at 1, 2, and 3 m intervals. Crossings are categorized as feasible passes if hurdles are cleared, stepped upon, or lightly contacted without falling, and as severefailures if the torso impacts the barrier or the robot collapses. (ii) Low bar: a horizontal beam (10 cm deep along the path, 80 cm wide, 50 cm tall) 1.5 m along the path, with its underside at 1.30, 1.25, or 1.20 m, with clean passes denoting collision-free traversal under the beam: a crossing beside it (root lateral offset beyond its half-width of 0.4 m) is not clean. Such detours are 1.5–7.6% of the encounters in every row of Tab. 3, steered, unsteered and edited alike, and the mean lateral offset at the beam is 8–18 cm. (iii) Spatial reaching: the right wrist must reach a target offset forward and upward from its nominal reference position within a mid-sequence time window. Target geometry and default guidance strengths are listed in Tab. S1.

Baselines and controls. The established alternative for de terministic controllers is to optimize kinematic reference modifications under identical cost objectives prior to tracking. We optimize joint-level trajectory edits of the taskrelevant body groups, preserving root kinematics (Tab. S1), under identical signed-distance or reaching costs alongside temporal smoothness regularizers. The modified trajectories are subsequently executed by the frozen RL teacher (Mimi cLite Huge). We benchmark against both offline kinematic editing and closed-loop kinematic editing, where the remain ing 1.2 s horizon of the reference trajectory is re-optimized from the robot’s measured root state at every 50 Hz contro step for hurdles and reaching and every 5 steps for the low bar (every step gives 0.10 instead of 0.11 at 1.25 m). Because low-bar steering can lower the root while these editors keep it, we add for the low bar an editor that also changes the root height and pitches the root about its own lateral axis, with a term that keeps the feet at their original heights so that a lower root comes with bent legs (weight 100, same cost, regularizer, smoothness, learning rate and budget otherwise; closed loop every 5 ticks with 30 Adam steps, and every tick or with 100 steps as controls). MimicLite observes the root height and the root orientation of the reference, so the teacher sees the edit. The edited references clear the bar (mean worst violation after editing at most 1.0 cm; 0.6 cm offline and 0.3 cm in closed loop at 1.25 m) and lower the pelvis by up to 14 cm, yet clean passes stay at 0.55, 0.03 and 0.00 offline and 0.67, 0.04 and 0.02 in closed loop for the 1.30, 1.25 and 1.20 m bars (closed loop at 1.25 m: 0.04 every tick, 0.02 with 100 steps). At the tick where the offline edit is deepest the teacher’s pelvis is 0.2, 1.0 and 1.2 cm below the original reference while the edit asks for 2.3, 6.2 and 9.3 cm. Reach steering also moves the root, so the same root editor is run on the reaching cost, with the legs freed in addition to the right arm and waist (offline; closed loop every tick as for the J editor). It hits the target in 0.31 (offline) and 0.60 (closed loop) of the trials, against 0.38 and 0.60 without the root. Hurdle steering keeps the root, so the J editor is already

the matching control there.

A stronger tracker of edited references. To test whether the edits fail because of the teacher, we let SONIC (release), the tracker with the highest success in Tab. 2, execute the same edited references in the MuJoCo harness of Tab. 2. The harness has no obstacles, so we score the executed wholebody capsules against the obstacle geometry (a pass is clean if no capsule enters the bar by 1 cm or more); without contact this is an upper bound. SONIC executes the edits no better than the teacher. Under the 1.25 m bar the edited reference is clean in every window, but the executed motion is clean in 0.03 of them for SONIC and 0.11 for the teacher (0.06 and 0.09 with the root edit; of the 6.1 cm by which this edit lowers the pelvis near the bar, the largest drop within 0.3 m of it averaged over windows, SONIC realizes 2.0 cm and the teacher 1.7 cm); at 1.30 m both reach 0.89, and at 1.20 m neither passes. Over the hurdles the edited reference avoids contact in 0.96 of the windows and neither tracker in any: both keep their feet near the original height (mean penetration 5.7 and 5.5 cm, against 5.8 cm on the original reference).

Planned and executed torque. The torque cost acts on the torque implied by the planned actions, while the robot executes the action of the head. To check how much of the planned reduction reaches the motor, we log the torque of the weak joint at every tick (Tab. S3; one run per setting, the settings of Tab. S5). Steering lowers the torque the head commands, on average from 1.00 to 0.69 of the degraded limit for the knee and from 1.47 to 1.07 for the hip, and shortens the time the weak motor saturates (13.9 to 9.9% and 24.1 to 19.7% of the ticks). The executed reduction is smaller than the planned one, and after steering the planned and commanded torques of the current tick agree less (correlation 0.88 to 0.31 for the knee, 0.92 to 0.55 for the hip). This is expected from how the head is trained: it learns to execute plans of natural motions, with no task, fault or steered data (Sec. S1), so a plan that shifts effort away from one joint lies partly outside what it has seen, and it follows that plan only in part while its feedback keeps the robot balanced. Steering still pays off at the task level (success 0.71 to 0.79 and 0.77 to 0.86 in these runs), and closing the remaining gap, e.g. by also training the head on steered plans in a task-agnostic way, is left to future work.

Latency. Tab. S8 separates the computation of each setting. The generation step is one forward pass of the generator; a steering update is one forward and one backward pass through it, so the cost grows with the number of gradient steps, not with a denoising chain. On the RTX 4090 the whole steered step is captured as one CUDA graph (batch of one). On the Orin NX (MAXN) the step is captured in the same way and timed back to back over 500 steps, including the input and output checks; the steering cost is compiled with torch.compile (FP32; actions within $1 0 ^ { - 6 }$ rad of the uncompiled step), and for both calls the generator and ac tion head are compiled as well, with TF32 matrix products (actions within 0.006 rad; in simulation this leaves steering unchanged: hurdles 0.68±0.03 vs. 0.66±0.03, 1.25 m bar 0.84±0.01 vs. $0 . 8 3 { \pm } 0 . 0 4 .$ , reach 0.54±0.05 vs. 0.52±0.03, three runs each). Uncompiled, the two steered settings take 28.3 and 48.7 ms. The deployed ONNX tracker, timed when it runs once every 20 ms as in deployment, takes 13.1 ms (a full control step including observation assembly and com mand output takes 15.5 ms); it is not captured as a graph, so these paced timings are not comparable with the backto-back ones of Tab. S8, and we did not time steering in the paced loop. In the hardware trials steering ran on a tethered RTX 4090 (below). Only the settings whose 95th percentile stays below 20 ms on the Orin NX fit the synchronous 50 Hz loop of the robot; the larger-budget settings and world-model projection do not, and we report them as computation–performance trade-offs rather than as real-time settings.

Steering on the robot. The real-robot steering runs use the distilled OneSteer (the same weights as in simulation) on a walking reference, with guidance in the second generator call only (two gradient steps). The cost is the whole-body SDF cost of the simulated low-bar task, evaluated on a distance field from the head-mounted LiDAR (Livox MID-360) instead of known obstacle geometry. Before a run, a short Li-DAR capture registered by LiDAR-inertial odometry (FAST-LIO2) is turned into an occupancy map and a 3D Euclidean distance field (ROG-Map, 1 cm voxels), which is then kept fixed; during the run the odometry only updates the robot’s pose in this map, and unobserved voxels add no cost. In these runs the generator, the cost and the action head run on a tethered computer with an RTX 4090, which also holds the distance field; the Orin NX runs the LiDAR-inertial odom etry. Under a bar at 1.1 m, the robot passed under the bar in four of five attempts; in the remaining attempt the robot crouched too far and its waist touched the ground. Without steering, the robot walked into the bar in all three attempts and broke it. Over a 12 cm hurdle, with the same cost, map pipeline and settings, the robot stepped over the hurdle in all three attempts: one crossing was clean, in one the foot landed on top of the hurdle and in one the foot kicked it. Without steering, the robot tripped over the hurdle in all three attempts. Fig. S3 shows one attempt of each setting as an image sequence, and Fig. S2 shows highly dynamic motions (backflips, running) tracked on the same robot without steering.

Map, pose drift and obstacle height in simulation. On the robot the cost reads a voxel distance field through an odometry pose. We reproduce this in simulation (Tab. S9, real-time setting, three runs): the analytic SDF is replaced by the same voxel pipeline (3 cm voxels) built from the true obstacles, and the pose is perturbed by an SE(2) random walk whose s.d. grows with the square root of the distance walked. What the relaxed and the unrelaxed parts of the reference do. Hiding a group hands it to the cost, but the other groups should still follow the reference. Tab. S15 measures both for reaching and the tray, whose costs act on the right arm, so the upper body is the relaxed group. Each setting uses the same frozen weights, two generator calls, both steered (two gradient steps per call; strength 2 for reaching, 0.5 for the tray). We report the task metric over all clips and, on the clips that succeed in every setting, the mean absolute joint error to the original reference per group, the root height and heading errors, and the horizontal root drift at the end of the window. Steering leaves the unrelaxed groups almost unchanged: the leg error moves from $4 . 4 ^ { \circ }$ to at most 4.5<sup>◦</sup>, the root height error from 0.74 to at most 0.77 cm and the heading error by at most 0.2<sup>◦</sup>; the drift grows from 21 to 22– 25 cm. The cost is paid by the relaxed group, including the left arm, which belongs to it although the cost does not use it. Where the mask is applied decides the trade-off. Hiding the arm reference from the executor only (our default) keeps the left arm within 2.8<sup>◦</sup> of nominal tracking and gives the lowest ball loss (0.07, mean of three runs); hiding it from the generator as well gives the most reaching hits (0.65) but lets the left arm drift by 8<sup>◦</sup>; hiding it from the generator only behaves like no mask, since the executor still pulls the arm back to the reference. On 512 held-out windows of all motion types (standing, turning, upper-body motions), with the same strength and no tuning, the unrelaxed groups change as little (legs 4.6 to 4.8<sup>◦</sup>, root height 1.21 to 1.27 cm, heading 2.6 to 3.0<sup>◦</sup>) while the hit rate rises from 0.02 to 0.52 (executor mask) or 0.55 (both masks) at a success rate of 0.95 against 0.96. Raising the strength to 4 adds no hits and raises the drift to 28–34 cm, so the default strength sits at the useful end of the trade-off.

Steering moves the plan. Fig. S5 shows the 0.96 s plan of the generator before and after steering, for a hurdle and for a 1.25 m bar. Without steering, the planned poses follow the reference and overlap the obstacle (red). With the cost, the generator returns a different plan (blue) that clears the obstacle, and the robot executes this plan. The reference, the weights and the executor are unchanged; only the input noise moves. Fig. S4 shows the same over a longer stretch for two other motions, a low bar while carrying a heavy object and a row of hurdles while walking in a loop.

Adding a condition: terrain. Starting from the model trained from scratch (Tab. 2), we encode a 21×21 height scan around the pelvis into 36 tokens that are appended to the input sequence of the generator; the reference stays a flat-ground motion and the inverse-dynamics head is unchanged. After RL fine-tuning on stairs, slopes, boxes and rough ground, the terrain-conditioned OneSteer succeeds on 0.90 of the cells (Fig. S6) against 0.43 for the same model without the scan, and masking the tokens of the finetuned weights gives 0.55, so the gain comes from the map (Tab. S17). On flat ground, success is unchanged and the lo cal error slightly higher (success 0.963 vs. 0.963, E<sub>l</sub> 27.6 vs. 26.0 mm, one run). The terrain-conditioned model remains steerable, with about half the gain of the model without terrain input, and has not been run on the real robot.

Terrain conditioning. The terrain-conditioned model adds one input to the generator and leaves the rest unchanged. The scan is 21×21 points at 0.1 m spacing over [−1, 1] m around the pelvis, in the pelvis yaw frame; each value is the pelvis height minus the ground height. A small CNN maps it to a 6×6 grid, and each cell becomes one token (with a position embedding) at the end of the generator’s input sequence, where it joins the other tokens by ordinary self-attention. The attention of the existing tokens onto the terrain tokens carries a learned bias per layer, initialised very negative, so training starts from the unchanged tracker and the new tokens are opened by training. The reference is a flat-ground motion that is re-anchored to the patch where the robot starts. Training is RL through the learned world model, on a procedural terrain with six families (flat, stairs down, stairs up, slope, boxes, rough ground) and ten difficulty levels with a curriculum, and runs in two stages: RL on the terrain curriculum (several runs; the last, 30,000 iterations, took 7.7 hours on four GPUs), then 10,000 further iterations with references that conform to the terrain. We evaluate with 88 clips that cross the terrain (a path longer than 2.5 m), on cells of terrain family and level, and a clip counts as a success if the robot does not fall and ends within 1 m of the end of the reference path. Single evaluations vary by about ±4 points, so Tab. S17 averages four passes. Figure S6 shows rollouts, and Tab. S18 the cost on flat ground.

Two points qualify the claim. First, terrain-conditioned OneSteer remains steerable: on the hurdle windows, steering raises its success from 0.65 to 0.76, about half the gain of the model without terrain input (0.32 to 0.55; Tab. S18). Second, the model was evaluated only in simulation; the height-scan pipeline for the robot (a LiDAR-inertial map sampled on the same grid) is implemented but has not been run on hardware.

## S4. Design of steering

Steps and calls. Tab. S19 varies the steering procedure. Executing guided plans. The action head executes guided plans without having seen them in training, and part of each edit is not realized. One reason is that the edit is not dynamically consistent: the one-step residual of a learned world model on the executed plans is 0.51 (normalized) for guided hurdle plans against 0.08 for unguided plans, mostly in root and joint velocities, since the edit moves joint angles without the matching velocities. A soft consistency cost in the guidance does not help: it shrinks the lift. Projecting the edited plan onto the world model (Sec. S1) closes part of this gap: on hurdles, clean crossings rise from 0.19 to 0.32 and trailing-foot kicks fall from 0.42 to 0.29, with severe failures and success unchanged (three runs; the edit is smoothed over ticks with coefficient 0.75 in both settings). Joint-edit tasks improve as well: the realized reach error falls from 0.039 to 0.025 m. For the low bar, which rewrites the root, the plain guided plan is already executed well (0.90 clean at 1.25 m), and the optimized actions cannot replace the closed-loop action head (success 0.10 open loop).

Where the update acts. Tab. S21 applies the same costs at different places, all in the real-time setting of Tab. 3 (second call, two steps, default strengths and masks, three runs on the same 79 windows). (i) A random direction with the same per-sample length as our update leaves every task at the unsteered level, so the gain does not come from perturbing the input. (ii) Steering the plan itself, with gradient steps of the same normalized length on the generator’s output and the result passed to the action head, matches our update at the 1.30 m bar and is better at reaching (0.75 vs. 0.57). Reaching asks for one joint group to arrive at a target without a transition: a direct edit moves the wrist in the plan at once, and the head executes arm joints directly. Our update passes through the generator, which turns it into a smooth change of the whole plan, so it moves the wrist less per step and needs more steps: with 12 smaller steps it also hits 0.75 (Tab. S11). The direct edit is worse on hurdles (0.48 vs. 0.68) and under the lower bars (0.80 and 0.43 vs. 0.90 and 0.56), where the plan has to change consistently across legs, root and the horizon, which the generator provides and a direct edit does not. (iii) Re-noising the pushed plan and regenerating it from the reference, as in SDEdit, erases the edit: every task falls to the unsteered level, since the generator maps the input back to a plan of the unchanged reference. (iv) Giving the offline-edited reference of Tab. 3 to OneSteer as its condition, with the action head seeing it, is reference editing inside our own model; it is close to the teacher on the same edit (hurdles 0.46 vs. 0.58, 1.30 m bar 0.64 vs. 0.72, reach 0.24 vs. 0.38) and far below steering, so the advantage of steering is not a weakness of the teacher as a tracker of edited references. That OneSteer tracks the edit somewhat worse than the teacher is expected: it was distilled to imitate the teacher on natural references, an edited reference lies outside that distribution for both, and a student matches its teacher only where it was trained to. (v) On the tray (strength 0.5, upper body hidden from the head), the direct edit fails outright: the ball leaves the tray in 0.97±0.01 of the windows, against 0.16 unsteered and 0.15±0.01 for our update in the same setting (0.08±0.01 with both calls steered, Tab. The hand tilt is reduced as much as with our update $( 1 1 . 6 ^ { \circ }$ vs. 11.8<sup>◦</sup>), so the ball is lost to the motion, not the tilt: a plausible reason is that the direct edit tilts the planned hand without the matching motion of the rest of the arm and body, and executing such a plan jolts the tray. (vi) Sampling instead of the gradient: an MPPI update with eight perturbations per step, each as long as a gradient step and weighted by its cost, gains a little at the hurdles and the 1.30 m bar (0.47 and 0.51) and nothing at the lower bars or reaching; sixteen perturbations do no better (hurdles 0.41, 1.25 m bar 0.02, reach 0.03). Within two steps, the direction has to come from the gradient. (vii) Limiting the size: projecting the steered input back into the ball of norm r (in units of ${ \sqrt { H d } } ;$ pure noise has norm 1.0) after every step keeps the hurdles (0.68 and 0.66 at $r = 1 . 5$ and 1.0) but lowers the 1.25 m bar from 0.90 to 0.40 and 0.26 and reaching from 0.57 to 0.39 and 0.22. The projection scales the whole input, the draft included, so it bounds what a step near the training noise can do rather than isolating the effect of the norm. Only the unconstrained gradient update on the generator input improves every task.

## S5. Self-assessment

Without a world model. The scores of Tab. S26 are computed on the model trained from scratch, which also has a world model and a critic. The spread needs neither, so we compute it on the distilled model, which has none, with the same protocol, and label failures by one simulated rollout of the distilled model per clip (666 of the 7,884 clips fail, 8.4%). The spread reaches AUC 0.80 and a top-2% precision of 0.86 (top 5%: 0.67 precision, 0.39 recall; whole clips: 0.81 and 0.87), against 0.83 and 0.91 on the scratch model; the plan deviation has a higher AUC (0.86) but a lower top-2% precision (0.69). Failure discovery by the spread therefore carries over to the distilled controller that steers and runs on the robot.

Why the spread measures model error. Fix a condition c (proprioception and reference tokens) and let $X ^ { 0 } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { D }$ be the future that is realized from it; the plan has $D =$ $H d = 2 4 \cdot 1 0 2 = 2 4 4 8$ entries, each standardized over the training data. At inference the generator is called once on pure noise, $z = \epsilon$ , which is independent of $X ^ { 0 }$ given c. Writing $\bar { f } ( c ) = \mathbb { E } _ { \epsilon } f _ { \theta } ( \epsilon , c )$ , the expected loss of this call splits exactly into three terms:

because ϵ and $X ^ { 0 }$ are independent given c, so the cross terms vanish. The irreducible term, i.e. the genuine multimodality of the future, does not enter the spread; the diversity of a multi-step sampler, in contrast, is mostly that term. The constant predictor $\mathbb { E } [ X ^ { 0 } \mid c ]$ attains the irreducible term alone, so bias and spread are the excess of $f _ { \theta }$ over it on pure-noise inputs, and the spread, which needs neither $X ^ { 0 }$ nor a simulator, is a lower bound on that excess.

The trained optimum is nearly constant on pure noise. Training does not use pure noise only: the generator predicts $X ^ { 0 }$ from $Y = ( 1 - T ) X ^ { 0 } + T \epsilon$ with $T = { \mathrm { s i g m o i d } } ( U )$ $U \sim \mathcal N ( 0 . 8 , 0 . 8 ^ { 2 } )$ , and it does not see T. Its loss-optimal form is therefore

$$

$$

From the spread to the score. Our score is a robust, empirical version of the spread: K=8 samples, joint positions only, plan ticks from 4 on, and the 95th percentile over time. It is not itself the bound above, which concerns the squared error of the whole plan; it is a proxy motivated by it, and its relation to tracking failures is measured (Tab. S26), not derived.

Three limits apply. (i) The relation is one-sided: a small spread does not imply a small error, since the model can be wrong with little spread (bias); consistently, clips with low spread but rare motions still fail at 6.1%, and rarity complements the spread. (ii) The floor of the optimum is derived for a Gaussian approximation of $X ^ { 0 }$ | c; it gives the order of magnitude, not an exact value, and a multimodal future (e.g. two ways around an obstacle) adds terms between modes that a Gaussian does not capture. (iii) The step from plan error to a tracking failure is empirical: the executed action is computed from the planned states, so a plan that varies with the noise yields actions that vary from tick to tick, but the link is measured (Tab. S26), not derived. After fine-tuning, the spread falls by only about 7% while half of the failures are fixed, which suggests that much of the gain comes from the action head and the closed loop rather than from a more accurate plan.

Selection cost. We time both scores on the same 1,000 random library clips (mean length 6.4 s), with the same checkpoint, one worker each, back to back on the same RTX 4090, once on the whole clips and once on their first 10 s. The noise spread (K=8, every fifth tick, all 100 clips of a chunk in one batch) takes 88 s on the whole clips (86 s of computation) and 71–91 s on the first 10 s (two runs). One simulated rollout per clip (mjlab, 100 clips per process in parallel, as in our scans) takes 489 s on the whole clips and 451 s on the first 10 s; of this, the simulation loops take 180 and 147 s and the rest is the start-up of each process (about 30 s). On the whole clips the spread is therefore 2.4× cheaper than one rollout per clip if the simulator is started once, 5.6× as our scans ran, and 11–28× cheaper than the five rollouts per clip of Extreme-RGMT [36] (first 10 s: 1.9–6.4× and 8–32×). Five rollouts are used because one rollout is a noisy label: with another seed, 16–18% of the clips that fail succeed. Ranking quality does not depend on the 10 s cut: on the whole clips the spread has AUC 0.84 and precision 0.90 among the top 2% (0.83 and 0.91 with the cut). The simulator numbers are for our mjlab stack, not for the simulator of Extreme-RGMT, and we compare cost only here; a list selected by one rollout per clip is trained in Tab. S27.
